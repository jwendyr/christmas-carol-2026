# Chapter 4: The Last of the Spirits

The Phantom slowly, gravely, and silently approached. When it drew near, Scrooge dropped to his knees, for the very air through which this Spirit moved seemed to scatter gloom and mystery.

It was shrouded in a deep black robe that concealed its head, face, and form, leaving nothing visible except a single outstretched hand. If not for that hand, it would have been difficult to distinguish its dark shape from the night, or separate it from the deep shadows surrounding it.

Scrooge felt that it was tall and stately as it stood beside him, and its mysterious presence filled him with a solemn dread. He knew nothing more, for the Spirit neither spoke nor moved.

"I am in the presence of the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come?" said Scrooge.

The Spirit did not answer, but pointed forward with its hand.

"You are about to show me shadows of things that have not happened yet, but will happen in the time before us," Scrooge continued. "Is that true, Spirit?"

The upper folds of the dark robe shifted for a moment, as if the Spirit had bowed its head. That was the only answer he received.

Although he was quite used to ghostly company by now, Scrooge feared this silent shape so much that his legs trembled beneath him. When he prepared to follow, he found he could hardly stand. The Spirit paused for a moment, observing his condition and giving him time to steady himself.

Yet this only made things worse for Scrooge. A vague, unsettling dread chilled him, knowing that behind that dusky shroud, ghostly eyes were fixed intently upon him—while no matter how hard he strained his own eyes, he could see nothing but a spectral hand and a great mass of black.

"Ghost of the Future!" he cried. "I fear you more than any spirit I have seen. But because I know your purpose is to do me good, and because I hope to live as a different man from what I once was, I am ready to go with you, and to do so with a thankful heart. Won't you speak to me?"

It gave him no reply. The hand simply pointed straight ahead.

"Lead on!" said Scrooge. "Lead on! The night is fading fast, and I know this time is precious to me. Lead on, Spirit!"

The Phantom moved away just as it had approached. Scrooge followed in the shadow of its robe, which seemed to lift him up and carry him along.

They hardly seemed to enter the city. Instead, the city seemed to spring up around them and swallow them whole. But there they were, right in the heart of it—on the Exchange, among the merchants and businessmen. Men hurried back and forth, jingling coins in their pockets, gathering in small groups, checking their pocket watches, and absently fingering their heavy gold watch seals, just as Scrooge had seen them do a thousand times before.

The Spirit stopped beside one little cluster of businessmen. Seeing that the hand was pointed toward them, Scrooge stepped forward to listen to their conversation.

"No," said a large, stout man with an enormous double chin. "I don't know much about it either way. I only know he's dead."

"When did he die?" asked another.

"Last night, I believe."

"Why, what was the matter with him?" asked a third, taking a huge pinch of snuff from an oversized snuffbox. "I thought he'd never die."

"God only knows," said the first man with a yawn.

"What has he done with all his money?" asked a red-faced gentleman with a pendulous wart hanging from the tip of his nose, which shook like the wattles of a turkey.

"I haven't heard," said the man with the large chin, yawning again. "Left it to his company, perhaps. He certainly hasn't left it to me. That's all I know."

This little joke was received with general laughter.

"It will probably be a very cheap funeral," the same speaker added. "Because on my life, I don't know a single soul who will go. What do you say we get a group together and volunteer?"

"I don't mind going if a lunch is provided," observed the gentleman with the wart on his nose. "Feed me, and I'll make one of the party."

Another laugh went round.

"Well, I suppose I am the most selfless among you after all," said the first speaker. "For I never wear black gloves to funerals, and I never eat lunch. But I'll offer to go if anyone else will. When I come to think of it, I'm not at all sure I wasn't his closest friend. We used to stop and chat whenever we met on the street. Goodbye, gentlemen!"

The speakers and listeners strolled away, blending into other crowds. Scrooge recognized every one of those men and turned to the Spirit for an explanation.

The Phantom glided on into another street. Its finger pointed to two men meeting. Scrooge listened closely again, thinking the explanation might lie here.

He knew these men intimately as well. They were wealthy, prominent figures in commerce. Scrooge had always made a point of standing high in their esteem—strictly from a business point of view, of course.

"How are you?" asked one.

"How are you?" replied the other.

"Well!" said the first. "Old Scratch has finally claimed his own, eh?"

"So I am told," replied the second. "Cold out, isn't it?"

"Normal for Christmas time. You're not a skater, I suppose?"

"No, no. Far too much else to think about. Good morning!"

Not another word. That was the entirety of their meeting, their conversation, and their parting.

At first, Scrooge was surprised that the Spirit should attach any importance to conversations so seemingly trivial. But feeling certain they must have a hidden purpose, he set his mind to consider what it could be. They could hardly have anything to do with the death of Jacob Marley, his old partner, for Marley was long in the past, and this Ghost's domain was the Future. Nor could he think of anyone close to him to whom the remarks might apply. Still, never doubting that whoever was being spoken of held some moral lesson for his own redemption, he resolved to treasure up every word he heard and everything he saw—and especially to watch for the shadow of himself when it appeared. He expected that the conduct of his future self would provide the missing key and solve these riddles easily.

He looked around that familiar place for his own reflection, but another man stood in his accustomed corner. And though the clock showed his usual time of day for being there, he saw no likeness of himself among the multitudes bustling through the entrance. It gave him little surprise, however. He had already been contemplating a complete change in his life, and he hoped this absence meant his new resolutions were already being carried out.

Silent and dark beside him stood the Phantom, its hand still outstretched. When Scrooge broke away from his search, he sensed from the turn of the hand and its position toward him that the Spirit's unseen eyes were watching him keenly. A sudden shudder ran through him, leaving him feeling bitterly cold.

They left the busy scene and moved into an obscure, rundown part of town where Scrooge had never set foot before, though he knew well enough where it was and what a wretched reputation it held. The alleys were narrow and foul, the shops and tenements rotting and filthy, and the people half-clothed, drunken, ragged, and rough. Dark passageways and archways, like open sewers, poured out their stench, dirt, and miserable lives onto the crooked streets. The entire quarter reeked of crime, filth, and misery.

Deep within this den of thieves and scoundrels sat a low-ceilinged, crooked shop under an overhanging roof, where scrap iron, old rags, empty bottles, bones, and tubs of foul animal fat were bought and sold. Heaped across the floor inside were piles of rusted keys, nails, chains, hinges, files, scales, counterweights, and scrap metal of every description. Secrets that few would ever care to uncover festered and hid beneath mountains of filthy rags, mounds of spoiled grease, and heaps of dry bones.

Sitting among the wares he traded in, beside a charcoal burner made of stacked old bricks, was a gray-haired rascal nearly seventy years old. To block out the biting cold from the street, he had hung up a greasy curtain of tattered rags along a line, and now he smoked his pipe with all the quiet satisfaction of a gentleman in luxury.

Scrooge and the Phantom entered just as a woman carrying a heavy bundle slipped inside. But she had barely stepped through the doorway when another woman, similarly loaded down, came in right behind her. Hard on her heels came a man dressed in faded black clothes, looking just as startled to see the women as they were to see each other. After a moment of blank astonishment, in which the old man with the pipe joined in, all three burst out laughing.

"Trust the cleaning woman to be first!" cried she who had entered first. "Trust the laundry woman to be second, and trust the undertaker's assistant to be third! Look at this, Old Joe—talk about a coincidence! What are the odds of all three of us meeting here without meaning to?"

"You couldn't have met in a better place," Old Joe said, removing the pipe from his mouth. "Come on into the parlour. You've had the run of the place for years, and the other two aren't strangers either. Hold on while I shut the shop door. Listen to how it screeches! I swear there isn't a rustier piece of iron in this whole place than those hinges, and I know there aren't any older bones in here than mine! Ha, ha! We all suit our trade nicely—we're a perfect match. Come on back. Come into the parlour."

The "parlour" was simply the small space behind the rag curtain. The old man poked the embers together using an old iron stair rod. After trimming his smoky oil lamp with the stem of his pipe—for night had fallen—he put the pipe back between his teeth.

While he did this, the first woman tossed her heavy bundle onto the floor and planted herself boldly on a stool. Resting her elbows on her knees, she stared down the other two with defiant eyes.

"What does it matter, Mrs. Dilber?" she said. "Every person has a right to take care of themselves. He always did!"

"That's the honest truth," the laundress agreed. "No man more so."

"Then don't stand there staring like you're frightened, woman! Who's going to know? We're not about to go picking holes in each other's coats, are we?"

"No, indeed!" Mrs. Dilber and the man answered together. "We should hope not!"

"Right then!" the woman cried. "That settles it. Who's any worse off for losing a few things like these? Not a dead man, I suppose!"

"No, indeed," Mrs. Dilber laughed.

"If that wicked old miser wanted to keep his things after he died," the woman continued, "why wasn't he decent while he was alive? If he had been human, he would have had someone to look after him when death struck, instead of lying there gasping out his last breath all alone in that empty room."

"Truer words were never spoken," said Mrs. Dilber. "It's a judgment on him."

"I only wish it were a heavier judgment," the woman snapped. "And it would have been, you can count on that, if I could have laid my hands on anything else. Open that bundle, Old Joe, and tell me what it's worth. Speak plainly. I'm not afraid to go first, and I don't care if they see it. We all know we were helping ourselves before we ran into each other here. It's no sin. Open the bundle, Joe."

Her companions' sense of manners would not allow her to be first, however. The man in faded black stepped up to the front and produced his loot. It was not extensive: a seal or two, a pencil case, a pair of cuff links, and a cheap brooch of little value. Old Joe inspected each item carefully, chalking the price he was willing to pay on the wall, and tallied the total when the man had emptied his pockets.

"That's your account," Joe said. "And I wouldn't give you another sixpence if I were to be boiled alive for refusing. Who's next?"

Mrs. Dilber went next. She had sheets, towels, a few articles of clothing, two antique silver teaspoons, a pair of sugar tongs, and a couple of boots. Her total was chalked up on the wall beside the first.

"I always give too much to the ladies. It's a weakness of mine—it'll ruin me one of these days," Old Joe joked. "That's your money. If you ask for a single penny more, I'll regret being so generous and knock off half a crown."

"And now unpack mine, Joe," the first woman said.

Joe dropped to his knees to untie the heavy bundle. After working loose knot after knot, he hauled out a thick, heavy roll of dark fabric.

"What on earth do you call this?" Joe asked. "Bed curtains?"

"You got it!" the woman laughed, leaning forward over her crossed arms. "Bed curtains!"

"You don't mean to tell me you took them down, rings and all, while he was lying right there on the bed?" Joe gasped.

"Yes, I do," she replied. "Why not?"

"You were born to strike it rich," said Joe, "and you certainly will."

"I certainly wasn't going to hold back when all I had to do was reach out my hand, not for the sake of a man like him, I can promise you that, Joe," she said coolly. "Watch it! Don't drip that lamp oil on those blankets."

"His blankets?" asked Joe.

"Whose else do you think?" she scoffed. "He's hardly going to catch a cold without them now, is he?"

"I hope he didn't die of anything contagious, did he?" Old Joe asked, stopping in his work and looking up with concern.

"Don't you worry your head about that," she replied. "I wasn't so fond of his company that I'd linger around his bed if he had something catching! Go ahead, look through that shirt until your eyes ache—you won't find a single hole or a threadbare patch anywhere. It was the finest shirt he owned, and a beauty too. They would have thrown it away if it hadn't been for me."

"What do you call throwing it away?" asked Old Joe.

"Putting it on him to be buried in, of course!" the woman laughed. "Somebody was fool enough to dress him in it, but I took it right back off. If plain cotton isn't good enough to rot in the ground, it isn't good enough for anything. It looks just as decent on a corpse. He couldn't look any uglier than he already did!"

Scrooge listened to this conversation in sheer horror. Watching them huddled around their stolen plunder in the dim glow of the old man's lamp, he felt a wave of disgust and revulsion so overpowering they might as well have been vile demons haggling over the dead man's flesh.

"Ha, ha!" the woman laughed again as Old Joe pulled out a flannel money pouch and counted out their coins onto the ground. "Look how it ends! He spent his whole life scaring everyone away just so we could profit off his death! Ha, ha, ha!"

"Spirit!" cried Scrooge, trembling from head to foot. "I see! I see! The fate of this miserable man could be my own. The life I am living is heading straight toward this very end. Merciful Heaven, what is this!"

He shrank back in terror, for the scene vanished in an instant, and now he found himself standing beside a bed—a bare, curtainless bed. Beneath a ragged sheet lay a shrouded shape that, without speaking a word, delivered an awful, chilling message.

The room was pitch black, too dark to make out clearly, though Scrooge glanced around instinctively, desperate to see what kind of room it was. A pale sliver of daylight crept through from the outside, falling straight across the bed. And there, robbed, abandoned, unwatched, unwept, and unmourned, lay the body of this man.

Scrooge looked at the Phantom. Its steady hand pointed directly at the corpse's head. The sheet had been pulled so loosely over the face that the slightest touch—the mere lift of Scrooge's finger—would have disclosed who it was. The thought crossed his mind; he knew how easily it could be done, and part of him burned to know. Yet he had no more power to lift that cloth than he had to banish the spirit standing beside him.

O cold, cold, rigid, dreadful Death! Set up your altar here and drape it with all the terrors at your command, for this is your kingdom! But over a beloved, respected, and honored head, you cannot turn a single hair to your cruel will, or make a single feature repulsive. It is not just that the hand is heavy and falls limp when released; it is not just that the heart and pulse are still. It is that the hand was once open, generous, and true; the heart brave, warm, and tender; and the life full of humanity. Strike, Shadow, strike! And see how good deeds burst forth from the wound, scattering seeds of eternal life across the world!

No living voice spoke those words into Scrooge's ears, yet he heard them ringing clearly in his mind as he gazed upon that lonely bed. He wondered: if this man could be raised up this very second, what would his first thoughts be? Greed? Ruthless deals? The endless pursuit of pennies? Look where all that clawing had brought him—to this grand, lonely end!

There he lay, in a dark, empty house, without a single man, woman, or child to say, "He was kind to me in this or that, and for the memory of one kind word, I will show kindness to him now." Outside the door, a stray cat clawed and scratched, while beneath the hearthstones, rats scurried and gnawed. What they wanted in that chamber of death, and why they were so frantic, Scrooge did not dare imagine.

"Spirit!" he pleaded. "This is a fearful place. In leaving it, I swear I will not forget its lesson. Please, let us go!"

Still the Ghost pointed with an unmoving finger at the head.

"I understand what you want," Scrooge answered. "And I would do it if I could. But I don't have the power, Spirit. I don't have the strength."

Again, the silent gaze of the Phantom seemed to bore into him.

"If there is anyone in this entire city who feels any emotion caused by this man's death," Scrooge cried in agony, "show them to me, Spirit, I beg of you!"

The Phantom spread its dark robe before him like a wing. When it drew back, a sunlit room appeared, where a young mother waited with her children.

She was waiting for someone with anxious suspense. She paced back and forth, jumping at every faint sound, looking out through the window, glancing at the clock, and trying in vain to work with her needle. Even the joyful voices of her children at play seemed more than her frayed nerves could bear.

At length, a long-expected knock sounded at the door. She hurried to open it and met her husband. He was a young man, but his face was worn with worry and exhaustion. Yet now, an unusual look rested upon his features—a strange flicker of quiet relief, something he seemed almost ashamed of and fought to hold back.

He sat down to the simple dinner that had been kept warm for him by the fire. When his wife softly asked him for news—only after a long, tense silence—he seemed at a loss for how to answer.

"Is it good news?" she asked, trying to help him speak. "Or bad?"

"Bad," he answered.

"Are we completely ruined?"

"No. There is still hope, Caroline."

"If he relents," she gasped in amazement, "then there is! Nothing is beyond hope if such a miracle has happened!"

"He is past relenting," her husband replied. "He is dead."

Her face was gentle and kind, the face of a patient soul; yet hearing those words, her heart filled with overwhelming gratitude. She clasped her hands together in thanksgiving. A heartbeat later, she bowed her head, asking forgiveness for feeling glad, and felt truly sorry. But the first, involuntary reaction of her heart had been pure relief.

"What that half-drunken woman told me last night—the one I mentioned when I tried to see him for a one-week delay on our payment—turns out to be completely true," her husband explained. "I thought she was just making excuses so he wouldn't have to talk to me. But he wasn't just sick. He was dying then."

"To whom will our debt be transferred?" she asked.

"I don't know. But by the time the estate is settled, we'll have the money ready. And even if we don't, we would have to suffer terrible misfortune to find a creditor as heartless as he was. We can sleep tonight with light hearts, Caroline!"

Yes. Disguise it however they tried, their burdens had lifted. The children gathered around, quieted by matters they could barely comprehend, their little faces brightening at their parents' eased spirits. It was a happier home because that man had died! The only emotion the Ghost could show him, sparked by his passing, was one of relief and joy.

"Let me see some tenderness connected with a death," Scrooge pleaded. "Or that dark room we just left will haunt me forever."

The Ghost led him through several streets familiar to his feet. As they walked, Scrooge searched everywhere for any glimpse of himself, but he was nowhere to be seen. Soon they entered poor Bob Cratchit's humble house—the same dwelling Scrooge had visited before. Inside, the mother and children sat gathered around the fire.

Quiet. Very quiet. The usually lively little Cratchits sat still as statues in the corner, gazing up at Peter, who held an open book before him. Mrs. Cratchit and her daughters were busy sewing. But how strangely quiet they were!

"'And He took a child, and set him in the midst of them.'"

Where had Scrooge heard those words before? He had not dreamed them. The boy must have just read them aloud as Scrooge and the Spirit crossed the threshold. Why didn't he keep reading?

The mother laid her needlework on the table and pressed her hand against her face.

"The color of this cloth hurts my eyes," she murmured.

The color? Ah, poor Tiny Tim!

"They're better now," Cratchit's wife said after a moment, trying to smile. "Working by candlelight makes them weak, and I wouldn't want your father to see me with weak eyes when he comes home for the world. It must be near his time."

"Past time, rather," Peter answered, shutting the book. "Though I think he has been walking a little slower than he used to these past few evenings, Mother."

Silence settled over the room once more. At last, Mrs. Cratchit spoke in a steady, cheerful voice that only faltered once:

"I have seen him walk with—I have seen him walk with Tiny Tim upon his shoulder, very fast indeed."

"So have I," cried Peter softly. "Often."

"So have I," exclaimed another child. They all remembered.

"He was so light to carry," she continued, intent upon her work, "and his father loved him so dearly that it was no trouble—no trouble at all. And look, there is your father at the door!"

She hurried out to meet him. Bob entered, wrapped in his long woolen scarf—he had great need of it, poor fellow. His hot tea was waiting by the hearth, and all the children rushed to help him take his coat and pour his cup. Then the two youngest Cratchits climbed onto his knees and laid their cheeks against his face, as if whispering without words, "Don't mind it, Father. Don't be grieved!"

Bob put on a cheerful face for them and spoke pleasantly to the whole family. He looked at the sewing spread across the table and praised the industry and speed of Mrs. Cratchit and the girls. He said they would surely be finished with the mourning clothes long before Sunday.

"Sunday! Did you go there today, Robert?" asked his wife.

"Yes, my dear," Bob replied. "I wish you could have gone. It would have done your heart good to see how green and peaceful a place it is. But you'll see it often. I promised him that I would walk out to visit him every Sunday. My little, little child!" cried Bob suddenly. "My little child!"

The grief broke through all at once. He couldn't help it. If he could have held back those tears, he and his little boy would have been farther apart in spirit than they were.

He stood up, left the room, and went upstairs to the small bedroom above. It was brightly lit and decorated with fresh Christmas greenery. Beside the bed sat a single chair, and there were signs that someone had been sitting there recently. Poor Bob sat down in it, and after sitting in quiet thought to compose himself, he leaned over and kissed the sweet little face. He found peace with what had happened, and when he came back downstairs, he was calm and gently smiling.

They gathered around the fire together to talk while the girls and their mother continued their stitching. Bob told them about an extraordinary act of kindness from Mr. Scrooge's nephew. Bob had scarcely seen him but once before, but when the nephew ran into him on the street that afternoon and noticed Bob looking down—"just a little down, you know," Bob said—he stopped and asked what had happened to distress him.

"And when I told him," Bob said, "for he is the pleasantest, warmest gentleman you could ever hope to hear, he took my hand and said, 'I am heartily sorry for your loss, Mr. Cratchit, and heartily sorry for your good wife.' By the way, how he ever knew you were a good wife, I don't know!"

"Knew what, my dear?" asked Mrs. Cratchit.

"Why, that you are such a wonderful wife!" Bob laughed gently.

"Everybody knows that!" Peter said proudly.

"Very well observed, my boy!" cried Bob. "I certainly hope they do. 'Heartily sorry,' he said, 'for your good wife. If I can be of service to you in any way,' he said, giving me his card, 'that's where I live. Please come see me.' Now," Bob explained, "it wasn't for the sake of anything he might be able to do for us, so much as for his sincere, heartfelt way, that made it so comforting. It really seemed as if he had known our Tiny Tim himself and felt our heartbreak."

"I'm sure he's a good soul," said Mrs. Cratchit softly.

"You would be even surer of it, my dear," returned Bob, "if you saw and spoke to him. In fact, mark my words—I wouldn't be at all surprised if he helped Peter find a better position."

"Listen to that, Peter!" beamed Mrs. Cratchit.

"And then," teased one of the girls, "Peter will be keeping company with someone and setting up a home of his own!"

"Get along with you!" retorted Peter, grinning and blushing.

"It's just as likely as not one of these days," Bob chuckled, "though there's plenty of time for that, my dear. But no matter how or when we part from one another, I know none of us will ever forget poor Tiny Tim—will we?—or this first parting that has come among us."

"Never, Father!" they all cried together.

"And I know," said Bob tenderly, "I know, my children, that when we remember how patient and how gentle he was—even though he was just a little, little child—we will not quarrel easily among ourselves, and forget poor Tiny Tim in doing it."

"No, never, Father!" they all promised again.

"I am very happy," whispered Bob. "I am very happy!"

Mrs. Cratchit kissed him, his daughters kissed him, the two young Cratchits wrapped their arms around him, and Peter shook his father's hand. Spirit of Tiny Tim, your innocent, loving soul was a gift straight from God!

"Spectre," said Scrooge, "something informs me that our parting moment is at hand. I know it, though I know not how. Tell me: what man was that whom we saw lying dead upon the bed?"

The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come transported him once again through the city. As before, Scrooge tried to understand the flow of time, though there seemed to be no order in these visions, except that they all took place in the Future. The Spirit paused for nothing, moving straight ahead as if driving toward the answer Scrooge sought, until Scrooge begged him to stop for a moment.

"This court," said Scrooge, "through which we are hurrying right now—this is where my place of business is, and has been for years. I see the building! Let me look inside and see what I shall be in the days to come!"

The Spirit stopped, but its hand pointed in a different direction.

"The building is right over there!" Scrooge urged. "Why do you point away?"

The unyielding finger underwent no change.

Scrooge hurried over to the window of his office and peered inside. It was an office still, but no longer his. The furniture was different, and the figure sitting in the chair was not himself. The Phantom stood pointing as before.

Scrooge rejoined it once again, wondering where he had gone and what could have happened, following the Spirit until they reached an old iron gate. He paused to look around before entering.

A graveyard.

Here, then, beneath the ground, lay the wretched man whose name he had now to learn. And what a fitting place it was! Hemmed in on all sides by crowded houses, overgrown with weeds and wild grass—the kind of vegetation that feeds on decay rather than life—choked with too many graves, bloated from decades of burials. A fitting place indeed!

The Spirit stood among the graves and pointed down at one neglected mound. Scrooge advanced toward it, trembling with every step. The Phantom remained exactly as it had been, wrapped in its solemn shroud, but Scrooge dreaded that he saw a terrifying new meaning in its posture.

"Before I step any closer to the stone to which you point," said Scrooge, "answer me one question. Are these the shadows of things that *will* be, or are they shadows of things that *may* be, only?"

Still the Ghost pointed downward at the grave beside it.

"A person's path in life foreshadows certain ends, and if they persevere in them, to those ends they must lead," said Scrooge. "But if the path is abandoned, the ends will change! Tell me it is so with what you show me!"

The Spirit remained as immovable as ever.

Scrooge crept forward, shaking from head to foot. Following the direction of that dark finger, he looked upon the weathered headstone of the neglected grave and read his own name:

EBENEZER SCROOGE.

"Am I that man who lay upon the bed?" he cried, falling to his knees.

The finger pointed from the grave to him, and back again.

"No, Spirit! Oh no, no!"

The finger was still there.

"Spirit!" he cried, clutching tightly at its robe. "Hear me! I am not the man I was! I will not be the man I would have been if not for this journey! Why show me this if I am past all hope?"

For the first time, the spectral hand appeared to shake.

"Good Spirit," Scrooge wept, throwing himself face down on the ground before it. "Your own nature pleads for me and pities me! Assure me that I may still change these shadows you have shown me by living an altered life!"

The kind hand trembled.

"I will honor Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year! I will live in the Past, the Present, and the Future. The Spirits of all Three shall strive within me. I will not shut out the lessons that they teach. Oh, tell me I may sponge away the writing on this stone!"

In his agony, he caught the spectral hand. It sought to free itself, but Scrooge was desperate in his plea and held on with all his strength. Yet the Spirit, stronger still, repulsed him.

Holding up his hands in one last prayer to have his fate reversed, Scrooge saw an alteration come over the Phantom's hood and robe.

It shrank, collapsed, and dwindled down...

Until it was nothing more than a bedpost.
