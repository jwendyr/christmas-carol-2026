# Chapter 2: The First of the Three Spirits

When Scrooge woke up, it was pitch-black. Peering out from his bed, he could barely tell the frosted window apart from the solid, shadowy walls of his room. He was squinting into the gloom with his sharp, ferret-like eyes when the chimes of a nearby church clock struck four quarters. He held his breath and listened for the hour.

To his utter astonishment, the deep, heavy bell kept tolling—six, seven, eight, climbing steadily all the way to twelve. Then it stopped.

Twelve! It had been past two in the morning when he went to sleep. The clock had to be broken. An icicle must have jammed the gears. Twelve? Impossible!

He pressed the spring of his pocket watch to check the chime and prove the church clock wrong. The little watch clicked, chimed twelve rapid beats, and went silent.

"That makes no sense," Scrooge muttered. "I couldn't have slept through an entire day and halfway into another night. And surely nothing happened to the sun—this can't be twelve noon!"

The thought rattled him so much that he scrambled out of bed and felt his way through the dark to the window. He had to wipe away the thick frost with the sleeve of his dressing gown before he could peer outside—and even then, he could see very little. All he could tell was that the fog was as thick as ever, the air bitterly cold, and the street completely silent. There were no crowds scurrying around or raising a fuss, as there surely would have been if eternal night had swallowed the daytime world.

That was an enormous relief. After all, financial promissory notes payable to "Mr. Ebenezer Scrooge or his order within three days" would be worthless scraps of paper if days stopped existing altogether!

Scrooge climbed back into bed and thought, and thought, and thought it over and over, unable to make head or tail of it. The harder he thought, the more confused he became; and the more he tried to force himself not to think, the more his mind raced.

Marley's ghost troubled him deeply. Every time he reasoned through it and concluded it must have been a dream, his thoughts snapped right back like a released spring, dropping him right back into the very same nagging question: *Was it a dream, or wasn't it?*

Scrooge lay there wrestling with his doubts until three more quarters of an hour chimed. Suddenly, he remembered the ghost's warning: a spirit would visit him when the clock struck one. He decided right then to stay awake until the hour had passed. And since he was about as likely to fall asleep as he was to stroll straight into Heaven, staying awake was probably the easiest promise he could make.

That final quarter of an hour dragged on for so long that more than once he was convinced he had dozed off and missed the chimes.

At last, the sound echoed in his ears.

"Ding, dong!"

"A quarter past," Scrooge counted.

"Ding, dong!"

"Half-past!" Scrooge whispered.

"Ding, dong!"

"A quarter to one," Scrooge said.

"Ding, dong!"

"One o'clock!" Scrooge cried out in triumph. "And nothing happened!"

He spoke a second too soon. The great bell tolled with a deep, heavy, hollow, mournful *ONE*.

Instantly, a flash of bright light filled the room, and the curtains of his bed were pulled wide.

The curtains were drawn aside by a hand—I tell you, a real hand! Not the curtains at his feet, and not the curtains behind him, but the exact ones right in front of his face. The bed curtains parted, and Scrooge, propping himself up on his elbows in shock, found himself face-to-face with the otherworldly visitor who had drawn them. It was as close to him as I am to you right now—standing right beside your shoulder.

It was an extraordinary figure. It looked like a child, yet at the same time like an old man seen through some enchanted lens that shrank him down to a child's size without taking away his years. His hair, hanging loose past his neck and down his back, was pure white as though aged by centuries, yet his face was smooth and completely unwrinkled, glowing with the fresh, tender bloom of youth. His arms were long and surprisingly muscular, and his hands looked uncommonly strong. His bare legs and feet were gracefully formed. He wore a simple tunic of the purest white, cinched at the waist by a shimmering belt with a beautiful sheen. In his hand he held a sprig of fresh green holly; yet in vivid contrast to that winter greenery, his tunic was trimmed with bright summer flowers.

Stranger still, from the very crown of his head shot a brilliant, pure beam of light that illuminated everything around him. This bright glow was clearly why he carried a large metal extinguisher cap tucked under his arm, which he evidently used like a candle-snuffer to dim his light when needed.

Yet as Scrooge stared closer and closer, even that light was not the strangest thing about him. Just as the figure's belt sparkled here and there, shifting between light and shadow, the spirit's form kept shimmering and changing. One moment he seemed to have only one arm; then one leg; then twenty legs; now a pair of legs without a head, and now a floating head with no body at all. The parts that faded dissolved cleanly into the surrounding gloom without leaving a trace. And just as Scrooge gasped in disbelief, the spirit would coalesce again, whole, solid, and crystal clear.

"Are you the Spirit whose coming was foretold to me?" Scrooge asked.

"I am."

The voice was soft and gentle—curiously quiet, as if coming from far away rather than right beside him.

"Who and what are you?" Scrooge demanded.

"I am the Ghost of Christmas Past."

"Long past?" Scrooge inquired, noticing the spirit's small stature.

"No. Your past."

Scrooge couldn't have explained why, but he felt an overwhelming urge to see the Spirit put on that extinguisher cap, and politely asked him to cover his head.

"What!" the Ghost exclaimed. "Would you snuff out the light I bring so quickly with your worldly hands? Isn't it enough that you are one of those whose selfish passions forged this cap, forcing me for endless years to wear it pulled low over my brow?"

Scrooge quickly and humbly apologized, assuring the Spirit he meant no offense and had never knowingly tried to cap him in all his life. He then worked up the courage to ask what business had brought the ghost here.

"Your welfare," said the Ghost.

Scrooge said he was deeply grateful, though he couldn't help thinking that a solid, uninterrupted night of sleep would have done his welfare a lot more good. The Spirit must have read his thoughts, for it answered at once:

"Your reclamation, then. Pay close attention!"

The Spirit reached out with its strong hand and took him gently by the arm.

"Rise, and walk with me."

It would have been completely useless for Scrooge to argue that the weather and the hour were hardly suitable for a stroll; that his bed was cozy while the temperature outside was well below freezing; that he was dressed only in slippers, a dressing gown, and a nightcap; or that he was nursing a nasty head cold. The Spirit's grip, though as gentle as a feather, could not be resisted. Scrooge got out of bed. But when he saw the ghost heading straight for the window, he grabbed the spirit's robe in terror.

"I am only human," Scrooge protested. "I'll fall!"

"Just a touch of my hand here," the Spirit replied, placing a hand over Scrooge's heart, "and you will be supported through far more than this."

The moment those words were spoken, they passed straight through the wall and stood on an open country road bordered by fields on both sides. The city had vanished completely—not a single trace of London remained. The gloom and dense fog had disappeared along with it, replaced by a crisp, bright winter day with fresh snow coating the ground.

"Good heavens!" Scrooge gasped, clasping his hands together as he looked around. "I grew up here! I was a boy in this place!"

The Spirit looked upon him with a gentle gaze. That brief, soft touch upon Scrooge's heart still lingered warmly in his chest. He breathed in a thousand familiar scents drifting on the breeze, each one unlocking a flood of memories—a thousand forgotten thoughts, childhood hopes, simple joys, and worries from long, long ago.

"Your lip is trembling," the Ghost noticed. "And what is that on your cheek?"

Scrooge mumbled, his voice catching with an uncharacteristic crack, that it was just a blemish, and begged the Ghost to lead the way wherever he wished.

"Do you remember the road?" the Spirit asked.

"Remember it?" Scrooge cried with sudden passion. "I could walk it blindfolded!"

"Strange that you forgot it for so many years," the Ghost remarked. "Let us move on."

They walked down the road, Scrooge pointing out every gatepost, fence, and tree. Soon a little market town appeared ahead, complete with its arched bridge, church steeple, and winding river.

A herd of shaggy ponies came trotting toward them, carrying boys on their backs who waved and shouted cheerfully to other boys riding in farm carts and country wagons. Every single boy was bursting with high spirits, calling back and forth until the wide-open fields echoed with joyful noise, and the crisp winter air seemed to laugh along with them.

"These are only shadows of things that once were," the Ghost explained. "They cannot see or hear us."

The cheerful travelers passed by, and Scrooge recognized and named every single one of them. Why was his heart overflowing with joy just to see them? Why did his cold, flinty eyes glisten with tears, and why did his pulse race with excitement as they trotted past? Why did he feel so thrilled when they called out "Merry Christmas!" to each other as they split off at crossroads and country lanes heading home for the holidays?

What was "Merry Christmas" to Scrooge? Bah! What good had Christmas ever done for him?

And yet, his heart sang.

"The schoolhouse is not entirely empty," the Ghost remarked. "One solitary child, forgotten by his friends, is still left behind."

Scrooge said he knew it. And he broke down and sobbed.

They turned off the main road down a familiar lane and soon drew near a grand red-brick building with a little bell cupola topped by a weather vane perched on the roof. It had once been an impressive estate, but now showed obvious signs of decay. The large outbuildings were abandoned, their walls damp and covered in moss, the windowpanes cracked, and the iron gates rusted and sagging. Chickens clucked and strutted about the empty stables, while weeds and tall grass choked the carriage houses and sheds.

Inside was just as bleak. Stepping into the drafty entryway and looking through the open doorways, they saw cavernous, chilly rooms with barely any furniture. A musty, earthy smell hung in the air—a cold, barren atmosphere that instantly brought back memories of waking up in freezing darkness by candlelight and never having quite enough to eat.

The Ghost and Scrooge walked across the hall toward a door at the back of the house. It swung open to reveal a long, bleak, dreary classroom made even more barren by rows of plain wooden benches and desks. At one desk, near a pitiful little fire, sat a lonely boy reading all by himself.

Scrooge sat down on an empty bench and wept to see his poor, abandoned younger self just as he had been.

Every faint echo in the empty house, every scuttle of mice behind the baseboards, every slow drip from the half-frozen gutter out in the bleak courtyard, every winter breeze sighing through the bare branches of a solitary poplar tree, every creak of an unlatched shed door, even the soft clicking of the embers in the grate—everything touched Scrooge's hardened heart, melting his defenses and letting his tears flow freely.

The Spirit tapped him on the arm and pointed toward his younger self, totally absorbed in his book.

Suddenly, a man dressed in foreign desert robes, astonishingly real and clear, appeared just outside the window. He had a woodcutter's axe tucked into his sash and led a donkey laden with firewood by its reins.

"Look, it's Ali Baba!" Scrooge cried out in absolute delight. "Dear old, honest Ali Baba! Yes, yes, I remember! One Christmas, when that lonely child was left behind all by himself, Ali Baba appeared to him for the very first time, just like that! Poor boy! And Valentine," Scrooge babbled, "and his wild brother, Orson—look, there they go! And what's his name—the fellow left asleep in his underclothes outside the Gate of Damascus—don't you see him? And look at the Sultan's groom turned upside down by the Genie! There he is, standing right on his head! Serves him right, too! I'm glad of it! What business did he have trying to marry the princess?"

To hear Scrooge pouring his whole heart and soul into these childhood stories—his voice swinging wildly between laughter and tears, his face flushed and alive with wonder—would have stunned his business associates back in London beyond belief.

"And there's the parrot!" Scrooge cheered. "Bright green body, yellow tail, and a crest on his head like a little head of lettuce—there he is! 'Poor Robin Crusoe!' he used to squawk when Robinson came back after sailing around his island. 'Poor Robin Crusoe, where have you been, Robin Crusoe?' The poor castaway thought he was losing his mind, but it was just the bird talking! And look, there goes Friday, sprinting for his life down toward the creek! Hey! Look out! Hooray!"

Then, in a sudden shift that was completely unlike his usual stony self, he looked back at the lonely boy. "Poor kid," he whispered softly, and broke down in tears once more.

"I wish..." Scrooge murmured, reaching into his coat pocket and looking down after wiping his damp eyes with his sleeve. "Ah, but it's too late now."

"What troubles you?" the Spirit asked.

"Nothing," Scrooge said quickly. "Nothing at all. Just... there was a boy singing a Christmas carol outside my door last night. I really wish I'd given him something, that's all."

The Ghost offered a knowing, thoughtful smile and waved its hand. "Let us look upon another Christmas."

As the Spirit spoke, Scrooge's younger self grew taller, and the room became older, darker, and dustier. The wooden wall panels shrank, cracks spiderwebbed across the windowpanes, and chunks of plaster broke away from the ceiling, exposing the bare wooden slats underneath. How this magical change happened, Scrooge had no idea. He only knew it was entirely true to his memory—that everything had happened exactly like this, and that once again, he was left behind, all alone, while every other boy had gone home for the joyous holidays.

He wasn't reading this time. He was pacing back and forth across the bare floorboards in despair. Scrooge looked at the Ghost, shook his head sorrowfully, and stared toward the doorway with anxious longing.

The door flew open. A little girl, much younger than the boy, came bursting into the room. She threw her arms around his neck, showering him with kisses and calling him her "Dear, dear brother!"

"I've come to bring you home, dear brother!" the child cried, clapping her small hands and bubbling over with laughter. "To bring you home, home, home!"

"Home, little Fan?" the boy asked in disbelief.

"Yes!" she beamed, radiating pure joy. "Home for good! Home forever and ever! Father is so much kinder than he used to be—home feels like Heaven now! He spoke so gently to me one night as I was heading to bed that I gathered up my courage to ask him one more time if you could come home. And he said yes, you could! He even sent me in a carriage to fetch you! And you're going to be a man now," she added, her eyes wide with excitement, "and you never have to come back to this awful place again! But first, we get to spend the whole Christmas season together and have the merriest time in the whole wide world!"

"You're quite a grown woman now, little Fan!" the boy laughed.

She clapped her hands and giggled, stretching up to ruffle his hair. Finding herself too short, she laughed again and stood on tiptoe to wrap him in another tight hug. Then, with eager, childish excitement, she began tugging him toward the door—and he, more than glad to leave, walked right alongside her.

A booming, dreadful voice bellowed from the hallway: "Bring down young Master Scrooge's trunk!"

There stood the headmaster himself. He glared down at young Ebenezer with terrifying condescension, rattling the boy's nerves simply by offering a stiff, chilling handshake. He then escorted Ebenezer and his sister into the freezing parlor—a frigid dungeon of a room where the wall maps and the world globes sitting by the windows looked frosted over with cold wax.

Here, the headmaster brought out a bottle of suspiciously thin, sour wine and a brick of astonishingly dense fruitcake, serving small helpings of these supposed treats to the children. Meanwhile, he sent a skinny house servant outside to offer a glass of "something" to the carriage driver. The driver replied that he was much obliged to the gentleman, but if it was from the same sour barrel he'd tasted last time, he'd rather pass!

With Master Scrooge's trunk now strapped securely to the carriage roof, the children said goodbye to the schoolmaster with genuine relief. They climbed aboard and rolled merrily down the winding driveway, the spinning carriage wheels sending showers of frost and snow spraying off the evergreen hedges like ocean surf.

"She was always a fragile soul—a strong wind might have carried her away," the Ghost remarked. "Yet she had an immense heart."

"She truly did," Scrooge agreed warmly. "You speak the truth, Spirit. I won't deny it—God forbid!"

"She grew into a woman before she passed away," said the Ghost, "and had children of her own, if I remember correctly."

"One child," Scrooge replied quietly.

"That's right," said the Ghost. "Your nephew."

A heavy pang of guilt washed over Scrooge. He answered in a strained whisper: "Yes."

Though they had only just stepped away from the old schoolhouse, they were suddenly standing in the bustling streets of a lively city. Ghostly figures hurried past in every direction; spectral wagons and carriages jostled for room on the cobblestones amid the clamor and energy of an active metropolis. The brightly decorated shop windows made it obvious that it was Christmas once again, but evening had fallen, and lanterns illuminated the streets.

The Ghost stopped outside the doorway of a large warehouse and asked Scrooge if he recognized the place.

"Recognize it?" Scrooge cried out. "I was an apprentice here!"

They stepped inside. Sitting behind a desk so ridiculously high that if he had been two inches taller he would have hit his head on the ceiling was a jolly older gentleman wearing a wool Welsh wig.

Scrooge beamed with excitement: "Why, it's old Fezziwig! Bless his heart—it's Fezziwig, alive and well!"

Old Fezziwig set down his quill and glanced up at the clock on the wall, which showed seven o'clock. He rubbed his hands together with relish, adjusted his roomy vest, and chuckled with his entire body, from the tips of his toes right up to his generous forehead. Then, in a warm, rich, booming, merry voice, he shouted:

"Yo ho, there! Ebenezer! Dick!"

Scrooge's younger self, now a spry young man, came trotting into the room, accompanied by his fellow apprentice.

"Dick Wilkins, of course!" Scrooge exclaimed to the Ghost. "Bless my soul, yes! There he is! Dick was so fond of me back then. Good old Dick! Ah, dear me!"

"Yo ho, my boys!" Fezziwig declared. "No more work tonight! It's Christmas Eve, Dick! It's Christmas, Ebenezer! Let's get the window shutters closed," cried old Fezziwig with a sharp clap of his hands, "before you can even say Jack Robinson!"

You wouldn't believe the speed of those two young fellows! They dashed out into the street with the heavy wooden shutters—one, two, three—slung them into place—four, five, six—locked and barred them tight—seven, eight, nine—and sprinted back inside before you could count to twelve, panting like racehorses!

"Hilli-ho!" cried old Fezziwig, hopping down from his towering desk with astonishing agility. "Clear the floor, boys! Let's make plenty of room! Come along, Dick! Cheer up, Ebenezer!"

Clear the floor? There wasn't an obstacle on earth they wouldn't have moved—or couldn't have moved—under old Fezziwig's cheerful supervision. Within sixty seconds, it was finished! Every crate and chair was whisked away as if packed off into permanent retirement. The floorboards were swept and lightly dampened, the oil lamps trimmed bright, fresh logs piled high onto the blazing fireplace, and presto! The drafty warehouse was transformed into the coziest, warmest, brightest ballroom you could ever hope to see on a chilly winter night.

In came a fiddler clutching a sheet-music book. He climbed right up behind the towering desk, turning it into his personal orchestra pit, and began tuning his violin with screeches that sounded like fifty stomachaches!

In swept Mrs. Fezziwig, one magnificent, beaming smile.

In bounced the three lovely Fezziwig daughters, radiant and charming.

In trailed the six hopeful young suitors whose hearts they were busy breaking.

In poured all the young clerks and shop hands working for the firm.

In came the housemaid, bringing along her cousin the baker.

In came the cook, accompanied by her brother's best buddy, the milkman.

In sneaked the apprentice boy from across the street, who everyone suspected wasn't getting enough to eat from his boss; he tried to duck out of sight behind the girl from down the block, whose ears had visibly been boxed by her grumpy mistress.

In they all came, one after another—some shy, some bold, some graceful, some clumsy, some nudging, some tugging—in they flooded, every which way!

And off they went, twenty couples hitting the floor at once! Hands across, circle half-round and back again; marching down the center and back to the top; spinning round and round in joyful clusters; the lead couple turning up in the wrong spot every time; the next couple darting ahead the moment they arrived; until eventually everyone was dancing as a lead couple with nobody left at the bottom to follow!

When the lively chaos finally tangled up, old Fezziwig clapped his hands, calling out, "Well done, everyone!"

The overheated fiddler immediately buried his face in a frothy mug of dark ale set out just for him. But refusing to rest, the moment he came up for air he started fiddling again—even though the dancers hadn't caught their breath yet—as if an imaginary rival fiddler had been carried off on a stretcher and he was determined to outplay him or drop dead trying!

There were more dances, and silly party games, and more dances again. There was sweet cake, and mugs of warm spiced wine, and a massive platter of cold roast beef, and another of cold boiled ham, along with spiced mince pies and pitchers of foaming ale.

Yet the crowning moment of the night arrived right after the feast. That clever rascal of a fiddler—a master of his craft who knew how to work a room better than anyone alive—struck up the opening notes of "Sir Roger de Coverley."

Old Fezziwig stepped right out into the middle of the floor to partner Mrs. Fezziwig! They took the lead spot as the head couple, and they had their work cut out for them—twenty-four eager couples lined up down the room, every one of them ready to dance their hearts out with no intention of just casually strolling through the steps!

Even if there had been twice as many dancers—no, four times as many!—old Fezziwig would have outdanced them all, and Mrs. Fezziwig right beside him. As for her, she was his perfect match in every possible way. If that isn't the highest praise, tell me a better compliment and I'll gladly use it!

A literal glow seemed to emanate from Fezziwig's calves. They flashed and shone through every spin and leap like twin full moons. You could never guess what fancy step his feet would pull off next.

Together, old Fezziwig and Mrs. Fezziwig breezed through the entire routine—advance and retreat, join hands with your partner, bow and curtsy, spin like a corkscrew, thread the needle, and skip right back to your spot! At the grand finish, Fezziwig performed a lightning-fast midair leg cross—so sharp and nimble that his legs seemed to give a cheeky wink—landing back on his feet without the slightest wobble!

When the clock struck eleven, the merry party drew to a close. Mr. and Mrs. Fezziwig stationed themselves on either side of the front door. They shook hands with each guest one by one as they bundled up and stepped out, wishing every single person a very Merry Christmas.

When everyone had left except the two apprentices, the Fezziwigs gave them the exact same warm handshake and holiday blessing. As the happy chatter faded into the night, the two young lads turned in to their own beds, tucked snugly under a work counter in the back of the shop.

Through the entire evening, Scrooge had behaved like a man possessed. His heart and soul were completely wrapped up in the scene, cheering along with his younger self. He verified every single detail, remembered every face, laughed along with every joke, and felt a strange, thrilling warmth swelling inside his chest.

It wasn't until young Ebenezer and Dick blew out their candles and went to sleep that Scrooge remembered the Ghost. He turned and realized the spirit was watching him intently, the bright beam of light atop its head blazing clear and pure.

"Such a tiny thing," the Ghost said casually, "to fill these foolish folks with so much gratitude."

"Tiny?" Scrooge echoed in disbelief.

The Spirit gestured for him to listen to the two apprentices under the counter, who were pouring their hearts out in praise of good old Fezziwig. Once Scrooge had listened, the Ghost spoke again:

"Well, isn't it? He spent barely a few pounds of your mortal money—maybe three or four. Is that really worth so much lavish praise?"

"It's not about the money!" Scrooge shot back defensively. Without realizing it, he was speaking from the heart of the young apprentice he used to be, not the bitter old miser he had become. "It's not that at all, Spirit! A boss has the power to make our lives happy or miserable—to make our work feel light as a feather or a crushing burden, a joy or pure drudgery! Even if that power only comes through kind words and welcoming smiles—little everyday things so small you can't measure or count them on a ledger—so what? The happiness he gives is worth just as much as if it had cost a king's fortune!"

He suddenly felt the Spirit's piercing gaze upon him, and stopped short.

"What is it?" the Ghost asked.

"Nothing in particular," Scrooge mumbled.

"I think there is something," the Ghost pressed gently.

"No," Scrooge said softly, looking away. "No. I... I just wish I could say a word or two to my clerk Bob right now. That's all."

As he spoke that wish aloud, his younger self extinguished the last lamp, and the warehouse dissolved away. Scrooge and the Ghost were standing side by side once more under the open sky.

"My time grows short," the Spirit noted quietly. "Hurry!"

The command wasn't addressed to Scrooge or anyone visible, yet its effect was immediate.

Once again, Scrooge saw himself. He was older now—a man in the prime of his life. His face hadn't yet set into the cruel, rigid scowl of his later years, but the earliest lines of worry and greed had begun to carve their way into his features. His eyes darted with a hungry, restless ambition that revealed the dark seed taking root in his soul, forecasting the deep shadow that would soon consume him.

He was not alone. He sat beside a lovely young woman dressed in simple mourning clothes. Tears glistened in her eyes, catching the pure light radiating from the Ghost of Christmas Past.

"It matters very little to you now," she said softly. "Hardly anything at all. Another idol has taken my place in your heart. And if it can comfort and sustain you in the years ahead as I once hoped to do, I have no right to complain."

"What idol has taken your place?" the younger Scrooge countered defensively.

"A golden one," she answered.

"That is the hypocritical way of the world!" he argued bitterly. "Society treats nothing more cruelly than poverty, yet nothing does it pretend to condemn more harshly than the pursuit of wealth!"

"You fear the world too much," she replied with gentle sadness. "All your other youthful dreams have melted away into a single obsession: shielding yourself from poverty and contempt. I have watched your nobler ideals wither and drop away, one by one, until a single drive—profit—has consumed you entirely. Isn't that the truth?"

"And so what if it is?" he snapped. "Even if I've grown wiser about the world, what difference does it make? My feelings toward you haven't changed."

She shook her head softly.

"Have they?" he challenged.

"Our promise to marry is an old one," she said. "We made it when we were both poor, and happy to remain so until our patient hard work could improve our fortunes. But you have changed. When you made that promise, you were a completely different man."

"I was just a kid then," he muttered impatiently.

"Your own conscience tells you that you weren't the man you are today," she answered. "I have stayed the same. What promised us a lifetime of happiness when we shared one heart will bring only misery now that our paths have split. How often and how painfully I have wept over this, I won't say. It is enough that I have thought it through, and that I can now set you free."

"Did I ever ask to be released?" he demanded.

"In words? No, never."

"Then in what way?"

"In your changed character. In your hardened spirit. In the selfish way you now live, and the cold ambition that drives you. In everything that once made my love mean anything to you. If we had never met until today," she said, looking straight into his eyes with steady sorrow, "tell me honestly: would you pursue me now? Would you try to win my heart today? Ah, no. You wouldn't."

He seemed to recognize the painful truth of her words, despite himself. Still, he forced out a weak reply: "You don't think so."

"Heaven knows I would love to believe otherwise if I could," she answered. "When the truth stares you in the face, you can't look away. If you were free today, could even I honestly believe that you would choose a penniless girl? You, who weigh every decision, even your closest confidences, on the scale of profit and loss? And if you did choose me in a momentary lapse from your golden rule, don't I know that bitter regret would follow soon after? I do know it. And so, I release you. I do it with all my heart, out of love for the man you used to be."

He opened his mouth to protest, but she turned her face away and continued gently:

"You may feel a sting of pain in this—and part of me, remembering the past, almost hopes you do. But it won't last long. Very soon, you will dismiss the memory of our love as a bad investment—an unprofitable daydream you were lucky to wake up from. May you find true happiness in the life you have chosen."

She stood up and walked away. And so they parted forever.

"Spirit!" Scrooge cried in anguish. "Show me no more! Take me home! Why do you take pleasure in torturing me?"

"One shadow more," the Ghost insisted.

"No more!" Scrooge pleaded, covering his eyes. "No more! I don't want to see it! Please, show me no more!"

But the relentless Spirit gripped him firmly by both arms and compelled him to witness what came next.

They stood in another room in another home. It wasn't large or lavish, but it was filled to the brim with warmth and cozy comfort.

Sitting beside the hearth was a lovely young woman, so strikingly like Belle that Scrooge was certain it was her—until he noticed Belle herself, now a beautiful, mature mother, sitting across from her daughter.

The room was in an uproar of delightful chaos. There were more children running around than Scrooge's frazzled mind could count. And unlike the calm cattle in the old poem that grazed as if forty were one, each of these children was carrying on as if they were forty kids packed into one body! The racket was wild beyond belief, but nobody minded in the least. In fact, both mother and daughter were laughing until their sides ached, thoroughly enjoying the commotion.

Soon the daughter jumped into the playful fray, only to be happily ambushed by the little bandits! What wouldn't I have given to be one of those rascals! Not that I could ever have been quite so rough—no, never! I wouldn't have mussed her lovely braided hair for all the money in the world. And as for pulling off her dainty shoe, bless my soul, I wouldn't have dared do that to save my neck! Nor could I have playfully wrapped my arms around her waist as that bold brood did; I would have feared my arm would freeze in a permanent circle as punishment and never straighten out again!

And yet, I admit, I would have loved beyond words to brush her cheek, to ask her a question just to hear her sweet voice answer, to gaze into her sparkling eyes without making her blush, or to catch a lock of her golden hair—a treasure more precious than any jewel. In short, I confess I would have given anything to share the innocent closeness of a child, while possessing the heart of a man who truly understood its worth.

Suddenly, a loud knock rattled the front door. A mad rush followed! The daughter, her hair mussed and dress pulled this way and that, was swept along like the queen of a wildly cheering parade right to the front hall.

There stood the father, freshly home from work, accompanied by a delivery man piled high with Christmas toys and wrapped packages.

Oh, the deafening cheers and wild scramble that followed! The poor delivery man was completely overwhelmed by the loving mob! The kids used chairs like stepladders to climb him, diving straight into his coat pockets, snatching brown-paper parcels, hanging from his necktie, wrapping their arms around his neck, patting his back, and kicking his shins in uncontainable affection!

Each unwrapped gift was greeted with gasps of awe and squeals of pure joy! Then came a sudden panic when someone shrieked that the baby had been caught trying to swallow a toy dollhouse frying pan, and was suspected of having eaten a fake roast turkey glued to a wooden plate! Then the massive wave of relief when it turned out to be a false alarm! The laughter, the gratitude, the pure holiday magic—words can't do it justice.

Eventually, one by one, the excited kids and their treasures were shepherded out of the parlor and marched up the stairs to bed, where the noisy house finally drifted into peaceful quiet.

Now Scrooge leaned forward, watching with an aching heart. The father sat down by the glowing fireplace, his loving daughter resting her head upon his shoulder while her mother sat close beside them.

As Scrooge looked upon the scene, the realization struck him that a daughter just as graceful, just as bright, and just as full of life might have called *him* father. She could have been the fresh springtime blossom in the lonely, barren winter of his old age. His vision blurred with thick tears.

"Belle," the husband said, turning toward his wife with a gentle smile, "I ran across an old friend of yours this afternoon."

"Oh? Who was it?"

"Take a guess!"

"How could I guess?" she teased, and then, catching the look in his eye, she laughed softly. "Wait... don't tell me. Mr. Scrooge?"

"Mr. Scrooge indeed. I walked past his office window. The shutters were open, and he had a lone candle burning inside, so I couldn't help seeing him. I hear his business partner is on his deathbed. And there Scrooge sat, all by himself. Utterly alone in the world, I truly believe."

"Spirit!" Scrooge pleaded in a broken, trembling voice. "Take me away from here!"

"I warned you these were shadows of what has been," the Ghost answered softly. "Do not blame me for what they are."

"Take me away!" Scrooge cried out, sobbing. "I cannot bear it!"

He turned upon the Ghost. As he looked into the Spirit's visage, he saw flickering reflections of every single face the ghost had shown him—his younger self, little Fan, Fezziwig, Belle. Desperate to escape the agonizing visions, he lunged and grappled with the spirit.

"Leave me alone! Take me back! Haunt me no more!"

It was hardly a fight—the Ghost offered no physical resistance whatsoever, remaining calm and completely unfazed by Scrooge's frantic efforts. But Scrooge noticed that the beacon of light atop the Spirit's head was blazing with blinding brilliance. Sensing instinctively that this light was the source of the ghost's uncanny power over him, Scrooge grabbed the metal extinguisher cap. With a desperate heave, he slammed it down over the Spirit's head.

The Ghost sank down beneath the cap until the metal cone covered its entire body. Yet no matter how hard Scrooge threw his weight onto the extinguisher, pressing down with every ounce of strength he possessed, he could not snuff out the light. It poured out from beneath the rim in an unbroken, dazzling flood that spilled across the floor.

An overwhelming wave of exhaustion washed over him, pulling him down into an irresistible, heavy drowsiness. He realized he was back in his own bedchamber. His grip relaxed on the cap. He gave it one last weak press, staggered blindly toward his bed, and collapsed onto the mattress, instantly plunging into a deep, dreamless sleep.
