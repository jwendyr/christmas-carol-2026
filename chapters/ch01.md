# Chapter 1: Marley's Ghost

Marley was dead, to begin with. There was no doubt about that whatsoever. The burial register had been signed by the clergyman, the town clerk, the undertaker, and the chief mourner. Scrooge signed it too. And Scrooge's name carried weight at the Royal Exchange for anything he chose to put his hand to.

Old Marley was as dead as a doornail.

Mind you! I don't mean to pretend that I know from personal experience why a doornail is supposed to be especially dead. Personally, I would have thought a coffin nail was the deadest piece of ironmongery in the hardware trade. But the wisdom of our ancestors lives in that phrase, and my unhallowed hands won't mess with it, or the whole country might fall apart. So permit me to repeat, emphatically: Marley was as dead as a doornail.

Did Scrooge know he was dead? Of course he did. How could he not? He and Scrooge had been business partners for who knows how many years. Scrooge was his sole executor, his sole administrator, his sole legal heir, his sole residuary legatee, his only friend, and his only mourner. And even Scrooge wasn't so heartbroken by the sad occasion that it stopped him from being an excellent businessman on the very day of the funeral, sealing the deal with an undeniable bargain.

The mention of Marley's funeral brings me right back to where I started. There is no doubt that Marley was dead. You have to clearly understand this, or nothing miraculous can come of the story I am about to tell. If we weren't completely convinced that Hamlet's father had died before the play even started, there would be nothing more remarkable about his ghost taking a midnight stroll in a biting east wind along the castle walls than there would be in any other middle-aged gentleman walking out after dark into a breezy spot—say, St. Paul's Churchyard—just to startle his poor son out of his wits.

Scrooge never painted over Old Marley's name. There it stayed, years later, right above the warehouse door: *Scrooge and Marley*. The firm was still known as Scrooge and Marley. Sometimes people new to the business called Scrooge "Scrooge," and sometimes they called him "Marley." He answered to both names. It was all the same to him.

Oh, but he was a tight-fisted penny-pincher at the grindstone, that Scrooge! A squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, greedy old sinner! Hard and sharp as flint, from which no steel had ever struck a single spark of generous fire. Secretive, self-contained, and as solitary as an oyster. The bitter cold inside him froze his aging features, nipped his pointed nose, shriveled his cheeks, and stiffened his walk. It turned his eyes red and his thin lips blue, and rasped out shrewdly in his grating voice. A frosty layer of white coated his head, his eyebrows, and his wiry chin. He carried his own freezing temperature wherever he went. He iced his office during the dog days of summer, and didn't thaw it by a single degree for Christmas.

External heat and cold had virtually no effect on Scrooge. No warmth could warm him; no winter storm could chill him. No howling wind was bitterer than he was, no falling snow more stubborn in its purpose, no driving rain less open to mercy. Bad weather simply had no hold on him. The heaviest downpours, snow, hail, and sleet could boast an advantage over him in only one way: they often came down generously, while Scrooge never came down with a penny.

Nobody ever stopped him on the street to say with a cheerful smile, "My dear Scrooge, how are you? When are you coming over to visit?" No beggars pleaded with him for spare change; no children ever asked him what time it was; not once in his entire life did any man or woman ask Scrooge for directions. Even the guide dogs of the blind seemed to know him. When they spotted him coming, they would tug their owners into doorways and down side alleys, wagging their tails as if to say, "No eyes at all are better than an evil eye, dark master!"

But what did Scrooge care? That was the very thing he liked best. To edge his way along the crowded paths of life, warning all human sympathy to keep its distance—that was pure heaven to Scrooge.

Once upon a time—of all the good days in the year, on Christmas Eve—old Scrooge sat busy in his countinghouse. The weather was cold, bleak, and biting, and thick with fog. Outside in the courtyard, he could hear people wheezing as they hurried past, beating their chests with their hands and stomping their feet on the cobblestones to keep warm. The city clocks had only just struck three, but it was pitch-dark already—it hadn't been properly light all day. Candles flickered in the windows of neighboring offices like reddish smears against the heavy brown air. Fog poured through every crack and keyhole, and hung so dense outside that even though the courtyard was very narrow, the houses opposite looked like mere phantoms. Watching the dingy smog roll down and swallow everything, you might have thought Nature itself lived right around the corner, brewing up storms on a massive industrial scale.

The door to Scrooge's office stayed open so he could keep an eye on his clerk, who was copying letters in a dismal little cubicle next door—a sort of freezing tank. Scrooge had a tiny coal fire, but the clerk's fire was so much smaller that it looked like a single glowing ember. Yet the clerk couldn't add more coal, because Scrooge kept the coal box in his own private room. As sure as the clerk walked in carrying the shovel, his boss would warn him that they might have to part ways. So the clerk wrapped his white woolen scarf tightly around his neck and tried to warm his frozen hands over the candle flame. Lacking a powerful imagination, he failed miserably.

"Merry Christmas, Uncle! God bless you!" cried a cheerful voice.

It was Scrooge's nephew, who arrived so briskly through the fog that his greeting was the first notice Scrooge had of his approach.

"Bah!" said Scrooge. "Humbug!"

The nephew had walked so fast through the frost and mist that he was positively glowing. His face was rosy and handsome, his eyes sparkled, and his breath puffed out in clouds of steam.

"Christmas a humbug, Uncle?" said Scrooge's nephew. "You don't really mean that, surely?"

"I do," said Scrooge. "Merry Christmas! What right do you have to be merry? What reason do you have to be merry? You're poor enough."

"Come on, then," the nephew replied cheerfully. "What right do you have to be dismal? What reason do you have to be morose? You're rich enough."

Having no better answer ready on the spur of the moment, Scrooge grunted, "Bah!" again, and followed it up with another "Humbug."

"Don't be cross, Uncle!" said the nephew.

"What else can I be," replied the uncle, "when I live in such a world of fools as this? Merry Christmas! Out upon 'Merry Christmas'! What's Christmas to you but a time for paying bills without money? A time for finding yourself a year older, but not an hour richer? A time for balancing your books and having every single entry across twelve long months stacked dead against you? If I had my way," Scrooge added indignantly, "every idiot who runs around with 'Merry Christmas' on their lips should be boiled in their own Christmas pudding, and buried with a stake of holly driven through their heart. They should!"

"Uncle!" the nephew pleaded.

"Nephew!" returned the uncle sternly. "Keep Christmas in your own way, and let me keep it in mine."

"Keep it!" repeated Scrooge's nephew. "But you don't keep it at all!"

"Then let me leave it alone," said Scrooge. "Much good may it do you! Much good it has ever done you!"

"There are many things from which I've derived good without profiting in cash, I dare say," returned the nephew. "Christmas among the rest. But I am sure I have always thought of Christmas, whenever it rolls around—apart from the sacred reverence due to its holy name and origin, if anything belonging to it can be set apart from that—as a good time. A kind, forgiving, charitable, pleasant time. It is the only time I know of, in the long calendar of the year, when men and women seem by one consent to open their shut-up hearts freely, and think of people below them as if they really were fellow passengers to the grave, and not another race of creatures bound on different journeys. And therefore, Uncle, even though it has never put a single scrap of gold or silver in my pocket, I believe that it has done me good, and will do me good. And I say: God bless it!"

The clerk in the tank couldn't help applauding. Instantly realizing how improper that was, he poked the fire and accidentally extinguished the last frail spark forever.

"Let me hear another sound from you," said Scrooge, "and you'll keep your Christmas by losing your job! You're quite the powerful speaker, sir," he added, turning back to his nephew. "I wonder you don't run for Parliament."

"Don't be angry, Uncle. Come! Dine with us tomorrow."

Scrooge told him he would see him—yes, indeed he did. He went the full length of the expression, and said he would see him in hell first.

"But why?" cried Scrooge's nephew. "Why?"

"Why did you get married?" said Scrooge.

"Because I fell in love."

"Because you fell in love!" growled Scrooge, as if that were the only thing in the world more ridiculous than a merry Christmas. "Good afternoon!"

"Come now, Uncle, you never came to visit me before that happened. Why give it as a reason for not coming now?"

"Good afternoon," said Scrooge.

"I want nothing from you. I ask nothing of you. Why can't we be friends?"

"Good afternoon," said Scrooge.

"I am sorry with all my heart to find you so determined. We have never had a quarrel that I started. But I made this effort in honor of Christmas, and I'll keep my Christmas spirit to the end. So: a Merry Christmas, Uncle!"

"Good afternoon!" said Scrooge.

"And a Happy New Year!"

"Good afternoon!" said Scrooge.

His nephew left the room without an angry word, in spite of everything. He stopped at the outer door to wish the season's greetings to the clerk, who, shivering as he was, was still warmer inside than Scrooge, for he returned them cordially.

"There's another lunatic," muttered Scrooge, who overheard him. "My clerk, earning fifteen shillings a week with a wife and family to feed, talking about a merry Christmas. I'll check myself into a madhouse."

In letting Scrooge's nephew out, the clerk had let two other gentlemen in. They were portly, pleasant-looking men, and now stood in Scrooge's office with their hats removed. They held books and papers in their hands, and bowed politely to him.

"Scrooge and Marley's, I believe," said one of the gentlemen, consulting his list. "Do I have the pleasure of addressing Mr. Scrooge or Mr. Marley?"

"Mr. Marley has been dead these seven years," Scrooge replied. "He died seven years ago this very night."

"We have no doubt his generosity is well represented by his surviving partner," said the gentleman, presenting his credentials.

It certainly was, for they had been two kindred spirits. At the ominous word "generosity," Scrooge frowned, shook his head, and handed the papers right back.

"At this festive season of the year, Mr. Scrooge," said the gentleman, picking up a pen, "it is more desirable than ever that we make some slight provision for the poor and destitute, who suffer greatly at the present time. Many thousands lack the common necessities of life; hundreds of thousands lack common comforts, sir."

"Are there no prisons?" asked Scrooge.

"Plenty of prisons," said the gentleman, laying down the pen again.

"And the union workhouses?" demanded Scrooge. "Are they still in operation?"

"They are. Though," returned the gentleman, "I wish I could say they were not."

"The treadmill and the Poor Law are in full swing, then?" said Scrooge.

"Both are very busy, sir."

"Oh! From what you said at first, I was afraid something had occurred to stop them in their useful course," said Scrooge. "I'm very glad to hear it."

"Under the impression that they scarcely furnish Christian cheer for mind or body to the multitude," replied the gentleman, "a few of us are trying to raise a fund to buy the poor some food, drink, and means of warmth. We choose this time because it is a season, above all others, when want is keenly felt and abundance rejoices. How much may I put you down for?"

"Nothing!" Scrooge replied.

"You wish to remain anonymous?"

"I wish to be left alone," said Scrooge. "Since you ask me what I wish, gentlemen, that is my answer. I don't celebrate Christmas myself, and I can't afford to make idle people merry. I help support the institutions I mentioned—they cost enough, and those who are badly off must go there."

"Many cannot go there, sir. And many would rather die."

"If they would rather die," said Scrooge, "they had better do it, and decrease the surplus population! Besides—excuse me—I don't know that for a fact."

"You could know it," observed the gentleman.

"It's not my business," Scrooge returned. "It's enough for a man to understand his own business, and not interfere with other people's. Mine occupies me constantly. Good afternoon, gentlemen!"

Seeing clearly that it would be useless to pursue the matter, the gentlemen withdrew. Scrooge resumed his work with a higher opinion of himself, and in a mood far more smug and facetious than was usual for him.

Meanwhile, the fog and darkness grew so thick that men ran about with blazing torches, offering to walk ahead of carriage horses and guide them on their way. The ancient tower of a church—whose gruff old bell was always peeping slyly down at Scrooge through a Gothic window in the stone—became invisible. It struck the hours and quarter-hours up in the clouds, with quivering vibrations afterward as if its teeth were chattering in its frozen head. The cold became intense. Around the corner on the main street, workers repairing the gas pipes had lit a roaring fire in a brazier. A ragged crowd of men and boys gathered around it, warming their hands and blinking their eyes before the blaze in pure delight. A forgotten water pump out on the street sullenly froze over, its overflow turning to bitter, misanthropic ice. The bright glow of shop windows, where sprigs of holly and red berries crackled in the lamp heat, made pale faces look rosy as they hurried by. Poulterers' and grocers' shops became a dazzling spectacle—a glorious holiday pageant that made it hard to believe dull everyday trade and commerce had anything to do with it. The Lord Mayor, inside the mighty fortress of the Mansion House, gave orders to his fifty cooks and butlers to celebrate Christmas in grand style. Even the little tailor, who had been fined five shillings that previous Monday for being drunk and rowdy in the street, was up in his attic stirring tomorrow's pudding, while his slender wife and baby stepped out into the freezing air to buy their Christmas beef.

Foggier yet, and colder still. Piercing, searching, biting cold. If Saint Dunstan had only pinched the Devil's nose with a blast of weather like that instead of using his trusty blacksmith's tongs, the fiend would have roared with a vengeance. A boy with a freezing, shivering nose—gnawed and bitten by the hungry cold like a bone chewed by dogs—bent down to Scrooge's keyhole to treat him to a Christmas carol. But at the very first words:

"God bless you, merry gentleman!
May nothing you dismay!"

Scrooge seized his heavy ruler with such sudden fury that the singer fled in terror, leaving the keyhole to the fog and the far more welcoming frost.

At last, the hour arrived to close up the office. With great reluctance, Scrooge climbed down from his stool, silently conceding the fact to his expectant clerk in the tank, who instantly snuffed out his candle and put on his hat.

"You'll want the entire day off tomorrow, I suppose?" said Scrooge.

"If it's quite convenient, sir."

"It's not convenient," said Scrooge, "and it's not fair. If I docked you half a crown for it, you'd think yourself mistreated, wouldn't you?"

The clerk smiled faintly.

"And yet," said Scrooge, "you don't think I'm mistreated when I pay you a full day's wages for no work."

The clerk observed that it was only once a year.

"A poor excuse for picking a man's pocket every twenty-fifth of December!" said Scrooge, buttoning his heavy overcoat right up to his chin. "Well, I suppose you must have the whole day. But be here all the earlier the next morning!"

The clerk promised that he would. Scrooge walked out with a growl. The office was closed in a heartbeat, and the clerk—with the long ends of his white scarf dangling past his waist, since he possessed no warm overcoat—ran down an icy slide on Cornhill behind a line of boys twenty times in honor of Christmas Eve. Then he ran home to Camden Town as fast as he could sprint, to play a lively game of blindman's buff.

Scrooge ate his melancholy dinner in his usual melancholy tavern. Having read all the newspapers and passed the rest of the evening checking over his bankbook, he headed home to bed.

He lived in a set of rooms that had once belonged to his deceased partner. They were a gloomy suite in a brooding, dark building tucked away in a courtyard. The house looked so out of place that one could scarcely help imagining it had run there when it was young, playing hide-and-seek with other buildings, and had forgotten the way back out. It was old now, and dreary enough, for nobody lived in it except Scrooge; all the other rooms were rented out as offices. The courtyard was so pitch-black that even Scrooge, who knew every single stone, had to feel his way along with his hands. The fog and frost hung so heavily around the blackened gateway of the house that it seemed as if the Spirit of the Weather sat in mournful meditation upon the threshold.

Now, it is a fact that there was nothing at all unusual about the knocker on the door, except that it was very large. It is also a fact that Scrooge had seen it morning and night every day he had lived there. Furthermore, Scrooge had as little imagination as any man in the City of London—which is a bold claim, even including the city council, aldermen, and livery companies. Let it also be borne in mind that Scrooge had not given a single thought to Marley since mentioning his seven-years-dead partner earlier that afternoon.

And then let any man explain to me, if he can, how it happened that as Scrooge put his key into the lock, he looked up at the knocker and saw—without any intermediate process of change—not a knocker, but Marley's face.

Marley's face.

It was not shrouded in deep shadow like the other objects in the courtyard, but gave off a faint, eerie light, like a decaying lobster in a dark cellar. It was not angry or ferocious. It looked at Scrooge exactly as Marley used to look: with ghostly spectacles pushed up onto its ghostly forehead. Its hair was curiously stirred, as if ruffled by warm air or hot breath. Though the eyes were wide open, they were completely motionless. That, combined with its pale, sickly color, made it horrible. Yet its horror seemed to be something inflicted upon the face rather than an expression it chose to make.

As Scrooge stared fixedly at this phenomenon, it was a knocker again.

To say that he was not startled, or that his blood did not run cold with a terrible shock he hadn't felt since childhood, would be untrue. But he took hold of the key he had dropped, turned it firmly, walked in, and lit his candle.

He did pause with a moment's hesitation before shutting the door. He looked cautiously behind it first, as if half-expecting to be terrified by the sight of Marley's pigtail sticking out into the hall. But there was nothing on the back of the door except the nuts and screws that held the knocker in place. So he muttered, "Nonsense, nonsense!" and slammed it shut with a bang.

The sound boomed through the house like thunder. Every room upstairs, and every wine cask down in the merchant's cellars below, seemed to echo with a crash of its own. But Scrooge was not a man to be frightened by echoes. He bolted the heavy door, crossed the hall, and climbed the stairs—slowly, trimming his candle as he went.

People talk casually about driving a four-horse coach up an old flight of stairs, or slipping a coach through a loosely written Act of Parliament. But I tell you, you could have driven a hearse up that staircase—broadside, with the crossbar toward the wall and the doors toward the banister—and done it with ease. There was plenty of width and room to spare. Perhaps that was why Scrooge thought he saw a ghostly hearse rolling up the gloomy stairway ahead of him. Half a dozen gas streetlamps wouldn't have lit the entryway properly, so you can imagine how dim it was with only Scrooge's cheap tallow candle.

Up Scrooge went, not caring a button for that. Darkness is cheap, and Scrooge liked it. But before closing his heavy inner door, he walked through his rooms to make sure everything was in order. He had just enough memory of the face to want to check.

Living room, bedroom, storage room. All as they should be. Nobody under the table, nobody under the sofa. A small fire burned in the grate. The spoon and bowl were ready, and a little saucepan of oatmeal gruel—Scrooge had a head cold—sat on the stove ledge. Nobody under the bed. Nobody in the closet. Nobody hiding in his dressing gown, which hung against the wall in a suspicious, lifelike drape. The storage room was entirely normal: an old fireplace fender, worn shoes, two fish baskets, a three-legged washstand, and a poker.

Quite satisfied, he closed his door and locked himself in—double-locked himself in, which was not his habit. Secure against surprises, he took off his necktie, put on his dressing gown, slippers, and nightcap, and sat down before the hearth to eat his gruel.

It was a very low fire indeed—practically nothing on such a bitter night. He had to sit right against it, hunched over the hearth, just to extract the slightest warmth from such a handful of fuel. The fireplace was an old one, built by a Dutch merchant long ago, bordered all around with quaint Dutch tiles illustrating scenes from Scripture. There were Cains and Abels, Pharaoh's daughters, Queens of Sheba, angels drifting down through the air on clouds like featherbeds, Abrahams, Belshazzars, and Apostles setting off to sea in butter-boat skiffs—hundreds of figures to catch his eye.

Yet that face of Marley, dead seven years, came like the ancient Prophet's rod and swallowed up the whole scene. If every smooth tile had been blank at first, with the power to shape some picture on its surface from the scattered fragments of his thoughts, a copy of old Marley's head would have appeared on every single one.

"Humbug!" said Scrooge, and paced across the room.

After several turns, he sat down again. As he leaned his head back against the chair, his gaze happened to rest upon a bell—an old, disused servant's bell hanging in the room that had once connected, for some long-forgotten purpose, to a chamber on the top floor of the building. To his immense astonishment and creeping dread, as he watched, the bell began to swing.

It swayed so softly at first that it scarcely made a sound. But soon it was ringing loudly, and suddenly every single bell in the entire house began to ring along with it!

This might have lasted half a minute, or maybe a full minute, but it felt like an hour. Then the bells ceased just as they had begun, all together.

Immediately following, a heavy clanking sound echoed from deep down below—as if someone were dragging a massive chain across the barrels in the wine merchant's cellar. Scrooge remembered hearing old stories about ghosts dragging heavy chains in haunted houses.

The cellar door flew open with a booming crash! The noise grew much louder on the floors below, then came clanking up the stairs, and headed straight toward his door.

"It's humbug still!" said Scrooge. "I won't believe it."

His color drained, however, when the figure passed straight through the heavy wooden door without pausing, and stepped directly into the room before his eyes. As it entered, the dying flame in the hearth leaped up, as if crying out, "I know him—it's Marley's Ghost!" and then dropped back down.

The same face—the very same. Marley wore his usual pigtail wig, familiar vest, tight trousers, and boots, with the tassels on his boots bristling just like his pigtail, his coat tails, and the hair on his head. The chain he dragged was clasped tightly around his waist. It was long, winding around him like a tail, and was made—for Scrooge observed it closely—of iron cash boxes, keys, padlocks, account ledgers, legal deeds, and heavy steel purses. His body was transparent, so that as Scrooge observed him and looked through his vest, he could clearly see the two buttons on the back of his coat.

Scrooge had often heard it said that Marley had no guts, but he had never believed it until now.

No, and he didn't truly believe it even now. Even though he looked the phantom through and through, and saw it standing right in front of him; even though he felt the chilling influence of its death-cold eyes, and marked the woven texture of the folded cloth tied around its head and under its jaw—a burial wrap he had not noticed before—he remained stubborn and fought against his senses.

"How now!" said Scrooge, sarcastic and cold as ever. "What do you want with me?"

"Much!"—it was Marley's voice, without a doubt.

"Who are you?"

"Ask me who I was."

"Who were you, then?" said Scrooge, raising his voice. "You're awfully particular for a ghost." He had been about to say "down to a shade," but thought better of it.

"In life, I was your partner, Jacob Marley."

"Can you... can you sit down?" asked Scrooge, looking doubtfully at him.

"I can."

"Do it, then."

Scrooge asked because he didn't know whether a ghost so transparent could actually use a chair, and feared that if it proved impossible, it might lead to an awkward explanation. But the ghost sat down on the opposite side of the fireplace as naturally as if he were quite used to it.

"You don't believe in me," observed the Ghost.

"I don't," said Scrooge.

"What evidence of my reality would you have beyond that of your senses?"

"I don't know," said Scrooge.

"Why do you doubt your senses?"

"Because," said Scrooge, "a little thing affects them. A slight stomach disorder makes them cheats. You may be an undigested bit of beef, a blot of mustard, a crumb of cheese, or a fragment of an underdone potato. There's more of gravy than of grave about you, whatever you are!"

Scrooge was not in the habit of cracking jokes, nor did he feel in any way funny at that moment. The truth was that he tried to sound clever just to distract himself and keep his terror down, for the phantom's voice chilled him to the very marrow of his bones.

To sit staring into those fixed, glazed eyes in silence was more than Scrooge could endure. There was something awful, too, about the eerie atmosphere that surrounded the phantom. Scrooge could not feel heat himself, but the Ghost sat enveloped in it; for though it remained motionless, its hair, coat tails, and boot tassels were constantly fluttering as if caught in the hot vapor of an oven.

"You see this toothpick?" said Scrooge, quickly pressing the point to divert the vision's stony gaze from himself, if only for a second.

"I do," replied the Ghost.

"You aren't looking at it," said Scrooge.

"Yet I see it notwithstanding," said the Ghost.

"Well!" returned Scrooge. "I have only to swallow this, and for the rest of my days I'll be tormented by a legion of goblins, all of my own creation. Humbug, I tell you! Humbug!"

At this, the spirit raised a frightful cry and shook its chain with such a dismal, horrifying racket that Scrooge clutched his chair with both hands to keep from fainting. But his terror grew tenfold when the phantom untied the cloth bandage around its head—as if it were too warm indoors—and its lower jaw dropped right down onto its chest!

Scrooge fell upon his knees and clasped his hands before his face.

"Mercy!" he cried. "Dreadful apparition, why do you trouble me?"

"Man of worldly greed!" replied the Ghost. "Do you believe in me or not?"

"I do!" said Scrooge. "I must! But why do spirits walk the earth, and why do they come to me?"

"It is required of every human being," the Ghost returned, "that the spirit within should walk among their fellow creatures, traveling far and wide. And if that spirit does not go forth in life, it is condemned to do so after death. It is doomed to wander through the world—oh, woe is me!—and witness the misery it can no longer share, but might have shared on earth and turned to happiness!"

Again the phantom let out a cry, shook its chain, and wrung its shadowy hands.

"You are chained," said Scrooge, trembling. "Tell me why!"

"I wear the chain I forged in life," replied the Ghost. "I made it link by link, and yard by yard. I girded it on of my own free will, and of my own free will I wore it. Is its pattern strange to you?"

Scrooge trembled more and more.

"Or would you know," pursued the Ghost, "the weight and length of the heavy coil you bear yourself? It was full as heavy and as long as this seven Christmas Eves ago. You have labored on it ever since. It is an enormous, crushing chain!"

Scrooge glanced down at the floor, half-expecting to find himself wrapped in fifty or sixty yards of heavy iron cable, but he could see nothing.

"Jacob!" he begged. "Old Jacob Marley, tell me more. Speak comfort to me, Jacob!"

"I have none to give," the Ghost replied. "Comfort comes from other regions, Ebenezer Scrooge, conveyed by other ministers to other kinds of men. Nor can I tell you all that I wish. Only a very little more is permitted to me. I cannot rest, I cannot stay, I cannot linger anywhere. In life, my spirit never walked beyond our countinghouse—mark me!—my soul never roved beyond the narrow limits of our money-changing den. And weary journeys stretch before me!"

Whenever Scrooge grew thoughtful, it was his habit to put his hands into his pockets. Pondering what the Ghost had said, he did so now, but without lifting his eyes or getting up from his knees.

"You must have been very slow about it, Jacob," Scrooge observed in a businesslike tone, though with humility and deference.

"Slow!" the Ghost repeated.

"Seven years dead," mused Scrooge, "and traveling all the time!"

"The whole time," said the Ghost. "No rest, no peace. Only the constant torture of remorse."

"Do you travel fast?" said Scrooge.

"On the wings of the wind," replied the Ghost.

"You must have covered a huge amount of ground in seven years," said Scrooge.

The Ghost, on hearing this, set up another terrible wail, and clanked its chain so hideously in the dead silence of the night that the city watch would have been fully justified in arresting it for disturbing the peace.

"Oh, captive, bound, and doubly chained!" cried the phantom. "To not know that ages of endless labor by immortal creatures could never exhaust all the good this earth can hold! To not know that any Christian spirit, working kindly in whatever small sphere it finds itself, will discover its mortal life too short for all the good it could do! To not know that no amount of regret can ever make amends for one life's wasted opportunities! Yet that was me! Oh, that was me!"

"But you were always a good man of business, Jacob," faltered Scrooge, who now began to apply this to himself.

"Business!" cried the Ghost, wringing its hands again. "Humanity was my business! The common welfare was my business! Charity, mercy, forbearance, and benevolence—those were all my true business! The dealings of my trade were but a single drop of water in the vast ocean of my business!"

It held up its chain at arm's length, as if it were the root of all its useless grief, and flung it heavily onto the floor again.

"At this time of the rolling year," the spirit groaned, "I suffer most. Why did I walk through crowds of my fellow human beings with my eyes cast down, and never raise them to that blessed Star which led the Wise Men to a humble stable? Were there no poor homes to which its light would have guided me?"

Scrooge was terrified to hear the ghost speaking with such desperate anguish, and began to tremble violently.

"Hear me!" cried the Ghost. "My time is nearly gone."

"I will," said Scrooge. "Only don't be hard on me! Don't be so dramatic, Jacob! I beg you!"

"How it is that I appear before you in a shape you can see, I cannot tell. I have sat invisible beside you for many and many a day."

It was not a comforting thought. Scrooge shivered, and wiped the cold sweat from his brow.

"That has been no small part of my penance," pursued the Ghost. "I am here tonight to warn you that you still have a hope and a chance of escaping my fate. A chance and a hope of my procuring, Ebenezer."

"You were always a good friend to me," said Scrooge. "Thank you."

"You will be haunted," resumed the Ghost, "by Three Spirits."

Scrooge's face fell almost as low as the ghost's jaw had done.

"Is that the chance and hope you mentioned, Jacob?" he asked in a faltering voice.

"It is."

"Then... I think I'd rather not," said Scrooge.

"Without their visits," warned the Ghost, "you cannot hope to escape the path I walk. Expect the first spirit tomorrow night, when the bell tolls One."

"Couldn't I take them all at once, and have it over with, Jacob?" hinted Scrooge.

"Expect the second spirit on the next night at the same hour. The third upon the following night, when the last chime of Twelve has ceased to vibrate. You will see me no more. And make sure that, for your own sake, you remember what has passed between us tonight!"

When it had said these words, the phantom took its cloth wrapper from the table and tied it securely around its head as before. Scrooge knew this by the sharp click its teeth made when its jaws were clamped back together. Gathering his courage, Scrooge looked up and found his supernatural visitor confronting him in an upright posture, with its heavy chain wound over and around its arm.

The apparition began to back away from him. At every step it took, the window raised itself a little, so that when the phantom reached the ledge, it was wide open.

It beckoned Scrooge to approach, and he did. When they were within two paces of each other, Marley's Ghost held up its hand, warning him to come no closer. Scrooge stopped.

He stopped not so much out of obedience as in shock and terror. As the ghost raised its hand, Scrooge became aware of confused cries filling the air outside—incoherent sounds of lamentation and regret, wailings inexpressibly sorrowful and self-accusing. The spirit listened for a moment, joined in the mournful chorus, and floated out into the bleak, dark night.

Scrooge rushed to the window, desperate in his curiosity. He looked out.

The sky was filled with phantoms, wandering restlessly back and forth in agonizing haste, moaning as they went. Every single one wore chains like Marley's Ghost. A few (perhaps guilty rulers or corrupt officials) were linked together, but none were free. Many had been personally known to Scrooge in life. He had been well acquainted with one elderly ghost in a white vest, with a monstrous iron safe chained to its ankle, who cried piteously at being unable to assist a wretched woman with a baby sitting on a doorstep below. The misery they all shared was painfully clear: they desperately wished to intervene for good in human lives, but had lost the power forever.

Whether these creatures faded into the mist or the mist swallowed them up, Scrooge could not tell. But both the phantoms and their ghostly voices melted away together, and the night became once more as it had been when he walked home.

Scrooge closed the window and examined the door through which the Ghost had entered. It was still double-locked, just as he had locked it with his own hands, and the bolts were completely undisturbed. He tried to say "Humbug!" but stopped after the very first syllable. Feeling utterly drained—whether from the terror he had undergone, the exhaustion of the day, his glimpse of the spirit world, the somber conversation with Marley, or simply the lateness of the hour—he desperately needed rest. Without even undressing, he climbed straight into bed and fell asleep the instant his head hit the pillow.
