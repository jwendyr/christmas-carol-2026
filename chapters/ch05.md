# Chapter 5: The End of It

Yes! The bedpost was his own! The bed was his own, the room was his own, and best and happiest of all, the time ahead was his own—time to make amends!

"I will live in the Past, the Present, and the Future!" Scrooge shouted, scrambling out of bed. "The spirits of all three will live within me! Oh, Jacob Marley! Heaven and Christmas time be praised for this! I say it on my knees, old Jacob—on my knees!"

He was trembling with excitement and glowing with good intentions, so choked up that his voice could barely answer his call. He had been sobbing violently during his struggle with the Spirit, and his face was still soaked with tears.

"They aren't torn down!" cried Scrooge, gathering one of his bed curtains into his arms. "They aren't torn down, rings and all! They're still here—I am still here—and the shadows of what might have been can be swept away! They will be! I know they will!"

His hands flew wildly over his clothes the whole time—turning them inside out, pulling them on upside down, tearing them, misplacing them, and subjecting them to every kind of joyful chaos.

"I don't know what to do!" cried Scrooge, laughing and crying in the same breath, completely tangled up in his stockings like a man wrestling sea serpents. "I'm as light as a feather! I'm as happy as an angel! I'm as merry as a schoolboy! I'm as dizzy as a drunken man! Merry Christmas to everybody! Happy New Year to the whole world! Woohoo! Hey! Hello!"

He bounded into the sitting room and stood there, completely out of breath.

"There's the saucepan the gruel was in!" cried Scrooge, taking off again and circling the fireplace. "There's the door the ghost of Jacob Marley walked through! There's the corner where the Ghost of Christmas Present sat! There's the window where I saw the wandering spirits! It's all right, it's all true, it really happened! Ha, ha, ha!"

Really, for a man who had been out of practice for so many years, it was a splendid laugh—a truly magnificent laugh. It was the father of a long, long line of brilliant laughs!

"I don't know what day of the month it is!" said Scrooge. "I don't know how long I've been with the spirits. I don't know anything! I'm practically a baby. Never mind—I don't care! I'd rather be a baby anyway! Hey! Woohoo! Hello there!"

He was stopped in his tracks by church bells ringing out the most joyful peals he had ever heard. Clash, clang, hammer! Ding, dong, bell! Bell, dong, ding! Hammer, clang, clash! Oh, glorious, glorious!

Running to the window, he threw it open and stuck his head out. No fog, no mist! It was clear, bright, cheerful, brisk, and cold—a cold that made the blood dance in your veins! Golden sunlight, a heavenly sky, sweet fresh air, and merry bells. Oh, glorious! Glorious!

"What day is today?" cried Scrooge, calling down to a boy in his Sunday clothes who had paused on the street to look around.

"Huh?" the boy replied, completely baffled.

"What's today, my fine fellow?" Scrooge asked.

"Today?" replied the boy. "Why, it's Christmas Day!"

"It's Christmas Day!" Scrooge said to himself. "I haven't missed it! The spirits did it all in one night! They can do whatever they want. Of course they can—of course they can! Hey, my fine fellow!"

"Hello!" the boy called back.

"Do you know the poultry shop two streets over, on the corner?" Scrooge inquired.

"I sure do!" replied the boy.

"Smart kid!" said Scrooge. "A remarkable boy! Do you know if they've sold the prize turkey hanging in the window? Not the little prize turkey—the big one?"

"What, the one as big as me?" returned the boy.

"What a delightful boy!" said Scrooge. "It's an absolute pleasure to talk to him. Yes, that's the one, my lad!"

"It's hanging there right now," replied the boy.

"Is it?" said Scrooge. "Go and buy it."

"No way! You're kidding!" exclaimed the boy.

"No, no," said Scrooge. "I'm completely serious! Go and buy it, and tell them to bring it right here so I can give them the delivery address. Come back with the delivery man, and I'll give you a shilling. Come back with him in less than five minutes, and I'll give you half a crown!"

The boy was off like a shot. A person would have needed a remarkably quick finger on the trigger to fire off a bullet half so fast.

"I'll send it to Bob Cratchit's!" whispered Scrooge, rubbing his hands together and splitting with laughter. "He won't have a clue who sent it! It's twice the size of Tiny Tim! No comedian in history ever pulled off a better joke than sending this to Bob will be!"

His hand wasn't steady as he wrote the address, but somehow he managed to write it. He hurried downstairs to open the front door, waiting eagerly for the delivery man to arrive. As he stood there waiting, the door knocker caught his eye.

"I'll love you as long as I live!" cried Scrooge, patting it with his hand. "I hardly ever looked at it before! What an honest expression it has on its face! It's a wonderful knocker! Ah, here's the turkey! Hey! Woohoo! How are you? Merry Christmas!"

And what a turkey it was! That bird could never have stood on its own legs—they would have snapped clean in two in a heartbeat, like brittle sticks of sealing wax.

"Why, it's impossible to carry that all the way to Camden Town," said Scrooge. "You'll have to take a cab."

The chuckle with which he said this, the chuckle with which he paid for the turkey, the chuckle with which he paid for the cab, and the chuckle with which he rewarded the boy were only outdone by the chuckle with which he sank back into his armchair, completely out of breath, and chuckled until tears ran down his face.

Shaving was no easy task, because his hand kept shaking terribly, and shaving demands attention even when you aren't dancing around while doing it. But even if he had sliced the tip of his nose right off, he would have stuck a bandage over it and been completely happy.

He dressed himself in his very best clothes and finally stepped out into the streets. Crowds were pouring outside by now, just as he had seen them with the Ghost of Christmas Present. Walking with his hands behind his back, Scrooge looked upon everyone with a delighted smile. In a word, he looked so irresistibly pleasant that three or four good-natured people called out, "Good morning, sir! A Merry Christmas to you!" And Scrooge often said afterward that of all the cheerful sounds he had ever heard, those were the sweetest to his ears.

He hadn't gone far when he saw the portly gentleman coming toward him—the very man who had walked into his office the day before and asked, "Scrooge and Marley's, I believe?" It sent a pang through his heart to think of how this kind gentleman would look at him when they met, but Scrooge knew which path lay straight ahead, and he took it.

"My dear sir," said Scrooge, quickening his pace and taking the gentleman warmly by both hands. "How do you do? I hope your work went well yesterday. It was so very kind of you. A Merry Christmas to you, sir!"

"Mr. Scrooge?"

"Yes," said Scrooge. "That is my name, and I'm afraid it might not sound very pleasant to you. Please allow me to ask your forgiveness. And would you have the goodness to—" Here, Scrooge leaned in and whispered in his ear.

"Lord bless me!" cried the gentleman, his breath completely taken away. "My dear Mr. Scrooge, are you serious?"

"If you please," said Scrooge. "Not a penny less. A great many back-payments are included in it, I assure you. Will you do me that favor?"

"My dear sir," said the gentleman, pumping his hand. "I don't know what to say to such munifi—"

"Don't say anything, please," Scrooge replied. "Just come and visit me. Will you come and see me?"

"I will!" cried the gentleman. And it was clear he truly meant it.

"Thank you," said Scrooge. "I am deeply grateful to you. I thank you fifty times over. God bless you!"

He went to church, walked through the streets, and watched the crowds bustling back and forth. He patted children on the head, spoke kindly with beggars, peered down into basement kitchens, looked up at glowing windows, and found that absolutely everything brought him pleasure. He had never dreamed that any walk—that anything in life—could give him so much happiness. In the afternoon, he made his way toward his nephew's house.

He paced past the front door a dozen times before finding the courage to go up and knock. But he took a breath, stepped forward, and did it.

"Is your master at home, my dear?" Scrooge asked the young maid who opened the door. A lovely girl! Truly lovely.

"Yes, sir."

"Where is he, my dear?" asked Scrooge.

"He's in the dining room with the mistress, sir. I'll show you upstairs, if you please."

"Thank you, but he knows me well," Scrooge said, his hand already resting on the dining room latch. "I'll go right in, my dear."

He turned the handle gently and peeked his face around the door. Fred and his wife were inspecting the dining table, which was lavishly spread out; young hosts are always anxious about such things and want to make sure every little detail is perfect.

"Fred!" said Scrooge.

Bless my heart, how Fred's wife jumped! For a moment Scrooge had forgotten she was sitting right there in the corner with her feet on the footstool, or he wouldn't have startled her for the world.

"Well, bless my soul!" cried Fred. "Who is that?"

"It's me. Your Uncle Scrooge. I've come for dinner. Will you let me in, Fred?"

Let him in! It was a mercy Fred didn't shake his uncle's arm clean off. Scrooge was completely at home within five minutes. Nothing could have been warmer or more welcoming. His niece looked just as charming as she had in the vision, and so did Topper when he arrived, and the plump sister when she arrived, and everyone else as they came in. It was a wonderful party, with wonderful games, wonderful harmony, and won-der-ful happiness!

The next morning, Scrooge arrived at the office early. Oh, he was early! If only he could get there first and catch Bob Cratchit coming in late—that was the one thing he had set his heart upon.

And he did it—yes, he did! The clock struck nine. No Bob. Quarter past nine. No Bob. He was a full eighteen and a half minutes late! Scrooge sat with his office door wide open so he could watch Bob sneak into his chilly little tank.

Bob had his hat off before he even opened the door, and his scarf was off too. He sprang onto his stool in a heartbeat, his pen flying across the paper as if he could somehow catch up to nine o'clock.

"Hey!" growled Scrooge in his old familiar voice, faking it as best he could. "What do you mean by coming in at this time of day?"

"I am very sorry, sir," said Bob. "I know I am late."

"You are?" repeated Scrooge. "Yes, I believe you are! Step this way, sir, if you please."

"It's only once a year, sir," pleaded Bob, stepping nervously out of his tank. "It won't happen again. We were celebrating a bit yesterday, sir."

"Now, let me tell you something, my friend," said Scrooge. "I am not going to stand for this sort of thing any longer! And therefore," he continued, leaping from his stool and giving Bob such a friendly jab in the ribs that Bob stumbled backward into the tank again, "and therefore, I am going to raise your salary!"

Bob trembled, his hand edging closer to the heavy wooden ruler. For a split second, he had half a mind to knock Scrooge flat with it, pin him to the floor, and shout out the window to the courtyard for help and a straitjacket!

"A Merry Christmas, Bob!" said Scrooge with an earnestness that could not be mistaken, clapping him warmly on the back. "A merrier Christmas, Bob, my good fellow, than any I have given you in many a year! I will raise your salary, and do everything I can to help your struggling family, and we will discuss your affairs this very afternoon over a warm bowl of spiced holiday punch, Bob! Build up the fires, and go buy another coal bucket before you dot another 'i', Bob Cratchit!"

Scrooge was better than his word. He did everything he promised, and infinitely more. And to Tiny Tim—who did *not* die—he became a second father. He became as good a friend, as good an employer, and as good a man as the grand old city had ever known, or any other city, town, or village in the good old world.

Some people laughed to see the dramatic change in him, but he let them laugh and paid them little mind. He was wise enough to know that nothing truly good ever happened on this earth without some people laughing at it in the beginning. Knowing that folks like that would be blind to the light anyway, he figured it was just as well for them to wrinkle up their eyes in grins rather than scowl in uglier ways. His own heart laughed, and that was more than enough for him.

He had no further encounters with spirits, but lived on the principle of total abstinence from ghosts ever after. And it was always said of Ebenezer Scrooge that he knew how to celebrate Christmas well, if any person alive possessed that knowledge.

May that be truly said of us, and of all of us!

And so, as Tiny Tim said:

"God bless us, every one!"
