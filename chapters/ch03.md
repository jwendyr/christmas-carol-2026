# Chapter 3: The Second of the Three Spirits

Waking right in the middle of a prodigiously loud snore and sitting up in bed to gather his thoughts, Scrooge didn't need anyone to tell him that the clock was about to strike one. He sensed he had regained consciousness in the nick of time, specifically to meet the second messenger dispatched through Jacob Marley's intervention. But when he started wondering which bed curtain this new phantom would pull back, an uncomfortable chill ran down his spine. So he pushed every single curtain aside with his own hands, lay back down, and set up a sharp lookout all around the bed. He wanted to confront the Spirit the very instant it appeared, refusing to be caught off guard and rattled.

Carefree, swaggering gentlemen who pride themselves on being street-smart and up for anything often express their appetite for adventure by claiming they're game for anything from a coin toss to manslaughter—extremes between which, no doubt, lies a comfortably wide territory. Without making quite such a bold claim for Scrooge, I don't hesitate to ask you to believe that he was ready for a broad variety of strange spectacles, and that nothing from a newborn baby to a rhinoceros would have surprised him all that much.

Yet, while he was prepared for almost anything, he was not at all prepared for nothing. Consequently, when the bell struck one and no ghostly shape appeared, he began to tremble violently. Five minutes, ten minutes, a quarter of an hour slipped by, and still nothing showed up. All this time he lay in bed, right at the glowing heart of a blaze of reddish light that had flooded over him the moment the clock chimed the hour. Being merely light, it was somehow more alarming than a dozen ghosts, since he couldn't figure out what it meant or what it was doing. At moments, he even worried he might be an intriguing case of spontaneous human combustion, without even having the comfort of knowing it. At last, however, he started thinking—just as you or I would have thought right from the start, for the person sitting comfortably outside a crisis always knows exactly what should have been done and would certainly have done it—at last, as I said, he began to suspect that the source of this eerie glow lay in the adjoining room, which was where the light seemed to be streaming from. Once this thought took hold, he got up quietly and shuffled in his slippers to the door.

The moment Scrooge's hand touched the handle, an unfamiliar voice called his name and told him to enter. He obeyed.

It was his own room—there was no doubt about that. But it had undergone an astonishing transformation. The walls and ceiling were draped in living greenery, turning it into a lush woodland grove, studded everywhere with bright, gleaming berries. Crisp leaves of holly, mistletoe, and ivy reflected the light like hundreds of tiny mirrors. A roaring fire blazed in the chimney, the likes of which that cold, stony hearth had never seen in Scrooge's time, or Marley's, or across countless winters gone by. Piled high on the floor like a magnificent throne was an incredible feast: turkeys, geese, wild game, poultry, cured meats, massive roasts, suckling pigs, long garlands of sausages, mince pies, plum puddings, barrels of oysters, piping-hot chestnuts, rosy-cheeked apples, juicy oranges, luscious pears, towering holiday cakes, and steaming bowls of punch that misted the room with a delicious aroma. Lounging comfortably on this feast was a jolly Giant, glorious to behold. He held a glowing torch shaped like a horn of plenty, raising it high to shine down on Scrooge as the old man peeked around the door.

"Come in!" exclaimed the Ghost. "Come in, and get to know me better, man!"

Scrooge stepped inside timidly and bowed his head before the Spirit. He was no longer the stubborn, obstinate Scrooge he used to be, and though the Spirit’s eyes were clear and kind, he hesitated to look directly into them.

"I am the Ghost of Christmas Present," said the Spirit. "Look at me!"

Scrooge looked up with deep respect. The Spirit wore a simple, deep-green robe bordered with white fur. The garment hung so loosely around him that his broad chest was bare, as if refusing to hide behind any armor or disguise. His bare feet peeked out from beneath the robe's generous folds, and upon his head rested only a simple wreath of holly, sparkling here and there with gleaming icicles. His long, dark brown curls flowed freely—as free and open as his genial face, his sparkling eyes, his welcoming hands, his cheery voice, and his joyful, uninhibited manner. Buckled around his waist was an antique scabbard, but there was no sword inside, and the ancient sheath was rusted through.

"You've never seen anyone like me before!" the Spirit exclaimed.

"Never," Scrooge answered.

"And you have never walked with the younger members of my family—by which I mean, since I am very young, my elder brothers born in recent years?" the phantom pressed.

"I don't think I have," said Scrooge. "I'm afraid not. Have you had many brothers, Spirit?"

"More than eighteen hundred," said the Ghost.

"What a tremendous family to provide for!" muttered Scrooge.

The Ghost of Christmas Present stood up.

"Spirit," Scrooge said submissively, "take me wherever you wish. Last night I was taken against my will, but I learned a lesson that is already changing me. Tonight, if you have anything to teach me, let me learn from it."

"Touch my robe!"

Scrooge did as he was told, gripping it tightly.

Instantly, the holly, mistletoe, red berries, ivy, turkeys, geese, game, roasts, pigs, sausages, oysters, pies, puddings, fruit, and punch all vanished. Gone too were the room, the fire, the warm glow, and the midnight hour. Instead, they stood right in the city streets on Christmas morning. The weather was biting cold, and the townsfolk created a rough, brisk, cheerful sort of music by shoveling snow off the sidewalks in front of their doorsteps and scraping it from their rooftops. Neighborhood boys were thrilled to watch great clumps plunge onto the street below, bursting into miniature artificial blizzards.

The building facades looked dark, and the windows even darker, standing out against the smooth blankets of white snow on the roofs and the slushy, grimy snow down on the ground. Below, heavy cart and wagon wheels had plowed the snow into deep ruts that crisscrossed hundreds of times at major intersections, turning into tangled channels of thick yellowish mud and freezing water. The sky hung gray and gloomy, and the narrower side streets were choked with a murky mist—half-thawed, half-frozen—whose heavier particles drifted down like soot, as though every chimney across Britain had agreed to catch fire at once and blaze to its heart's content. There was certainly nothing cheerful about the weather or the town, yet an undeniable spirit of joy filled the air, the kind that even the brightest summer sun and clearest blue skies could never quite match.

The folks shoveling snow from the rooftops were hearty and full of cheer, shouting out to one another across the gutters and playfully hurling snowballs—far friendlier missiles than many a biting joke—laughing just as hard whether their aim was true or wildly off. The poultry shops were still half-open, and the produce markets were radiant in their festive glory. Big, round, pot-bellied baskets of chestnuts, shaped like the vests of jolly old gentlemen, sprawled by the doorways and spilled out onto the sidewalk in bursting abundance. Plump, brown, wide-girthed Spanish onions shone with rich ripeness like cheerful monks, winking cheekily from the shelves at passing girls who glanced shyly at the sprigs of mistletoe hanging overhead. Pears and apples were stacked high in mouthwatering pyramids. Bunches of grapes hung dangling from prominent hooks through the grocers' good graces, making passersby drool free of charge. Piles of brown, earthy hazelnuts gave off a scent that stirred memories of forest walks and kicking ankle-deep through fallen autumn leaves. Squat, dark-red baked apples stood out against the bright yellow of oranges and lemons, begging so earnestly in their plump juiciness to be carried home in paper sacks for dessert. Even the goldfish and silver fish swimming in bowls among the fruit displays, cold-blooded and sluggish by nature, seemed to sense the excitement, swimming round and round their little glass bowls in slow, calm fascination.

And the grocery shops! Oh, the grocery shops! They were almost closed, with only a shutter or two left open, but what wonderful sights peeked through the gaps! It wasn't just the merry clinking of brass scales hitting the counter, or the rapid snap of twine unrolling from its spool, or the spice tins rattling in rhythmic tricks like a juggler's props. It wasn't even the rich aroma of tea and coffee blending in the air, or the abundant raisins, the snow-white almonds, the long, straight cinnamon sticks, the fragrant spices, or the candied fruits so glazed and crusted with sugar that they made passersby dizzy with craving. Nor was it simply that the figs were plump and juicy, or that the French plums blushed from their decorative boxes, or that every single treat looked dressed for the holiday. It was the shoppers themselves—everyone was in such a delightful hurry, buzzing with anticipation, bumping into one another at the entrance with a clatter of wicker baskets, leaving their bags on the counters and dashing back to grab them, making a hundred harmless blunders in the best spirits imaginable. Meanwhile, the grocer and his staff were so warm and genuine that the shiny metal clasps fastening their aprons behind their backs might as well have been their own open hearts, displayed for the whole world to see without a single care.

Soon, church bells began calling everyone to worship, and families came flocking through the streets dressed in their Sunday best, their faces beaming. At the very same moment, out from countless side alleys, narrow lanes, and unnamed courts came crowds of working-class folks carrying their prepared holiday meals to the local bakeries to be roasted in the big ovens. This sight seemed to fascinate the Spirit. Standing with Scrooge beside a baker's doorway, he lifted the covers as people carried their pots and pans past, sprinkling incense over their dinners from his torch. It was no ordinary torch either: once or twice, when people bumped into each other and began to exchange sharp words, the Spirit flicked a few drops of moisture over them from the torch, and their smiles returned instantly. They reminded themselves that it was a crying shame to quarrel on Christmas Day. And indeed it was! God bless them, it truly was!

Eventually the church bells fell silent and the bakeries closed their doors. Yet a delicious preview of all those roasting feasts lingered on in the thawed, steamy patch of sidewalk directly above each baker’s basement oven, where the street pavers smoked as if the cobblestones themselves were being baked.

"Is there a special flavor in what you sprinkle from your torch?" Scrooge asked.

"There is. My own."

"Does it work on any meal today?" Scrooge asked.

"On any meal offered with kindness. Most of all, on a poor family's dinner."

"Why most of all on a poor one?" asked Scrooge.

"Because it needs it most."

"Spirit," Scrooge said after thinking for a moment, "it surprises me that someone like you, of all beings, would want to restrict these people's simple pleasures."

"Me?" cried the Spirit.

"You'd take away their only chance to cook a hot meal every seventh day—often the only day they can afford to eat well at all," said Scrooge. "Wouldn't you?"

"Me?" cried the Spirit.

"You want to shut these bakeries on the Sabbath," Scrooge said. "And that amounts to the same thing."

"I want that?" exclaimed the Spirit.

"Forgive me if I'm mistaken, but it's done in your name, or at least in the name of your family," Scrooge replied.

"There are people on this earth of yours," the Spirit answered, "who claim to speak for us, yet carry out acts of cruelty, pride, malice, hatred, envy, bigotry, and selfishness in our name! They are as foreign to us and our kin as if they had never existed. Remember that, and blame their deeds on them, not on us."

Scrooge promised he would keep that in mind, and the two walked on, invisible as before, out toward the city outskirts. A remarkable thing about the Ghost—which Scrooge had already noticed at the bakery—was that despite his massive size, he could easily fit into any space. He stood beneath a low ceiling with the same natural grace and majestic presence he would have shown in a towering palace hall.

Perhaps the good Spirit took joy in demonstrating this power, or perhaps it was simply his generous, loving nature and deep empathy for the poor that guided him straight to the home of Scrooge’s clerk. For there he went, carrying Scrooge along as the old man held tightly to his robe. Pausing at the doorstep, the Spirit smiled and blessed Bob Cratchit’s modest home with a sprinkle from his torch. Just imagine: Bob earned only fifteen shillings a week—taking home barely fifteen coins sharing his own nickname every Saturday—yet the Ghost of Christmas Present blessed his humble four-room house!

Inside, Mrs. Cratchit, Bob's wife, was already on her feet. She wore a modest dress that had been turned inside out and resewn twice to hide the wear, but she was adorned bravely with bright ribbons—which cost only pennies yet make a cheerful display. She spread the tablecloth with help from Belinda, her second daughter, who was likewise decked out in proud ribbons. Young Peter Cratchit poked a fork into the pot of boiling potatoes, accidentally getting the stiff corners of his enormous shirt collar into his mouth. The oversized collar was Bob's own property, loaned to his son and heir in honor of the holiday, and Peter felt so dashingly dressed that he dreamed of showing off his fine shirt in the city's fashionable parks. Just then, the two youngest Cratchits, a boy and a girl, came bursting in, shrieking with excitement that they had caught the scent of the roast goose outside the bakery and knew instantly it was theirs! Lost in glorious daydreams of sage and onion stuffing, the little Cratchits danced around the table, praising Peter to the skies. Peter—humble despite the collar that nearly strangled him—stoked the fire until the boiling potatoes began to rattle the pot lid, banging to be taken out and peeled.

"Whatever is keeping your dear father?" Mrs. Cratchit said. "And your brother Tiny Tim! And Martha wasn't this late last Christmas by half an hour!"

"Here I am, Mother!" said a young woman, walking through the door.

"Here's Martha, Mother!" yelled the two little Cratchits. "Hurrah! Martha, you should see the goose!"

"Bless your heart, my darling, you're so late!" cried Mrs. Cratchit, showering her with hugs and kisses and bustling about to help her off with her shawl and bonnet.

"We had so much work to finish up late last night," Martha explained, "and we had to clean up the shop this morning, Mother!"

"Well, never mind now that you're here!" said Mrs. Cratchit. "Sit right down by the fire, dear, and get warm, bless your heart!"

"No, no! Father's coming!" cried the two little Cratchits, darting everywhere at once. "Hide, Martha, hide!"

So Martha ducked out of sight just as little Bob, the father, walked in. A long woolen scarf hung down past his knees, its fringe dangling toward the floor. His worn, threadbare coat was brushed and patched to look as neat as possible for the holiday, and Tiny Tim was perched upon his shoulder. Poor Tiny Tim carried a small crutch, and his fragile legs were supported by an iron frame.

"Why, where's our Martha?" Bob called out, looking around the room.

"She's not coming," said Mrs. Cratchit.

"Not coming?" repeated Bob, his high spirits instantly crashing. He had played racehorse carrying Tim on his shoulders all the way from church, galloping home full of cheer. "Not coming on Christmas Day!"

Martha couldn't bear to see him heartbroken, even for a moment's prank. She popped out from behind the pantry door and ran straight into his open arms. Meanwhile, the two little Cratchits whisked Tiny Tim away to the washhouse so he could listen to the Christmas pudding bubbling and singing in the boiler.

"And how did little Tim behave?" asked Mrs. Cratchit, once she had teased Bob for falling for the joke and Bob had embraced his daughter to his heart’s content.

"As good as gold," Bob answered, "and even better. Spending so much quiet time sitting by himself makes him thoughtful, and he comes up with the sweetest ideas you've ever heard. On the walk home, he told me he hoped the congregation saw him at church, because being disabled, it might remind people on Christmas Day of the One who made lame beggars walk and gave sight to the blind."

Bob’s voice wavered as he shared this, and it trembled all the more when he insisted that Tiny Tim was growing stronger and healthier every day.

The tap-tap of a little crutch echoed across the floorboards, and back came Tiny Tim before another word was spoken. His brother and sister guided him proudly to his stool by the warm hearth. Bob rolled up his cuffs—as though the poor fellow's frayed sleeves could look any more worn than they already were—and mixed up a hot spiced drink in a pitcher with gin and lemons, stirring it well before setting it on the stove ledge to simmer. Meanwhile, Peter and the two energetic little Cratchits hurried off to the bakery to fetch the goose, returning shortly in a grand, triumphant parade.

Such excitement broke out that you'd think a roast goose was the rarest bird on earth—a miraculous creature compared to which even a black swan seemed ordinary. And truthfully, in that house, it was pretty close to a miracle! Mrs. Cratchit got the gravy hissing hot in a saucepan; Peter mashed the potatoes with extraordinary muscle; Belinda stirred extra sweetness into the applesauce; Martha warmed and dusted the serving plates; Bob pulled Tiny Tim close beside him at a cozy corner of the table; and the two younger Cratchits set chairs for everyone, making sure not to leave themselves out. Taking their stations like guards, the two youngsters shoved spoons into their mouths so they wouldn't scream for goose before being served. Finally, the platters were set down, and they said grace. A breathless hush followed. Mrs. Cratchit slowly ran her eyes along the edge of the carving knife, poised to plunge it into the bird's breast. When she sliced in, and a savory wave of fragrant stuffing burst forth, a collective cheer of delight swept around the table. Tiny Tim, egged on by the younger two, banged the table with the wooden handle of his knife and cheered a frail, joyful, "Hurrah!"

There was never a goose like it. Bob declared that he didn't believe a finer bird had ever been roasted. Its tenderness, flavor, size, and bargain price were admired by everyone at the table. Stretched out with plenty of applesauce and mashed potatoes, it made a complete and satisfying feast for the whole family. In fact, as Mrs. Cratchit pointed out with immense pride while eyeing a single tiny scrap of bone left on the platter, they hadn't even eaten every last bit! Everyone was completely full, and the two youngest Cratchits were stuffed with sage and onion right up to their eyebrows. With Belinda clearing the dinner plates, Mrs. Cratchit slipped out of the room by herself—far too nervous to let anyone watch—to unmold the Christmas pudding and carry it in.

What if it wasn't cooked through? What if it cracked when she turned it out of the bowl? What if an intruder had climbed the backyard wall and snatched it while they were busy eating the goose?—a horrifying thought that made the two youngest Cratchits turn pale with terror! Every imaginable disaster was feared.

Look out! Clouds of steam poured from the doorway! The pudding was out of the boiler! A scent like laundry day wafted in—that was the boiling cloth. Then came an aroma like a diner and a pastry shop side by side, with a laundromat right next door—that was the pudding itself! Within thirty seconds, Mrs. Cratchit reentered the room. Her cheeks were flushed, but she was smiling proudly, carrying the pudding like a speckled cannonball—dense and firm, blazing with glowing blue flames from a splash of lit brandy, and crowned with a cheerful sprig of Christmas holly right on top.

Oh, what a wonderful pudding! Bob Cratchit announced, with absolute sincerity, that he considered it the greatest achievement of Mrs. Cratchit's entire married life. Mrs. Cratchit admitted that now that the pressure was off, she'd had secret worries about whether she had used enough flour. Everyone heaped praise upon it, and nobody said—or even thought—that it was a small pudding for such a big family. To suggest such a thing would have been outright treason. Any Cratchit would have died of embarrassment before hinting at it.

At last the feast was finished. The tablecloth was cleared away, the hearth swept clean, and the fire stoked up. Bob sampled the warm spiced punch in the pitcher, pronounced it absolute perfection, and set out apples and oranges on the table while shoveling fresh chestnuts onto the coals. Then the entire Cratchit family gathered around the hearth in what Bob called a circle—though it was really a semicircle. Right beside Bob's elbow sat the family's finest glassware: two everyday tumblers and a little custard cup missing its handle.

Yet these simple cups held the steaming drink just as sweetly as golden goblets could have done. Bob poured with a radiant smile while the chestnuts hissed and popped over the fire. Raising his glass, Bob toasted:

"A Merry Christmas to us all, my dears! God bless us!"

The whole family echoed his toast with joy.

"God bless us, every one!" said Tiny Tim, the last of all.

He sat right beside his father on his little stool. Bob cradled the boy's frail, thin hand in his own, as if he loved him with all his soul, yearning to keep him close and terrified that he might soon be taken away.

"Spirit," Scrooge said, feeling a compassion he had never known before, "tell me if Tiny Tim will live."

"I see an empty chair," the Ghost answered, "in that humble chimney corner, and a crutch kept with loving care, but with no owner to hold it. If these shadows remain unchanged by the Future, the child will die."

"No, no!" cried Scrooge. "Oh no, kind Spirit! Tell me he'll be spared!"

"If these shadows remain unchanged by the Future, none of my kind will ever find him here again," the Ghost replied. "What of it? If he is likely to die, he had better do it, and decrease the surplus population!"

Scrooge hung his head, devastated to hear his own cruel words thrown back at him, overcome with remorse and grief.

"Man," the Ghost rebuked him, "if you truly have a heart of flesh and not cold stone, stop repeating that wicked nonsense until you learn what this 'surplus' really is and where to find it! Who are you to decide who should live and who should die? In the eyes of Heaven, you may be far less worthy of life than millions just like this poor man's child! Good heavens! To hear an insect perched on a leaf lecturing on the 'excess population' of its starving brothers down in the dirt!"

Scrooge bowed beneath the Ghost's scolding, his eyes fixed trembling on the floor. But he quickly looked up when he heard his own name.

"To Mr. Scrooge!" Bob announced, raising his glass. "I give you Mr. Scrooge, the Founder of our Feast!"

"The Founder of the Feast, indeed!" Mrs. Cratchit scoffed, her face turning red. "I only wish I had him here right now! I'd give him a piece of my mind to feast on, and I certainly hope he'd have the stomach for it!"

"My dear," pleaded Bob gently, "remember the children! It's Christmas Day."

"It would take Christmas Day to make anyone drink to the health of an odious, stingy, hard-hearted, unfeeling man like Mr. Scrooge!" she retorted. "You know very well he is, Robert! Nobody knows it better than you, my poor husband!"

"My love," Bob said softly, "it's Christmas Day."

"I'll drink to his health for your sake and for the holiday," said Mrs. Cratchit, "but certainly not for his! Long life to him! A merry Christmas and a happy New Year! I'm sure he'll be positively thrilled and overjoyed!"

The children drank the toast after her, but for the first time all day, there was no joy in it. Tiny Tim drank last of all, though he couldn't have cared less. Scrooge was the bogeyman of the family. The mere sound of his name cast a dark chill over the gathering that didn't lift for five full minutes.

Once the shadow passed, however, they were ten times happier than before, purely out of relief that they were done talking about Scrooge the Terrible. Bob told the family about a job lead he had found for Peter, which would bring in a handsome five shillings and sixpence a week if he landed it. The two young Cratchits burst into giggles at the thought of Peter becoming a businessman, while Peter stared thoughtfully into the fire from between his oversized collars, looking for all the world like a mogul deciding which big investments to make once that staggering fortune rolled in. Martha, who worked as an apprentice at a hat shop, described the endless hours of sewing she had to do and how she planned to sleep in late tomorrow morning for a well-deserved rest, since tomorrow was a day off she could spend at home. She also shared how she had spotted a countess and a lord a few days earlier, and that the lord was "about the same height as Peter"—at which Peter yanked his collars so high that you wouldn't have been able to see his head at all. All this time, the warm punch and roasted chestnuts made their rounds. Soon, Tiny Tim sang a sweet, touching song about a child lost in the snow; his little voice was clear and plaintive, and he sang it beautifully.

There was nothing fancy or grand about any of this. They weren't an exceptionally handsome family, and they were certainly poorly dressed. Their shoes leaked in the rain, their clothing was threadbare, and Peter almost certainly knew the way to the pawnshop. But they were joyful, deeply thankful, devoted to one another, and content with what little they had. As their home began to fade—looking all the brighter under a parting shower of sparkles from the Spirit’s torch—Scrooge kept his eyes glued to them, watching Tiny Tim until they disappeared completely.

By now dusk had settled, and the snow was falling heavily. As Scrooge and the Spirit moved through the streets, the glow of roaring fires in kitchens, living rooms, and parlors shone brilliantly. Through one window, flickering flames revealed preparations for an intimate dinner, with plates warming before the hearth and deep red curtains ready to be pulled against the freezing dark. In another yard, children came running out into the snow to greet married siblings, cousins, aunts, and uncles, competing to be the first to welcome them with open arms. Across the street, silhouettes of arriving guests danced against frosted window blinds. Down the path, a lively group of young women in warm hoods and fur-lined boots hurried along, talking and laughing all at once on their way to a neighbor’s party—and heaven help any bachelor who saw them arrive, glowing and charming, well aware of the spell they cast!

If you had judged by the sheer number of folks hurrying off to parties, you might have worried that no one was left at home to greet them—when in fact every single house was eagerly awaiting guests and piling its fireplace high with logs. Bless its heart, how the Spirit rejoiced! He opened his arms and spread his wide hands, gliding forward and showering bright, pure joy over everything around him. Even the lamplighter, running ahead with his ladder to dot the evening twilight with sparks of streetlamp light—dressed up for a holiday party of his own—burst out laughing as the Spirit drifted by, with no idea he was brushing shoulders with Christmas itself!

Suddenly, without a single word of warning, they were standing on a bleak, desolate moor. Enormous boulders lay scattered across the wasteland as though it were a graveyard for ancient giants. Water pooled everywhere it could, frozen solid by the bitter frost, and nothing grew except patches of moss, gorse, and stiff wild grass. To the west, the setting sun left a streak of fiery crimson that glared over the wasteland like a brooding eye before sinking lower and lower, finally vanishing into the pitch-black night.

"Where are we?" asked Scrooge.

"A place where miners live, men who labor deep in the bowels of the earth," the Spirit replied. "Yet they know me. Look!"

A light flickered from the window of a small hut, and they moved swiftly toward it. Passing effortlessly through the stone and mud wall, they found a cheerful family gathered around a crackling hearth. An elderly great-grandfather and great-grandmother sat surrounded by their children, grandchildren, and great-grandchildren, all dressed up in colorful holiday clothes. The old man, his voice barely louder than the howling wind outside, was singing an ancient Christmas carol—one that had been old even when he was a boy—and every few verses the whole family joined in the chorus. Whenever their voices swelled, the old man grew lively and strong; whenever they paused, his energy faded back into frail old age.

The Spirit did not linger long. Telling Scrooge to grip his robe, he swept up into the air above the moor and sped away—where? Surely not out to sea? Yes, out to sea! To Scrooge's sheer terror, when he looked back, the last sliver of land—a jagged reef of dangerous cliffs—was receding behind them. His ears roared with the thunderous crash of the ocean, raging against sea-carved caverns as if trying to tear the earth down into the deep.

Perched on a treacherous reef a few miles off the coast, where waves battered and smashed against the stone all year long, stood a lonely lighthouse. Massive mats of seaweed clung to its foundation, and sea birds—seeming born of the wind just as the kelp was born of the sea—wheeled and dove around the tower like the surging waves below.

Yet even out here, the two lightkeepers had built a cozy fire that beamed through a slit in the thick stone wall, casting a ray of warmth out across the churning ocean. Grasping their calloused hands across a weathered table, they clinked their mugs of rum and wished each other a Merry Christmas. Then the older keeper—his face weathered and scarred by sea storms like the carved figurehead of a wooden ship—struck up a booming sea shanty that sounded like a gale all on its own.

Onward the Ghost flew across the pitch-black, pitching sea—on and on—until, far from any coast, they touched down on a ship. They stood beside the helmsman at the steering wheel, the lookout stationed at the bow, and the watch officers on duty. Dark, shadowy figures at their posts, yet each and every man hummed a Christmas carol, harbored a warm holiday thought, or whispered memories of Christmases past to his crewmate, dreaming of returning home. Every soul aboard, whether awake or sleeping, good or flawed, spoke with greater kindness that day than any other day of the year. Each shared in the holiday cheer, thought lovingly of family far away across the sea, and knew that their loved ones were thinking of them in turn.

It was an incredible shock to Scrooge, while listening to the wailing wind and pondering how chilling it was to drift through the black void above an endless ocean as deep and mysterious as death itself, suddenly to hear a booming burst of laughter! It was an even greater shock to recognize that laughter as belonging to his own nephew Fred—and to find himself standing in a bright, dry, glowing living room, with the Spirit smiling beside him and watching Fred with delighted approval!

"Ha, ha!" Fred roared with laughter. "Ha, ha, ha!"

If you happen, by some slim chance, to know anyone blessed with a better laugh than Scrooge's nephew, all I can say is that I’d love to meet him. Introduce us, and I’ll gladly become his best friend.

It is one of life’s most wonderful, fair-minded truths that while sickness and sadness can be contagious, absolutely nothing on earth spreads faster or more irresistibly than genuine laughter and good cheer. When Scrooge’s nephew laughed like this—holding his sides, shaking his head, his face contorting into hilarious expressions—his wife laughed just as hard. And their circle of friends, refusing to be left behind, joined right in, roaring with delight:

"Ha, ha! Ha, ha, ha, ha!"

"As I live and breathe, he said Christmas was a humbug!" Fred cried. "And what's more, he actually meant it!"

"Shame on him, Fred!" his wife said indignantly. Bless women like her; they never do anything halfway. They mean every word they say.

She was very pretty—extraordinarily pretty. She had a dimpled, wide-eyed, delightful face, a sweet mouth clearly made for kissing (and undoubtedly kissed often), charming little beauty marks on her chin that crinkled together when she laughed, and the sparkliest, sunniest eyes you could ever hope to see. Altogether, she was playful, teasing, and utterly enchanting. Truly, completely captivating.

"He's a funny old guy, that's the honest truth," Fred said, "and certainly not as pleasant as he could be. Still, his bad habits are their own punishment, so I have no real grudge against him."

"I know he's very wealthy, Fred," his wife pointed out. "At least, that's what you always tell me."

"And what good does that do him, my dear?" Fred replied. "His money is totally useless to him. He doesn't do any good with it, he doesn't use it to make his own life comfortable, and he doesn't even have the joy of thinking—ha, ha, ha!—that he’s ever going to leave any of it to us!"

"I have zero patience for him," Fred's wife remarked, and her sisters and the other ladies in the room completely agreed.

"Oh, I do!" Fred said. "I feel sorry for him. I couldn't stay angry at him if I tried. Who suffers from his grumpy moods? He does, every single time! Look, he gets it into his head to dislike us, so he refuses to come have Christmas dinner with us. What's the result? Well, he doesn't miss out on much of a meal."

"Actually, he missed out on a wonderful dinner!" his wife chimed in. Everyone else immediately agreed, and they were certainly qualified to judge, having just eaten it themselves and now sitting around the hearth by lamplight with dessert on the table.

"Well, I'm delighted to hear that," Fred laughed, "because I never place too much trust in these new young homemakers! What do you say, Topper?"

Topper clearly had a crush on one of Fred's sisters-in-law, because he answered that an unmarried bachelor was a pathetic outcast who had no right to have an opinion on home cooking. At that, the sister-in-law—the curvy one wearing the lace collar, not the one with the roses in her hair—blushed bright red.

"Do go on, Fred!" his wife urged, clapping her hands. "He never finishes a story! He's utterly ridiculous!"

Fred burst into another fit of laughter, and because his joy was so contagious—even though the blushing sister tried to hide her giggles behind a scented handkerchief—the entire room dissolved into laughter with him.

"All I was going to say," Fred explained, "is that the only result of his disliking us and refusing to celebrate is that he misses out on some happy moments, which would do him a world of good. He certainly loses out on better company than he'll ever find in his own bitter thoughts, trapped in his dreary old office or his dusty rooms. I plan to give him the exact same invitation every single year, whether he likes it or not, because I truly feel for him. He can grumble about Christmas until his dying day, but he won't be able to ignore it if I show up year after year with a cheerful smile, saying, 'Uncle Scrooge, how are you?' If it softens him enough to leave fifty pounds to his poor clerk, that's already something! And I think I made a bit of an impression on him yesterday."

Everyone laughed at the idea of Fred rattling Scrooge. But being thoroughly good-natured and simply wanting everyone to be happy, Fred laughed right along with them and cheerfully poured more drinks.

After tea, they enjoyed some music. They were a talented family, and when they harmonized on classic tunes, they really knew what they were doing—especially Topper, who could rumble out rich bass notes effortlessly, without breaking a sweat or turning red in the face. Fred's wife played beautifully on the harp, and among the songs she played was a simple little melody—a sweet little tune you could learn to whistle in two minutes. It was the very same song Scrooge's little sister had loved when she came to bring him home from boarding school, just as the Ghost of Christmas Past had shown him. As the delicate notes drifted through the room, everything the first Spirit had shown him came flooding back. Scrooge's heart softened deeper and deeper. He realized that if he had only listened to music like that more often over the years, he might have nurtured life’s simple kindnesses for his own happiness with his own hands, instead of waiting for the grave-digger's spade that buried Jacob Marley to remind him of mortality.

They didn't spend the whole evening just singing, though. Before long they moved on to party games. It does people good to act like children sometimes, and never more so than at Christmas, when its great Founder was a child Himself. Wait—first they played blindman's buff! Of course they did. And I don't believe for a single second that Topper was genuinely blindfolded, any more than I believe he had eyes in his boots. In my view, it was all arranged between him and Fred, and the Ghost of Christmas Present knew it too! The way Topper chased after that pretty, curvy sister in the lace collar was an absolute insult to common sense! Knocking over fireplace tools, tripping over chairs, bumping into the piano, tangling himself in window drapes—wherever she ran, there he went! He always knew exactly where she was. He stubbornly refused to tag anyone else. If someone deliberately bumped into him (as several people did), he made a ridiculous half-hearted grab that fooled nobody, then immediately veered back toward the curvy sister. She shrieked repeatedly that it wasn't fair—and it certainly wasn't! But when he finally trapped her in a corner with no way out, despite all her rustling silk skirts and quick dodges, his behavior was delightfully shameless! He pretended he didn't know who she was, insisting he had to touch her hair ribbons, inspect a specific ring on her finger, and feel a particular necklace around her throat just to confirm her identity! Outrageous! Of course, she gave him a piece of her mind later when someone else took a turn wearing the blindfold—while the two of them whispered secrets together behind the drapes.

Fred's wife didn't join the blindman's buff, resting comfortably in a plush armchair with a footstool in a cozy corner, where the Ghost and Scrooge stood right behind her. But she enthusiastically joined in the guessing games, reciting word puzzles through every letter of the alphabet with flying colors. She was fantastic at the question-and-answer games too, thoroughly beating her sisters—much to Fred's delight, even though her sisters were plenty quick-witted themselves, as Topper could attest. There were about twenty guests, young and old, and every single one played—including Scrooge! Caught up in the fun and completely forgetting that they couldn't hear him, Scrooge shouted his guesses out loud, and more often than not, he guessed correctly! For all his stubborn bluntness, Scrooge was still as sharp as a tack.

The Ghost was thrilled to see Scrooge enjoying himself like this, looking on him with such warmth that Scrooge pleaded like an excited schoolboy to stay until the guests left. But the Spirit told him that wasn't possible.

"Wait, here's a new game!" Scrooge begged. "Just thirty more minutes, Spirit—only half an hour!"

It was a game called "Yes and No," where Fred had to think of something and everyone else had to guess what it was by asking questions that he could only answer with yes or no. A rapid fire of questions revealed that he was thinking of an animal—a living animal, a rather unpleasant animal, a savage animal, one that growled and grunted, but also talked! It lived in London, roamed the city streets, wasn't put on display in a circus, wasn't led around on a leash, didn't live in a zoo, was never butchered for meat, and was definitely not a horse, a donkey, a cow, a bull, a tiger, a dog, a pig, a cat, or a bear. With every new question, Fred collapsed in fresh roars of laughter, so thoroughly amused that he jumped up from the sofa and stomped his feet. Finally, the curvy sister, catching his infectious laughter, shrieked:

"I’ve got it! I know what it is, Fred! I know!"

"What is it?" cried Fred.

"It's your Uncle Scro-o-o-oge!"

And so it was! Everyone cheered in delight, though a few jokingly argued that when asked, "Is it a bear?" Fred really should have said yes, since saying no had thrown everyone off track!

"He's certainly given us plenty of laughs tonight," Fred said, "and it would be ungrateful not to raise a glass to him. Here's a mug of warm mulled wine right in front of us, and I propose: Uncle Scrooge!"

"Hear, hear! Uncle Scrooge!" they all echoed.

"A Merry Christmas and a Happy New Year to the old man, whatever his faults!" Fred declared. "He refused my wishes in person, but I hope he finds joy nonetheless. To Uncle Scrooge!"

Scrooge had grown so lighthearted and merry that he would have gladly raised a toast right back to the oblivious party and thanked them with an earnest speech if the Ghost had given him the chance. But the entire room vanished on the very breath of Fred's final words, and Scrooge and the Spirit were traveling once more.

They saw so much and journeyed far, visiting countless homes, and each visit brought comfort and joy. The Spirit stood beside hospital sickbeds, and the ill found peace and good cheer; he visited foreign lands, and travelers felt close to home; he paused beside weary, struggling workers, and they found patience in a brighter hope; he touched deep poverty, and made it feel spiritually rich. In shelters, hospitals, and prisons—in every sanctuary of human sorrow where arrogant mortals in their brief moments of power hadn't locked the doors to shut the Spirit out—he left his blessing, teaching Scrooge how love and kindness can heal the world.

It felt like an extraordinarily long night—if indeed it was only a single night. Scrooge began to doubt that it was, for the entire holiday season seemed packed into the time they spent wandering together. Stranger still, while Scrooge didn't change at all on the outside, the Ghost was visibly aging. Scrooge had noticed this gradual shift but kept quiet about it until they left a festive Twelfth Night party for children. Standing together in an open square, Scrooge looked up and saw that the Spirit’s hair had turned silvery gray.

"Are a spirit's days so short?" Scrooge asked.

"My life on this earth is very brief," the Ghost answered softly. "It ends tonight."

"Tonight!" cried Scrooge.

"Tonight at midnight. Listen! The hour is almost here."

Right on cue, church bells rang out quarter to twelve.

"Pardon me if I have no right to ask," said Scrooge, staring intently at the hem of the Spirit’s robe, "but I notice something strange peeking out from beneath your folds. Is that a foot or a claw?"

"It might as well be a claw, given how little flesh is on it," the Spirit replied with profound sorrow. "Look here."

From beneath the folds of his green robe, the Spirit revealed two children. They were wretched, starving, terrified, and heartbreakingly miserable. They dropped to their knees at his feet, clinging desperately to his garment.

"Look, O Mankind! Look down here!" the Ghost cried.

They were a boy and a girl. Sallow, gaunt, dressed in rags, scowling and feral, yet bowed low in desperate pleading. Where youthful beauty should have rounded their hollow cheeks and painted them with healthy color, a cruel, withered hand—like the touch of bitter old age—had pinched and twisted their features into misery. Where the light of innocence should have shone, despair and menace lurked. In all the vast wonders of creation, no decay, no corruption, no distortion of human nature could produce monsters half so terrifying and tragic.

Scrooge recoiled in horror. He wanted to say they were handsome children, but the words caught in his throat, refusing to utter such a monstrous lie.

"Spirit! Are they yours?" Scrooge choked out.

"They belong to Man," the Spirit answered, looking down at them. "They cling to me, crying out against the cruelty of their fathers. This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want. Beware them both, and all who follow them! But most of all, beware this boy, for across his forehead I see written DOOM, unless that curse is wiped away! Deny it!" shouted the Spirit, thrusting an accusing hand toward the distant city. "Slander those who speak the truth! Twist it for your political gain and make it worse—and suffer the consequences when the end arrives!"

"Do they have nowhere to go? No help at all?" cried Scrooge.

"Are there no prisons?" the Spirit turned on him, piercing him for the last time with his own cold words. "Are there no workhouses?"

The clock struck midnight.

Scrooge looked around for the Ghost, but he was gone. As the final chime faded into silence, he remembered Jacob Marley's warning. Lifting his eyes, he saw a dark, solemn Phantom, draped and hooded, gliding toward him like a thick mist sweeping along the ground.
