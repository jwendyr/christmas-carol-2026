# Chapter 1: Marley's Ghost

Marley was dead, to begin with. There was no doubt about that whatsoever. The burial register had been signed by the clergyman, the town clerk, the undertaker, and the chief mourner. Scrooge signed it too. And Scrooge's name carried weight at the Royal Exchange for anything he chose to put his hand to.

Old Marley was as dead as a doornail.

Mind you! I don't mean to pretend that I know from personal experience why a doornail is supposed to be especially dead. Personally, I would have thought a coffin nail was the deadest piece of ironmongery in the hardware trade. But the wisdom of our ancestors lives in that phrase, and my unhallowed hands won't mess with it, or the whole country might fall apart. So permit me to repeat, emphatically: Marley was as dead as a doornail.

Did Scrooge know he was dead? Of course he did. How could he not? He and Scrooge had been business partners for who knows how many years. Scrooge was his sole executor, his sole administrator, his sole legal heir, his sole residuary legatee, his only friend, and his only mourner. And even Scrooge wasn't so heartbroken by the sad occasion that it stopped him from being an excellent businessman on the very day of the funeral, sealing the deal with an undeniable bargain.

The mention of Marley's funeral brings me right back to where I started. There is no doubt that Marley was dead. You have to clearly understand this, or nothing miraculous can come of the story I am about to tell. If we weren't completely convinced that Hamlet's father had died before the play even started, there would be nothing more remarkable about his ghost taking a midnight stroll in a biting east wind along the castle walls than there would be in any other middle-aged gentleman walking out after dark into a breezy spot—say, St. Paul's Churchyard—just to startle his poor son out of his wits.

Scrooge never painted over Old Marley's name. There it stayed, years later, right above the warehouse door: *Scrooge and Marley*. The firm was still known as Scrooge and Marley. Sometimes people new to the business called Scrooge "Scrooge," and sometimes they called him "Marley." He answered to both names. It was all the same to him.

Oh, but he was a tight-fisted penny-pincher at the grindstone, that Scrooge! A squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, greedy old sinner! Hard and sharp as flint, from which no steel had ever struck a single spark of generous fire. Secretive, self-contained, and as solitary as an oyster. The bitter cold inside him froze his aging features, nipped his pointed nose, shriveled his cheeks, and stiffened his walk. It turned his eyes red and his thin lips blue, and rasped out shrewdly in his grating voice. A frosty layer of white coated his head, his eyebrows, and his wiry chin. He carried his own freezing temperature wherever he went. He iced his office during the dog days of summer, and didn't thaw it by a single degree for Christmas.

External heat and cold had virtually no effect on Scrooge. No warmth could warm him; no winter storm could chill him. No howling wind was bitterer than he was, no falling snow more stubborn in its purpose, no driving rain less open to mercy. Bad weather simply had no hold on him. The heaviest downpours, snow, hail, and sleet could boast an advantage over him in only one way: they often came down generously, while Scrooge never came down with a penny.

Nobody ever stopped him on the street to say with a cheerful smile, "My dear Scrooge, how are you? When are you coming over to visit?" No beggars pleaded with him for spare change; no children ever asked him what time it was; not once in his entire life did any man or woman ask Scrooge for directions. Even the guide dogs of the blind seemed to know him. When they spotted him coming, they would tug their owners into doorways and down side alleys, wagging their tails as if to say, "No eyes at all are better than an evil eye, dark master!"

But what did Scrooge care? That was the very thing he liked best. To edge his way along the crowded paths of life, warning all human sympathy to keep its distance—that was pure heaven to Scrooge.

Once upon a time—of all the good days in the year, on Christmas Eve—old Scrooge sat busy in his countinghouse. The weather was cold, bleak, and biting, and thick with fog. Outside in the courtyard, he could hear people wheezing as they hurried past, beating their chests with their hands and stomping their feet on the cobblestones to keep warm. The city clocks had only just struck three, but it was pitch-dark already—it hadn't been properly light all day. Candles flickered in the windows of neighboring offices like reddish smears against the heavy brown air. Fog poured through every crack and keyhole, and hung so dense outside that even though the courtyard was very narrow, the houses opposite looked like mere phantoms. Watching the dingy smog roll down and swallow everything, you might have thought Nature itself lived right around the corner, brewing up storms on a massive industrial scale.

The door to Scrooge's office stayed open so he could keep an eye on his clerk, who was copying letters in a dismal little cubicle next door—a sort of freezing tank. Scrooge had a tiny coal fire, but the clerk's fire was so much smaller that it looked like a single glowing ember. Yet the clerk couldn't add more coal, because Scrooge kept the coal box in his own private room. As sure as the clerk walked in carrying the shovel, his boss would warn him that they might have to part ways. So the clerk wrapped his white woolen scarf tightly around his neck and tried to warm his frozen hands over the candle flame. Lacking a powerful imagination, he failed miserably.

"Merry Christmas, Uncle! God bless you!" cried a cheerful voice.

It was Scrooge's nephew, who arrived so briskly through the fog that his greeting was the first notice Scrooge had of his approach.

"Bah!" said Scrooge. "Humbug!"

The nephew had walked so fast through the frost and mist that he was positively glowing. His face was rosy and handsome, his eyes sparkled, and his breath puffed out in clouds of steam.

"Christmas a humbug, Uncle?" said Scrooge's nephew. "You don't really mean that, surely?"

"I do," said Scrooge. "Merry Christmas! What right do you have to be merry? What reason do you have to be merry? You're poor enough."

"Come on, then," the nephew replied cheerfully. "What right do you have to be dismal? What reason do you have to be morose? You're rich enough."

Having no better answer ready on the spur of the moment, Scrooge grunted, "Bah!" again, and followed it up with another "Humbug."

"Don't be cross, Uncle!" said the nephew.

"What else can I be," replied the uncle, "when I live in such a world of fools as this? Merry Christmas! Out upon 'Merry Christmas'! What's Christmas to you but a time for paying bills without money? A time for finding yourself a year older, but not an hour richer? A time for balancing your books and having every single entry across twelve long months stacked dead against you? If I had my way," Scrooge added indignantly, "every idiot who runs around with 'Merry Christmas' on their lips should be boiled in their own Christmas pudding, and buried with a stake of holly driven through their heart. They should!"

"Uncle!" the nephew pleaded.

"Nephew!" returned the uncle sternly. "Keep Christmas in your own way, and let me keep it in mine."

"Keep it!" repeated Scrooge's nephew. "But you don't keep it at all!"

"Then let me leave it alone," said Scrooge. "Much good may it do you! Much good it has ever done you!"

"There are many things from which I've derived good without profiting in cash, I dare say," returned the nephew. "Christmas among the rest. But I am sure I have always thought of Christmas, whenever it rolls around—apart from the sacred reverence due to its holy name and origin, if anything belonging to it can be set apart from that—as a good time. A kind, forgiving, charitable, pleasant time. It is the only time I know of, in the long calendar of the year, when men and women seem by one consent to open their shut-up hearts freely, and think of people below them as if they really were fellow passengers to the grave, and not another race of creatures bound on different journeys. And therefore, Uncle, even though it has never put a single scrap of gold or silver in my pocket, I believe that it has done me good, and will do me good. And I say: God bless it!"

The clerk in the tank couldn't help applauding. Instantly realizing how improper that was, he poked the fire and accidentally extinguished the last frail spark forever.

"Let me hear another sound from you," said Scrooge, "and you'll keep your Christmas by losing your job! You're quite the powerful speaker, sir," he added, turning back to his nephew. "I wonder you don't run for Parliament."

"Don't be angry, Uncle. Come! Dine with us tomorrow."

Scrooge told him he would see him—yes, indeed he did. He went the full length of the expression, and said he would see him in hell first.

"But why?" cried Scrooge's nephew. "Why?"

"Why did you get married?" said Scrooge.

"Because I fell in love."

"Because you fell in love!" growled Scrooge, as if that were the only thing in the world more ridiculous than a merry Christmas. "Good afternoon!"

"Come now, Uncle, you never came to visit me before that happened. Why give it as a reason for not coming now?"

"Good afternoon," said Scrooge.

"I want nothing from you. I ask nothing of you. Why can't we be friends?"

"Good afternoon," said Scrooge.

"I am sorry with all my heart to find you so determined. We have never had a quarrel that I started. But I made this effort in honor of Christmas, and I'll keep my Christmas spirit to the end. So: a Merry Christmas, Uncle!"

"Good afternoon!" said Scrooge.

"And a Happy New Year!"

"Good afternoon!" said Scrooge.

His nephew left the room without an angry word, in spite of everything. He stopped at the outer door to wish the season's greetings to the clerk, who, shivering as he was, was still warmer inside than Scrooge, for he returned them cordially.

"There's another lunatic," muttered Scrooge, who overheard him. "My clerk, earning fifteen shillings a week with a wife and family to feed, talking about a merry Christmas. I'll check myself into a madhouse."

In letting Scrooge's nephew out, the clerk had let two other gentlemen in. They were portly, pleasant-looking men, and now stood in Scrooge's office with their hats removed. They held books and papers in their hands, and bowed politely to him.

"Scrooge and Marley's, I believe," said one of the gentlemen, consulting his list. "Do I have the pleasure of addressing Mr. Scrooge or Mr. Marley?"

"Mr. Marley has been dead these seven years," Scrooge replied. "He died seven years ago this very night."

"We have no doubt his generosity is well represented by his surviving partner," said the gentleman, presenting his credentials.

It certainly was, for they had been two kindred spirits. At the ominous word "generosity," Scrooge frowned, shook his head, and handed the papers right back.

"At this festive season of the year, Mr. Scrooge," said the gentleman, picking up a pen, "it is more desirable than ever that we make some slight provision for the poor and destitute, who suffer greatly at the present time. Many thousands lack the common necessities of life; hundreds of thousands lack common comforts, sir."

"Are there no prisons?" asked Scrooge.

"Plenty of prisons," said the gentleman, laying down the pen again.

"And the union workhouses?" demanded Scrooge. "Are they still in operation?"

"They are. Though," returned the gentleman, "I wish I could say they were not."

"The treadmill and the Poor Law are in full swing, then?" said Scrooge.

"Both are very busy, sir."

"Oh! From what you said at first, I was afraid something had occurred to stop them in their useful course," said Scrooge. "I'm very glad to hear it."

"Under the impression that they scarcely furnish Christian cheer for mind or body to the multitude," replied the gentleman, "a few of us are trying to raise a fund to buy the poor some food, drink, and means of warmth. We choose this time because it is a season, above all others, when want is keenly felt and abundance rejoices. How much may I put you down for?"

"Nothing!" Scrooge replied.

"You wish to remain anonymous?"

"I wish to be left alone," said Scrooge. "Since you ask me what I wish, gentlemen, that is my answer. I don't celebrate Christmas myself, and I can't afford to make idle people merry. I help support the institutions I mentioned—they cost enough, and those who are badly off must go there."

"Many cannot go there, sir. And many would rather die."

"If they would rather die," said Scrooge, "they had better do it, and decrease the surplus population! Besides—excuse me—I don't know that for a fact."

"You could know it," observed the gentleman.

"It's not my business," Scrooge returned. "It's enough for a man to understand his own business, and not interfere with other people's. Mine occupies me constantly. Good afternoon, gentlemen!"

Seeing clearly that it would be useless to pursue the matter, the gentlemen withdrew. Scrooge resumed his work with a higher opinion of himself, and in a mood far more smug and facetious than was usual for him.

Meanwhile, the fog and darkness grew so thick that men ran about with blazing torches, offering to walk ahead of carriage horses and guide them on their way. The ancient tower of a church—whose gruff old bell was always peeping slyly down at Scrooge through a Gothic window in the stone—became invisible. It struck the hours and quarter-hours up in the clouds, with quivering vibrations afterward as if its teeth were chattering in its frozen head. The cold became intense. Around the corner on the main street, workers repairing the gas pipes had lit a roaring fire in a brazier. A ragged crowd of men and boys gathered around it, warming their hands and blinking their eyes before the blaze in pure delight. A forgotten water pump out on the street sullenly froze over, its overflow turning to bitter, misanthropic ice. The bright glow of shop windows, where sprigs of holly and red berries crackled in the lamp heat, made pale faces look rosy as they hurried by. Poulterers' and grocers' shops became a dazzling spectacle—a glorious holiday pageant that made it hard to believe dull everyday trade and commerce had anything to do with it. The Lord Mayor, inside the mighty fortress of the Mansion House, gave orders to his fifty cooks and butlers to celebrate Christmas in grand style. Even the little tailor, who had been fined five shillings that previous Monday for being drunk and rowdy in the street, was up in his attic stirring tomorrow's pudding, while his slender wife and baby stepped out into the freezing air to buy their Christmas beef.

Foggier yet, and colder still. Piercing, searching, biting cold. If Saint Dunstan had only pinched the Devil's nose with a blast of weather like that instead of using his trusty blacksmith's tongs, the fiend would have roared with a vengeance. A boy with a freezing, shivering nose—gnawed and bitten by the hungry cold like a bone chewed by dogs—bent down to Scrooge's keyhole to treat him to a Christmas carol. But at the very first words:

"God bless you, merry gentleman!
May nothing you dismay!"

Scrooge seized his heavy ruler with such sudden fury that the singer fled in terror, leaving the keyhole to the fog and the far more welcoming frost.

At last, the hour arrived to close up the office. With great reluctance, Scrooge climbed down from his stool, silently conceding the fact to his expectant clerk in the tank, who instantly snuffed out his candle and put on his hat.

"You'll want the entire day off tomorrow, I suppose?" said Scrooge.

"If it's quite convenient, sir."

"It's not convenient," said Scrooge, "and it's not fair. If I docked you half a crown for it, you'd think yourself mistreated, wouldn't you?"

The clerk smiled faintly.

"And yet," said Scrooge, "you don't think I'm mistreated when I pay you a full day's wages for no work."

The clerk observed that it was only once a year.

"A poor excuse for picking a man's pocket every twenty-fifth of December!" said Scrooge, buttoning his heavy overcoat right up to his chin. "Well, I suppose you must have the whole day. But be here all the earlier the next morning!"

The clerk promised that he would. Scrooge walked out with a growl. The office was closed in a heartbeat, and the clerk—with the long ends of his white scarf dangling past his waist, since he possessed no warm overcoat—ran down an icy slide on Cornhill behind a line of boys twenty times in honor of Christmas Eve. Then he ran home to Camden Town as fast as he could sprint, to play a lively game of blindman's buff.

Scrooge ate his melancholy dinner in his usual melancholy tavern. Having read all the newspapers and passed the rest of the evening checking over his bankbook, he headed home to bed.

He lived in a set of rooms that had once belonged to his deceased partner. They were a gloomy suite in a brooding, dark building tucked away in a courtyard. The house looked so out of place that one could scarcely help imagining it had run there when it was young, playing hide-and-seek with other buildings, and had forgotten the way back out. It was old now, and dreary enough, for nobody lived in it except Scrooge; all the other rooms were rented out as offices. The courtyard was so pitch-black that even Scrooge, who knew every single stone, had to feel his way along with his hands. The fog and frost hung so heavily around the blackened gateway of the house that it seemed as if the Spirit of the Weather sat in mournful meditation upon the threshold.

Now, it is a fact that there was nothing at all unusual about the knocker on the door, except that it was very large. It is also a fact that Scrooge had seen it morning and night every day he had lived there. Furthermore, Scrooge had as little imagination as any man in the City of London—which is a bold claim, even including the city council, aldermen, and livery companies. Let it also be borne in mind that Scrooge had not given a single thought to Marley since mentioning his seven-years-dead partner earlier that afternoon.

And then let any man explain to me, if he can, how it happened that as Scrooge put his key into the lock, he looked up at the knocker and saw—without any intermediate process of change—not a knocker, but Marley's face.

Marley's face.

It was not shrouded in deep shadow like the other objects in the courtyard, but gave off a faint, eerie light, like a decaying lobster in a dark cellar. It was not angry or ferocious. It looked at Scrooge exactly as Marley used to look: with ghostly spectacles pushed up onto its ghostly forehead. Its hair was curiously stirred, as if ruffled by warm air or hot breath. Though the eyes were wide open, they were completely motionless. That, combined with its pale, sickly color, made it horrible. Yet its horror seemed to be something inflicted upon the face rather than an expression it chose to make.

As Scrooge stared fixedly at this phenomenon, it was a knocker again.

To say that he was not startled, or that his blood did not run cold with a terrible shock he hadn't felt since childhood, would be untrue. But he took hold of the key he had dropped, turned it firmly, walked in, and lit his candle.

He did pause with a moment's hesitation before shutting the door. He looked cautiously behind it first, as if half-expecting to be terrified by the sight of Marley's pigtail sticking out into the hall. But there was nothing on the back of the door except the nuts and screws that held the knocker in place. So he muttered, "Nonsense, nonsense!" and slammed it shut with a bang.

The sound boomed through the house like thunder. Every room upstairs, and every wine cask down in the merchant's cellars below, seemed to echo with a crash of its own. But Scrooge was not a man to be frightened by echoes. He bolted the heavy door, crossed the hall, and climbed the stairs—slowly, trimming his candle as he went.

People talk casually about driving a four-horse coach up an old flight of stairs, or slipping a coach through a loosely written Act of Parliament. But I tell you, you could have driven a hearse up that staircase—broadside, with the crossbar toward the wall and the doors toward the banister—and done it with ease. There was plenty of width and room to spare. Perhaps that was why Scrooge thought he saw a ghostly hearse rolling up the gloomy stairway ahead of him. Half a dozen gas streetlamps wouldn't have lit the entryway properly, so you can imagine how dim it was with only Scrooge's cheap tallow candle.

Up Scrooge went, not caring a button for that. Darkness is cheap, and Scrooge liked it. But before closing his heavy inner door, he walked through his rooms to make sure everything was in order. He had just enough memory of the face to want to check.

Living room, bedroom, storage room. All as they should be. Nobody under the table, nobody under the sofa. A small fire burned in the grate. The spoon and bowl were ready, and a little saucepan of oatmeal gruel—Scrooge had a head cold—sat on the stove ledge. Nobody under the bed. Nobody in the closet. Nobody hiding in his dressing gown, which hung against the wall in a suspicious, lifelike drape. The storage room was entirely normal: an old fireplace fender, worn shoes, two fish baskets, a three-legged washstand, and a poker.

Quite satisfied, he closed his door and locked himself in—double-locked himself in, which was not his habit. Secure against surprises, he took off his necktie, put on his dressing gown, slippers, and nightcap, and sat down before the hearth to eat his gruel.

It was a very low fire indeed—practically nothing on such a bitter night. He had to sit right against it, hunched over the hearth, just to extract the slightest warmth from such a handful of fuel. The fireplace was an old one, built by a Dutch merchant long ago, bordered all around with quaint Dutch tiles illustrating scenes from Scripture. There were Cains and Abels, Pharaoh's daughters, Queens of Sheba, angels drifting down through the air on clouds like featherbeds, Abrahams, Belshazzars, and Apostles setting off to sea in butter-boat skiffs—hundreds of figures to catch his eye.

Yet that face of Marley, dead seven years, came like the ancient Prophet's rod and swallowed up the whole scene. If every smooth tile had been blank at first, with the power to shape some picture on its surface from the scattered fragments of his thoughts, a copy of old Marley's head would have appeared on every single one.

"Humbug!" said Scrooge, and paced across the room.

After several turns, he sat down again. As he leaned his head back against the chair, his gaze happened to rest upon a bell—an old, disused servant's bell hanging in the room that had once connected, for some long-forgotten purpose, to a chamber on the top floor of the building. To his immense astonishment and creeping dread, as he watched, the bell began to swing.

It swayed so softly at first that it scarcely made a sound. But soon it was ringing loudly, and suddenly every single bell in the entire house began to ring along with it!

This might have lasted half a minute, or maybe a full minute, but it felt like an hour. Then the bells ceased just as they had begun, all together.

Immediately following, a heavy clanking sound echoed from deep down below—as if someone were dragging a massive chain across the barrels in the wine merchant's cellar. Scrooge remembered hearing old stories about ghosts dragging heavy chains in haunted houses.

The cellar door flew open with a booming crash! The noise grew much louder on the floors below, then came clanking up the stairs, and headed straight toward his door.

"It's humbug still!" said Scrooge. "I won't believe it."

His color drained, however, when the figure passed straight through the heavy wooden door without pausing, and stepped directly into the room before his eyes. As it entered, the dying flame in the hearth leaped up, as if crying out, "I know him—it's Marley's Ghost!" and then dropped back down.

The same face—the very same. Marley wore his usual pigtail wig, familiar vest, tight trousers, and boots, with the tassels on his boots bristling just like his pigtail, his coat tails, and the hair on his head. The chain he dragged was clasped tightly around his waist. It was long, winding around him like a tail, and was made—for Scrooge observed it closely—of iron cash boxes, keys, padlocks, account ledgers, legal deeds, and heavy steel purses. His body was transparent, so that as Scrooge observed him and looked through his vest, he could clearly see the two buttons on the back of his coat.

Scrooge had often heard it said that Marley had no guts, but he had never believed it until now.

No, and he didn't truly believe it even now. Even though he looked the phantom through and through, and saw it standing right in front of him; even though he felt the chilling influence of its death-cold eyes, and marked the woven texture of the folded cloth tied around its head and under its jaw—a burial wrap he had not noticed before—he remained stubborn and fought against his senses.

"How now!" said Scrooge, sarcastic and cold as ever. "What do you want with me?"

"Much!"—it was Marley's voice, without a doubt.

"Who are you?"

"Ask me who I was."

"Who were you, then?" said Scrooge, raising his voice. "You're awfully particular for a ghost." He had been about to say "down to a shade," but thought better of it.

"In life, I was your partner, Jacob Marley."

"Can you... can you sit down?" asked Scrooge, looking doubtfully at him.

"I can."

"Do it, then."

Scrooge asked because he didn't know whether a ghost so transparent could actually use a chair, and feared that if it proved impossible, it might lead to an awkward explanation. But the ghost sat down on the opposite side of the fireplace as naturally as if he were quite used to it.

"You don't believe in me," observed the Ghost.

"I don't," said Scrooge.

"What evidence of my reality would you have beyond that of your senses?"

"I don't know," said Scrooge.

"Why do you doubt your senses?"

"Because," said Scrooge, "a little thing affects them. A slight stomach disorder makes them cheats. You may be an undigested bit of beef, a blot of mustard, a crumb of cheese, or a fragment of an underdone potato. There's more of gravy than of grave about you, whatever you are!"

Scrooge was not in the habit of cracking jokes, nor did he feel in any way funny at that moment. The truth was that he tried to sound clever just to distract himself and keep his terror down, for the phantom's voice chilled him to the very marrow of his bones.

To sit staring into those fixed, glazed eyes in silence was more than Scrooge could endure. There was something awful, too, about the eerie atmosphere that surrounded the phantom. Scrooge could not feel heat himself, but the Ghost sat enveloped in it; for though it remained motionless, its hair, coat tails, and boot tassels were constantly fluttering as if caught in the hot vapor of an oven.

"You see this toothpick?" said Scrooge, quickly pressing the point to divert the vision's stony gaze from himself, if only for a second.

"I do," replied the Ghost.

"You aren't looking at it," said Scrooge.

"Yet I see it notwithstanding," said the Ghost.

"Well!" returned Scrooge. "I have only to swallow this, and for the rest of my days I'll be tormented by a legion of goblins, all of my own creation. Humbug, I tell you! Humbug!"

At this, the spirit raised a frightful cry and shook its chain with such a dismal, horrifying racket that Scrooge clutched his chair with both hands to keep from fainting. But his terror grew tenfold when the phantom untied the cloth bandage around its head—as if it were too warm indoors—and its lower jaw dropped right down onto its chest!

Scrooge fell upon his knees and clasped his hands before his face.

"Mercy!" he cried. "Dreadful apparition, why do you trouble me?"

"Man of worldly greed!" replied the Ghost. "Do you believe in me or not?"

"I do!" said Scrooge. "I must! But why do spirits walk the earth, and why do they come to me?"

"It is required of every human being," the Ghost returned, "that the spirit within should walk among their fellow creatures, traveling far and wide. And if that spirit does not go forth in life, it is condemned to do so after death. It is doomed to wander through the world—oh, woe is me!—and witness the misery it can no longer share, but might have shared on earth and turned to happiness!"

Again the phantom let out a cry, shook its chain, and wrung its shadowy hands.

"You are chained," said Scrooge, trembling. "Tell me why!"

"I wear the chain I forged in life," replied the Ghost. "I made it link by link, and yard by yard. I girded it on of my own free will, and of my own free will I wore it. Is its pattern strange to you?"

Scrooge trembled more and more.

"Or would you know," pursued the Ghost, "the weight and length of the heavy coil you bear yourself? It was full as heavy and as long as this seven Christmas Eves ago. You have labored on it ever since. It is an enormous, crushing chain!"

Scrooge glanced down at the floor, half-expecting to find himself wrapped in fifty or sixty yards of heavy iron cable, but he could see nothing.

"Jacob!" he begged. "Old Jacob Marley, tell me more. Speak comfort to me, Jacob!"

"I have none to give," the Ghost replied. "Comfort comes from other regions, Ebenezer Scrooge, conveyed by other ministers to other kinds of men. Nor can I tell you all that I wish. Only a very little more is permitted to me. I cannot rest, I cannot stay, I cannot linger anywhere. In life, my spirit never walked beyond our countinghouse—mark me!—my soul never roved beyond the narrow limits of our money-changing den. And weary journeys stretch before me!"

Whenever Scrooge grew thoughtful, it was his habit to put his hands into his pockets. Pondering what the Ghost had said, he did so now, but without lifting his eyes or getting up from his knees.

"You must have been very slow about it, Jacob," Scrooge observed in a businesslike tone, though with humility and deference.

"Slow!" the Ghost repeated.

"Seven years dead," mused Scrooge, "and traveling all the time!"

"The whole time," said the Ghost. "No rest, no peace. Only the constant torture of remorse."

"Do you travel fast?" said Scrooge.

"On the wings of the wind," replied the Ghost.

"You must have covered a huge amount of ground in seven years," said Scrooge.

The Ghost, on hearing this, set up another terrible wail, and clanked its chain so hideously in the dead silence of the night that the city watch would have been fully justified in arresting it for disturbing the peace.

"Oh, captive, bound, and doubly chained!" cried the phantom. "To not know that ages of endless labor by immortal creatures could never exhaust all the good this earth can hold! To not know that any Christian spirit, working kindly in whatever small sphere it finds itself, will discover its mortal life too short for all the good it could do! To not know that no amount of regret can ever make amends for one life's wasted opportunities! Yet that was me! Oh, that was me!"

"But you were always a good man of business, Jacob," faltered Scrooge, who now began to apply this to himself.

"Business!" cried the Ghost, wringing its hands again. "Humanity was my business! The common welfare was my business! Charity, mercy, forbearance, and benevolence—those were all my true business! The dealings of my trade were but a single drop of water in the vast ocean of my business!"

It held up its chain at arm's length, as if it were the root of all its useless grief, and flung it heavily onto the floor again.

"At this time of the rolling year," the spirit groaned, "I suffer most. Why did I walk through crowds of my fellow human beings with my eyes cast down, and never raise them to that blessed Star which led the Wise Men to a humble stable? Were there no poor homes to which its light would have guided me?"

Scrooge was terrified to hear the ghost speaking with such desperate anguish, and began to tremble violently.

"Hear me!" cried the Ghost. "My time is nearly gone."

"I will," said Scrooge. "Only don't be hard on me! Don't be so dramatic, Jacob! I beg you!"

"How it is that I appear before you in a shape you can see, I cannot tell. I have sat invisible beside you for many and many a day."

It was not a comforting thought. Scrooge shivered, and wiped the cold sweat from his brow.

"That has been no small part of my penance," pursued the Ghost. "I am here tonight to warn you that you still have a hope and a chance of escaping my fate. A chance and a hope of my procuring, Ebenezer."

"You were always a good friend to me," said Scrooge. "Thank you."

"You will be haunted," resumed the Ghost, "by Three Spirits."

Scrooge's face fell almost as low as the ghost's jaw had done.

"Is that the chance and hope you mentioned, Jacob?" he asked in a faltering voice.

"It is."

"Then... I think I'd rather not," said Scrooge.

"Without their visits," warned the Ghost, "you cannot hope to escape the path I walk. Expect the first spirit tomorrow night, when the bell tolls One."

"Couldn't I take them all at once, and have it over with, Jacob?" hinted Scrooge.

"Expect the second spirit on the next night at the same hour. The third upon the following night, when the last chime of Twelve has ceased to vibrate. You will see me no more. And make sure that, for your own sake, you remember what has passed between us tonight!"

When it had said these words, the phantom took its cloth wrapper from the table and tied it securely around its head as before. Scrooge knew this by the sharp click its teeth made when its jaws were clamped back together. Gathering his courage, Scrooge looked up and found his supernatural visitor confronting him in an upright posture, with its heavy chain wound over and around its arm.

The apparition began to back away from him. At every step it took, the window raised itself a little, so that when the phantom reached the ledge, it was wide open.

It beckoned Scrooge to approach, and he did. When they were within two paces of each other, Marley's Ghost held up its hand, warning him to come no closer. Scrooge stopped.

He stopped not so much out of obedience as in shock and terror. As the ghost raised its hand, Scrooge became aware of confused cries filling the air outside—incoherent sounds of lamentation and regret, wailings inexpressibly sorrowful and self-accusing. The spirit listened for a moment, joined in the mournful chorus, and floated out into the bleak, dark night.

Scrooge rushed to the window, desperate in his curiosity. He looked out.

The sky was filled with phantoms, wandering restlessly back and forth in agonizing haste, moaning as they went. Every single one wore chains like Marley's Ghost. A few (perhaps guilty rulers or corrupt officials) were linked together, but none were free. Many had been personally known to Scrooge in life. He had been well acquainted with one elderly ghost in a white vest, with a monstrous iron safe chained to its ankle, who cried piteously at being unable to assist a wretched woman with a baby sitting on a doorstep below. The misery they all shared was painfully clear: they desperately wished to intervene for good in human lives, but had lost the power forever.

Whether these creatures faded into the mist or the mist swallowed them up, Scrooge could not tell. But both the phantoms and their ghostly voices melted away together, and the night became once more as it had been when he walked home.

Scrooge closed the window and examined the door through which the Ghost had entered. It was still double-locked, just as he had locked it with his own hands, and the bolts were completely undisturbed. He tried to say "Humbug!" but stopped after the very first syllable. Feeling utterly drained—whether from the terror he had undergone, the exhaustion of the day, his glimpse of the spirit world, the somber conversation with Marley, or simply the lateness of the hour—he desperately needed rest. Without even undressing, he climbed straight into bed and fell asleep the instant his head hit the pillow.

# Chapter 2: The First of the Three Spirits

When Scrooge woke up, it was pitch-black. Peering out from his bed, he could barely tell the frosted window apart from the solid, shadowy walls of his room. He was squinting into the gloom with his sharp, ferret-like eyes when the chimes of a nearby church clock struck four quarters. He held his breath and listened for the hour.

To his utter astonishment, the deep, heavy bell kept tolling—six, seven, eight, climbing steadily all the way to twelve. Then it stopped.

Twelve! It had been past two in the morning when he went to sleep. The clock had to be broken. An icicle must have jammed the gears. Twelve? Impossible!

He pressed the spring of his pocket watch to check the chime and prove the church clock wrong. The little watch clicked, chimed twelve rapid beats, and went silent.

"That makes no sense," Scrooge muttered. "I couldn't have slept through an entire day and halfway into another night. And surely nothing happened to the sun—this can't be twelve noon!"

The thought rattled him so much that he scrambled out of bed and felt his way through the dark to the window. He had to wipe away the thick frost with the sleeve of his dressing gown before he could peer outside—and even then, he could see very little. All he could tell was that the fog was as thick as ever, the air bitterly cold, and the street completely silent. There were no crowds scurrying around or raising a fuss, as there surely would have been if eternal night had swallowed the daytime world.

That was an enormous relief. After all, financial promissory notes payable to "Mr. Ebenezer Scrooge or his order within three days" would be worthless scraps of paper if days stopped existing altogether!

Scrooge climbed back into bed and thought, and thought, and thought it over and over, unable to make head or tail of it. The harder he thought, the more confused he became; and the more he tried to force himself not to think, the more his mind raced.

Marley's ghost troubled him deeply. Every time he reasoned through it and concluded it must have been a dream, his thoughts snapped right back like a released spring, dropping him right back into the very same nagging question: *Was it a dream, or wasn't it?*

Scrooge lay there wrestling with his doubts until three more quarters of an hour chimed. Suddenly, he remembered the ghost's warning: a spirit would visit him when the clock struck one. He decided right then to stay awake until the hour had passed. And since he was about as likely to fall asleep as he was to stroll straight into Heaven, staying awake was probably the easiest promise he could make.

That final quarter of an hour dragged on for so long that more than once he was convinced he had dozed off and missed the chimes.

At last, the sound echoed in his ears.

"Ding, dong!"

"A quarter past," Scrooge counted.

"Ding, dong!"

"Half-past!" Scrooge whispered.

"Ding, dong!"

"A quarter to one," Scrooge said.

"Ding, dong!"

"One o'clock!" Scrooge cried out in triumph. "And nothing happened!"

He spoke a second too soon. The great bell tolled with a deep, heavy, hollow, mournful *ONE*.

Instantly, a flash of bright light filled the room, and the curtains of his bed were pulled wide.

The curtains were drawn aside by a hand—I tell you, a real hand! Not the curtains at his feet, and not the curtains behind him, but the exact ones right in front of his face. The bed curtains parted, and Scrooge, propping himself up on his elbows in shock, found himself face-to-face with the otherworldly visitor who had drawn them. It was as close to him as I am to you right now—standing right beside your shoulder.

It was an extraordinary figure. It looked like a child, yet at the same time like an old man seen through some enchanted lens that shrank him down to a child's size without taking away his years. His hair, hanging loose past his neck and down his back, was pure white as though aged by centuries, yet his face was smooth and completely unwrinkled, glowing with the fresh, tender bloom of youth. His arms were long and surprisingly muscular, and his hands looked uncommonly strong. His bare legs and feet were gracefully formed. He wore a simple tunic of the purest white, cinched at the waist by a shimmering belt with a beautiful sheen. In his hand he held a sprig of fresh green holly; yet in vivid contrast to that winter greenery, his tunic was trimmed with bright summer flowers.

Stranger still, from the very crown of his head shot a brilliant, pure beam of light that illuminated everything around him. This bright glow was clearly why he carried a large metal extinguisher cap tucked under his arm, which he evidently used like a candle-snuffer to dim his light when needed.

Yet as Scrooge stared closer and closer, even that light was not the strangest thing about him. Just as the figure's belt sparkled here and there, shifting between light and shadow, the spirit's form kept shimmering and changing. One moment he seemed to have only one arm; then one leg; then twenty legs; now a pair of legs without a head, and now a floating head with no body at all. The parts that faded dissolved cleanly into the surrounding gloom without leaving a trace. And just as Scrooge gasped in disbelief, the spirit would coalesce again, whole, solid, and crystal clear.

"Are you the Spirit whose coming was foretold to me?" Scrooge asked.

"I am."

The voice was soft and gentle—curiously quiet, as if coming from far away rather than right beside him.

"Who and what are you?" Scrooge demanded.

"I am the Ghost of Christmas Past."

"Long past?" Scrooge inquired, noticing the spirit's small stature.

"No. Your past."

Scrooge couldn't have explained why, but he felt an overwhelming urge to see the Spirit put on that extinguisher cap, and politely asked him to cover his head.

"What!" the Ghost exclaimed. "Would you snuff out the light I bring so quickly with your worldly hands? Isn't it enough that you are one of those whose selfish passions forged this cap, forcing me for endless years to wear it pulled low over my brow?"

Scrooge quickly and humbly apologized, assuring the Spirit he meant no offense and had never knowingly tried to cap him in all his life. He then worked up the courage to ask what business had brought the ghost here.

"Your welfare," said the Ghost.

Scrooge said he was deeply grateful, though he couldn't help thinking that a solid, uninterrupted night of sleep would have done his welfare a lot more good. The Spirit must have read his thoughts, for it answered at once:

"Your reclamation, then. Pay close attention!"

The Spirit reached out with its strong hand and took him gently by the arm.

"Rise, and walk with me."

It would have been completely useless for Scrooge to argue that the weather and the hour were hardly suitable for a stroll; that his bed was cozy while the temperature outside was well below freezing; that he was dressed only in slippers, a dressing gown, and a nightcap; or that he was nursing a nasty head cold. The Spirit's grip, though as gentle as a feather, could not be resisted. Scrooge got out of bed. But when he saw the ghost heading straight for the window, he grabbed the spirit's robe in terror.

"I am only human," Scrooge protested. "I'll fall!"

"Just a touch of my hand here," the Spirit replied, placing a hand over Scrooge's heart, "and you will be supported through far more than this."

The moment those words were spoken, they passed straight through the wall and stood on an open country road bordered by fields on both sides. The city had vanished completely—not a single trace of London remained. The gloom and dense fog had disappeared along with it, replaced by a crisp, bright winter day with fresh snow coating the ground.

"Good heavens!" Scrooge gasped, clasping his hands together as he looked around. "I grew up here! I was a boy in this place!"

The Spirit looked upon him with a gentle gaze. That brief, soft touch upon Scrooge's heart still lingered warmly in his chest. He breathed in a thousand familiar scents drifting on the breeze, each one unlocking a flood of memories—a thousand forgotten thoughts, childhood hopes, simple joys, and worries from long, long ago.

"Your lip is trembling," the Ghost noticed. "And what is that on your cheek?"

Scrooge mumbled, his voice catching with an uncharacteristic crack, that it was just a blemish, and begged the Ghost to lead the way wherever he wished.

"Do you remember the road?" the Spirit asked.

"Remember it?" Scrooge cried with sudden passion. "I could walk it blindfolded!"

"Strange that you forgot it for so many years," the Ghost remarked. "Let us move on."

They walked down the road, Scrooge pointing out every gatepost, fence, and tree. Soon a little market town appeared ahead, complete with its arched bridge, church steeple, and winding river.

A herd of shaggy ponies came trotting toward them, carrying boys on their backs who waved and shouted cheerfully to other boys riding in farm carts and country wagons. Every single boy was bursting with high spirits, calling back and forth until the wide-open fields echoed with joyful noise, and the crisp winter air seemed to laugh along with them.

"These are only shadows of things that once were," the Ghost explained. "They cannot see or hear us."

The cheerful travelers passed by, and Scrooge recognized and named every single one of them. Why was his heart overflowing with joy just to see them? Why did his cold, flinty eyes glisten with tears, and why did his pulse race with excitement as they trotted past? Why did he feel so thrilled when they called out "Merry Christmas!" to each other as they split off at crossroads and country lanes heading home for the holidays?

What was "Merry Christmas" to Scrooge? Bah! What good had Christmas ever done for him?

And yet, his heart sang.

"The schoolhouse is not entirely empty," the Ghost remarked. "One solitary child, forgotten by his friends, is still left behind."

Scrooge said he knew it. And he broke down and sobbed.

They turned off the main road down a familiar lane and soon drew near a grand red-brick building with a little bell cupola topped by a weather vane perched on the roof. It had once been an impressive estate, but now showed obvious signs of decay. The large outbuildings were abandoned, their walls damp and covered in moss, the windowpanes cracked, and the iron gates rusted and sagging. Chickens clucked and strutted about the empty stables, while weeds and tall grass choked the carriage houses and sheds.

Inside was just as bleak. Stepping into the drafty entryway and looking through the open doorways, they saw cavernous, chilly rooms with barely any furniture. A musty, earthy smell hung in the air—a cold, barren atmosphere that instantly brought back memories of waking up in freezing darkness by candlelight and never having quite enough to eat.

The Ghost and Scrooge walked across the hall toward a door at the back of the house. It swung open to reveal a long, bleak, dreary classroom made even more barren by rows of plain wooden benches and desks. At one desk, near a pitiful little fire, sat a lonely boy reading all by himself.

Scrooge sat down on an empty bench and wept to see his poor, abandoned younger self just as he had been.

Every faint echo in the empty house, every scuttle of mice behind the baseboards, every slow drip from the half-frozen gutter out in the bleak courtyard, every winter breeze sighing through the bare branches of a solitary poplar tree, every creak of an unlatched shed door, even the soft clicking of the embers in the grate—everything touched Scrooge's hardened heart, melting his defenses and letting his tears flow freely.

The Spirit tapped him on the arm and pointed toward his younger self, totally absorbed in his book.

Suddenly, a man dressed in foreign desert robes, astonishingly real and clear, appeared just outside the window. He had a woodcutter's axe tucked into his sash and led a donkey laden with firewood by its reins.

"Look, it's Ali Baba!" Scrooge cried out in absolute delight. "Dear old, honest Ali Baba! Yes, yes, I remember! One Christmas, when that lonely child was left behind all by himself, Ali Baba appeared to him for the very first time, just like that! Poor boy! And Valentine," Scrooge babbled, "and his wild brother, Orson—look, there they go! And what's his name—the fellow left asleep in his underclothes outside the Gate of Damascus—don't you see him? And look at the Sultan's groom turned upside down by the Genie! There he is, standing right on his head! Serves him right, too! I'm glad of it! What business did he have trying to marry the princess?"

To hear Scrooge pouring his whole heart and soul into these childhood stories—his voice swinging wildly between laughter and tears, his face flushed and alive with wonder—would have stunned his business associates back in London beyond belief.

"And there's the parrot!" Scrooge cheered. "Bright green body, yellow tail, and a crest on his head like a little head of lettuce—there he is! 'Poor Robin Crusoe!' he used to squawk when Robinson came back after sailing around his island. 'Poor Robin Crusoe, where have you been, Robin Crusoe?' The poor castaway thought he was losing his mind, but it was just the bird talking! And look, there goes Friday, sprinting for his life down toward the creek! Hey! Look out! Hooray!"

Then, in a sudden shift that was completely unlike his usual stony self, he looked back at the lonely boy. "Poor kid," he whispered softly, and broke down in tears once more.

"I wish..." Scrooge murmured, reaching into his coat pocket and looking down after wiping his damp eyes with his sleeve. "Ah, but it's too late now."

"What troubles you?" the Spirit asked.

"Nothing," Scrooge said quickly. "Nothing at all. Just... there was a boy singing a Christmas carol outside my door last night. I really wish I'd given him something, that's all."

The Ghost offered a knowing, thoughtful smile and waved its hand. "Let us look upon another Christmas."

As the Spirit spoke, Scrooge's younger self grew taller, and the room became older, darker, and dustier. The wooden wall panels shrank, cracks spiderwebbed across the windowpanes, and chunks of plaster broke away from the ceiling, exposing the bare wooden slats underneath. How this magical change happened, Scrooge had no idea. He only knew it was entirely true to his memory—that everything had happened exactly like this, and that once again, he was left behind, all alone, while every other boy had gone home for the joyous holidays.

He wasn't reading this time. He was pacing back and forth across the bare floorboards in despair. Scrooge looked at the Ghost, shook his head sorrowfully, and stared toward the doorway with anxious longing.

The door flew open. A little girl, much younger than the boy, came bursting into the room. She threw her arms around his neck, showering him with kisses and calling him her "Dear, dear brother!"

"I've come to bring you home, dear brother!" the child cried, clapping her small hands and bubbling over with laughter. "To bring you home, home, home!"

"Home, little Fan?" the boy asked in disbelief.

"Yes!" she beamed, radiating pure joy. "Home for good! Home forever and ever! Father is so much kinder than he used to be—home feels like Heaven now! He spoke so gently to me one night as I was heading to bed that I gathered up my courage to ask him one more time if you could come home. And he said yes, you could! He even sent me in a carriage to fetch you! And you're going to be a man now," she added, her eyes wide with excitement, "and you never have to come back to this awful place again! But first, we get to spend the whole Christmas season together and have the merriest time in the whole wide world!"

"You're quite a grown woman now, little Fan!" the boy laughed.

She clapped her hands and giggled, stretching up to ruffle his hair. Finding herself too short, she laughed again and stood on tiptoe to wrap him in another tight hug. Then, with eager, childish excitement, she began tugging him toward the door—and he, more than glad to leave, walked right alongside her.

A booming, dreadful voice bellowed from the hallway: "Bring down young Master Scrooge's trunk!"

There stood the headmaster himself. He glared down at young Ebenezer with terrifying condescension, rattling the boy's nerves simply by offering a stiff, chilling handshake. He then escorted Ebenezer and his sister into the freezing parlor—a frigid dungeon of a room where the wall maps and the world globes sitting by the windows looked frosted over with cold wax.

Here, the headmaster brought out a bottle of suspiciously thin, sour wine and a brick of astonishingly dense fruitcake, serving small helpings of these supposed treats to the children. Meanwhile, he sent a skinny house servant outside to offer a glass of "something" to the carriage driver. The driver replied that he was much obliged to the gentleman, but if it was from the same sour barrel he'd tasted last time, he'd rather pass!

With Master Scrooge's trunk now strapped securely to the carriage roof, the children said goodbye to the schoolmaster with genuine relief. They climbed aboard and rolled merrily down the winding driveway, the spinning carriage wheels sending showers of frost and snow spraying off the evergreen hedges like ocean surf.

"She was always a fragile soul—a strong wind might have carried her away," the Ghost remarked. "Yet she had an immense heart."

"She truly did," Scrooge agreed warmly. "You speak the truth, Spirit. I won't deny it—God forbid!"

"She grew into a woman before she passed away," said the Ghost, "and had children of her own, if I remember correctly."

"One child," Scrooge replied quietly.

"That's right," said the Ghost. "Your nephew."

A heavy pang of guilt washed over Scrooge. He answered in a strained whisper: "Yes."

Though they had only just stepped away from the old schoolhouse, they were suddenly standing in the bustling streets of a lively city. Ghostly figures hurried past in every direction; spectral wagons and carriages jostled for room on the cobblestones amid the clamor and energy of an active metropolis. The brightly decorated shop windows made it obvious that it was Christmas once again, but evening had fallen, and lanterns illuminated the streets.

The Ghost stopped outside the doorway of a large warehouse and asked Scrooge if he recognized the place.

"Recognize it?" Scrooge cried out. "I was an apprentice here!"

They stepped inside. Sitting behind a desk so ridiculously high that if he had been two inches taller he would have hit his head on the ceiling was a jolly older gentleman wearing a wool Welsh wig.

Scrooge beamed with excitement: "Why, it's old Fezziwig! Bless his heart—it's Fezziwig, alive and well!"

Old Fezziwig set down his quill and glanced up at the clock on the wall, which showed seven o'clock. He rubbed his hands together with relish, adjusted his roomy vest, and chuckled with his entire body, from the tips of his toes right up to his generous forehead. Then, in a warm, rich, booming, merry voice, he shouted:

"Yo ho, there! Ebenezer! Dick!"

Scrooge's younger self, now a spry young man, came trotting into the room, accompanied by his fellow apprentice.

"Dick Wilkins, of course!" Scrooge exclaimed to the Ghost. "Bless my soul, yes! There he is! Dick was so fond of me back then. Good old Dick! Ah, dear me!"

"Yo ho, my boys!" Fezziwig declared. "No more work tonight! It's Christmas Eve, Dick! It's Christmas, Ebenezer! Let's get the window shutters closed," cried old Fezziwig with a sharp clap of his hands, "before you can even say Jack Robinson!"

You wouldn't believe the speed of those two young fellows! They dashed out into the street with the heavy wooden shutters—one, two, three—slung them into place—four, five, six—locked and barred them tight—seven, eight, nine—and sprinted back inside before you could count to twelve, panting like racehorses!

"Hilli-ho!" cried old Fezziwig, hopping down from his towering desk with astonishing agility. "Clear the floor, boys! Let's make plenty of room! Come along, Dick! Cheer up, Ebenezer!"

Clear the floor? There wasn't an obstacle on earth they wouldn't have moved—or couldn't have moved—under old Fezziwig's cheerful supervision. Within sixty seconds, it was finished! Every crate and chair was whisked away as if packed off into permanent retirement. The floorboards were swept and lightly dampened, the oil lamps trimmed bright, fresh logs piled high onto the blazing fireplace, and presto! The drafty warehouse was transformed into the coziest, warmest, brightest ballroom you could ever hope to see on a chilly winter night.

In came a fiddler clutching a sheet-music book. He climbed right up behind the towering desk, turning it into his personal orchestra pit, and began tuning his violin with screeches that sounded like fifty stomachaches!

In swept Mrs. Fezziwig, one magnificent, beaming smile.

In bounced the three lovely Fezziwig daughters, radiant and charming.

In trailed the six hopeful young suitors whose hearts they were busy breaking.

In poured all the young clerks and shop hands working for the firm.

In came the housemaid, bringing along her cousin the baker.

In came the cook, accompanied by her brother's best buddy, the milkman.

In sneaked the apprentice boy from across the street, who everyone suspected wasn't getting enough to eat from his boss; he tried to duck out of sight behind the girl from down the block, whose ears had visibly been boxed by her grumpy mistress.

In they all came, one after another—some shy, some bold, some graceful, some clumsy, some nudging, some tugging—in they flooded, every which way!

And off they went, twenty couples hitting the floor at once! Hands across, circle half-round and back again; marching down the center and back to the top; spinning round and round in joyful clusters; the lead couple turning up in the wrong spot every time; the next couple darting ahead the moment they arrived; until eventually everyone was dancing as a lead couple with nobody left at the bottom to follow!

When the lively chaos finally tangled up, old Fezziwig clapped his hands, calling out, "Well done, everyone!"

The overheated fiddler immediately buried his face in a frothy mug of dark ale set out just for him. But refusing to rest, the moment he came up for air he started fiddling again—even though the dancers hadn't caught their breath yet—as if an imaginary rival fiddler had been carried off on a stretcher and he was determined to outplay him or drop dead trying!

There were more dances, and silly party games, and more dances again. There was sweet cake, and mugs of warm spiced wine, and a massive platter of cold roast beef, and another of cold boiled ham, along with spiced mince pies and pitchers of foaming ale.

Yet the crowning moment of the night arrived right after the feast. That clever rascal of a fiddler—a master of his craft who knew how to work a room better than anyone alive—struck up the opening notes of "Sir Roger de Coverley."

Old Fezziwig stepped right out into the middle of the floor to partner Mrs. Fezziwig! They took the lead spot as the head couple, and they had their work cut out for them—twenty-four eager couples lined up down the room, every one of them ready to dance their hearts out with no intention of just casually strolling through the steps!

Even if there had been twice as many dancers—no, four times as many!—old Fezziwig would have outdanced them all, and Mrs. Fezziwig right beside him. As for her, she was his perfect match in every possible way. If that isn't the highest praise, tell me a better compliment and I'll gladly use it!

A literal glow seemed to emanate from Fezziwig's calves. They flashed and shone through every spin and leap like twin full moons. You could never guess what fancy step his feet would pull off next.

Together, old Fezziwig and Mrs. Fezziwig breezed through the entire routine—advance and retreat, join hands with your partner, bow and curtsy, spin like a corkscrew, thread the needle, and skip right back to your spot! At the grand finish, Fezziwig performed a lightning-fast midair leg cross—so sharp and nimble that his legs seemed to give a cheeky wink—landing back on his feet without the slightest wobble!

When the clock struck eleven, the merry party drew to a close. Mr. and Mrs. Fezziwig stationed themselves on either side of the front door. They shook hands with each guest one by one as they bundled up and stepped out, wishing every single person a very Merry Christmas.

When everyone had left except the two apprentices, the Fezziwigs gave them the exact same warm handshake and holiday blessing. As the happy chatter faded into the night, the two young lads turned in to their own beds, tucked snugly under a work counter in the back of the shop.

Through the entire evening, Scrooge had behaved like a man possessed. His heart and soul were completely wrapped up in the scene, cheering along with his younger self. He verified every single detail, remembered every face, laughed along with every joke, and felt a strange, thrilling warmth swelling inside his chest.

It wasn't until young Ebenezer and Dick blew out their candles and went to sleep that Scrooge remembered the Ghost. He turned and realized the spirit was watching him intently, the bright beam of light atop its head blazing clear and pure.

"Such a tiny thing," the Ghost said casually, "to fill these foolish folks with so much gratitude."

"Tiny?" Scrooge echoed in disbelief.

The Spirit gestured for him to listen to the two apprentices under the counter, who were pouring their hearts out in praise of good old Fezziwig. Once Scrooge had listened, the Ghost spoke again:

"Well, isn't it? He spent barely a few pounds of your mortal money—maybe three or four. Is that really worth so much lavish praise?"

"It's not about the money!" Scrooge shot back defensively. Without realizing it, he was speaking from the heart of the young apprentice he used to be, not the bitter old miser he had become. "It's not that at all, Spirit! A boss has the power to make our lives happy or miserable—to make our work feel light as a feather or a crushing burden, a joy or pure drudgery! Even if that power only comes through kind words and welcoming smiles—little everyday things so small you can't measure or count them on a ledger—so what? The happiness he gives is worth just as much as if it had cost a king's fortune!"

He suddenly felt the Spirit's piercing gaze upon him, and stopped short.

"What is it?" the Ghost asked.

"Nothing in particular," Scrooge mumbled.

"I think there is something," the Ghost pressed gently.

"No," Scrooge said softly, looking away. "No. I... I just wish I could say a word or two to my clerk Bob right now. That's all."

As he spoke that wish aloud, his younger self extinguished the last lamp, and the warehouse dissolved away. Scrooge and the Ghost were standing side by side once more under the open sky.

"My time grows short," the Spirit noted quietly. "Hurry!"

The command wasn't addressed to Scrooge or anyone visible, yet its effect was immediate.

Once again, Scrooge saw himself. He was older now—a man in the prime of his life. His face hadn't yet set into the cruel, rigid scowl of his later years, but the earliest lines of worry and greed had begun to carve their way into his features. His eyes darted with a hungry, restless ambition that revealed the dark seed taking root in his soul, forecasting the deep shadow that would soon consume him.

He was not alone. He sat beside a lovely young woman dressed in simple mourning clothes. Tears glistened in her eyes, catching the pure light radiating from the Ghost of Christmas Past.

"It matters very little to you now," she said softly. "Hardly anything at all. Another idol has taken my place in your heart. And if it can comfort and sustain you in the years ahead as I once hoped to do, I have no right to complain."

"What idol has taken your place?" the younger Scrooge countered defensively.

"A golden one," she answered.

"That is the hypocritical way of the world!" he argued bitterly. "Society treats nothing more cruelly than poverty, yet nothing does it pretend to condemn more harshly than the pursuit of wealth!"

"You fear the world too much," she replied with gentle sadness. "All your other youthful dreams have melted away into a single obsession: shielding yourself from poverty and contempt. I have watched your nobler ideals wither and drop away, one by one, until a single drive—profit—has consumed you entirely. Isn't that the truth?"

"And so what if it is?" he snapped. "Even if I've grown wiser about the world, what difference does it make? My feelings toward you haven't changed."

She shook her head softly.

"Have they?" he challenged.

"Our promise to marry is an old one," she said. "We made it when we were both poor, and happy to remain so until our patient hard work could improve our fortunes. But you have changed. When you made that promise, you were a completely different man."

"I was just a kid then," he muttered impatiently.

"Your own conscience tells you that you weren't the man you are today," she answered. "I have stayed the same. What promised us a lifetime of happiness when we shared one heart will bring only misery now that our paths have split. How often and how painfully I have wept over this, I won't say. It is enough that I have thought it through, and that I can now set you free."

"Did I ever ask to be released?" he demanded.

"In words? No, never."

"Then in what way?"

"In your changed character. In your hardened spirit. In the selfish way you now live, and the cold ambition that drives you. In everything that once made my love mean anything to you. If we had never met until today," she said, looking straight into his eyes with steady sorrow, "tell me honestly: would you pursue me now? Would you try to win my heart today? Ah, no. You wouldn't."

He seemed to recognize the painful truth of her words, despite himself. Still, he forced out a weak reply: "You don't think so."

"Heaven knows I would love to believe otherwise if I could," she answered. "When the truth stares you in the face, you can't look away. If you were free today, could even I honestly believe that you would choose a penniless girl? You, who weigh every decision, even your closest confidences, on the scale of profit and loss? And if you did choose me in a momentary lapse from your golden rule, don't I know that bitter regret would follow soon after? I do know it. And so, I release you. I do it with all my heart, out of love for the man you used to be."

He opened his mouth to protest, but she turned her face away and continued gently:

"You may feel a sting of pain in this—and part of me, remembering the past, almost hopes you do. But it won't last long. Very soon, you will dismiss the memory of our love as a bad investment—an unprofitable daydream you were lucky to wake up from. May you find true happiness in the life you have chosen."

She stood up and walked away. And so they parted forever.

"Spirit!" Scrooge cried in anguish. "Show me no more! Take me home! Why do you take pleasure in torturing me?"

"One shadow more," the Ghost insisted.

"No more!" Scrooge pleaded, covering his eyes. "No more! I don't want to see it! Please, show me no more!"

But the relentless Spirit gripped him firmly by both arms and compelled him to witness what came next.

They stood in another room in another home. It wasn't large or lavish, but it was filled to the brim with warmth and cozy comfort.

Sitting beside the hearth was a lovely young woman, so strikingly like Belle that Scrooge was certain it was her—until he noticed Belle herself, now a beautiful, mature mother, sitting across from her daughter.

The room was in an uproar of delightful chaos. There were more children running around than Scrooge's frazzled mind could count. And unlike the calm cattle in the old poem that grazed as if forty were one, each of these children was carrying on as if they were forty kids packed into one body! The racket was wild beyond belief, but nobody minded in the least. In fact, both mother and daughter were laughing until their sides ached, thoroughly enjoying the commotion.

Soon the daughter jumped into the playful fray, only to be happily ambushed by the little bandits! What wouldn't I have given to be one of those rascals! Not that I could ever have been quite so rough—no, never! I wouldn't have mussed her lovely braided hair for all the money in the world. And as for pulling off her dainty shoe, bless my soul, I wouldn't have dared do that to save my neck! Nor could I have playfully wrapped my arms around her waist as that bold brood did; I would have feared my arm would freeze in a permanent circle as punishment and never straighten out again!

And yet, I admit, I would have loved beyond words to brush her cheek, to ask her a question just to hear her sweet voice answer, to gaze into her sparkling eyes without making her blush, or to catch a lock of her golden hair—a treasure more precious than any jewel. In short, I confess I would have given anything to share the innocent closeness of a child, while possessing the heart of a man who truly understood its worth.

Suddenly, a loud knock rattled the front door. A mad rush followed! The daughter, her hair mussed and dress pulled this way and that, was swept along like the queen of a wildly cheering parade right to the front hall.

There stood the father, freshly home from work, accompanied by a delivery man piled high with Christmas toys and wrapped packages.

Oh, the deafening cheers and wild scramble that followed! The poor delivery man was completely overwhelmed by the loving mob! The kids used chairs like stepladders to climb him, diving straight into his coat pockets, snatching brown-paper parcels, hanging from his necktie, wrapping their arms around his neck, patting his back, and kicking his shins in uncontainable affection!

Each unwrapped gift was greeted with gasps of awe and squeals of pure joy! Then came a sudden panic when someone shrieked that the baby had been caught trying to swallow a toy dollhouse frying pan, and was suspected of having eaten a fake roast turkey glued to a wooden plate! Then the massive wave of relief when it turned out to be a false alarm! The laughter, the gratitude, the pure holiday magic—words can't do it justice.

Eventually, one by one, the excited kids and their treasures were shepherded out of the parlor and marched up the stairs to bed, where the noisy house finally drifted into peaceful quiet.

Now Scrooge leaned forward, watching with an aching heart. The father sat down by the glowing fireplace, his loving daughter resting her head upon his shoulder while her mother sat close beside them.

As Scrooge looked upon the scene, the realization struck him that a daughter just as graceful, just as bright, and just as full of life might have called *him* father. She could have been the fresh springtime blossom in the lonely, barren winter of his old age. His vision blurred with thick tears.

"Belle," the husband said, turning toward his wife with a gentle smile, "I ran across an old friend of yours this afternoon."

"Oh? Who was it?"

"Take a guess!"

"How could I guess?" she teased, and then, catching the look in his eye, she laughed softly. "Wait... don't tell me. Mr. Scrooge?"

"Mr. Scrooge indeed. I walked past his office window. The shutters were open, and he had a lone candle burning inside, so I couldn't help seeing him. I hear his business partner is on his deathbed. And there Scrooge sat, all by himself. Utterly alone in the world, I truly believe."

"Spirit!" Scrooge pleaded in a broken, trembling voice. "Take me away from here!"

"I warned you these were shadows of what has been," the Ghost answered softly. "Do not blame me for what they are."

"Take me away!" Scrooge cried out, sobbing. "I cannot bear it!"

He turned upon the Ghost. As he looked into the Spirit's visage, he saw flickering reflections of every single face the ghost had shown him—his younger self, little Fan, Fezziwig, Belle. Desperate to escape the agonizing visions, he lunged and grappled with the spirit.

"Leave me alone! Take me back! Haunt me no more!"

It was hardly a fight—the Ghost offered no physical resistance whatsoever, remaining calm and completely unfazed by Scrooge's frantic efforts. But Scrooge noticed that the beacon of light atop the Spirit's head was blazing with blinding brilliance. Sensing instinctively that this light was the source of the ghost's uncanny power over him, Scrooge grabbed the metal extinguisher cap. With a desperate heave, he slammed it down over the Spirit's head.

The Ghost sank down beneath the cap until the metal cone covered its entire body. Yet no matter how hard Scrooge threw his weight onto the extinguisher, pressing down with every ounce of strength he possessed, he could not snuff out the light. It poured out from beneath the rim in an unbroken, dazzling flood that spilled across the floor.

An overwhelming wave of exhaustion washed over him, pulling him down into an irresistible, heavy drowsiness. He realized he was back in his own bedchamber. His grip relaxed on the cap. He gave it one last weak press, staggered blindly toward his bed, and collapsed onto the mattress, instantly plunging into a deep, dreamless sleep.

# Chapter 3: The Second of the Three Spirits

Waking right in the middle of a prodigiously loud snore and sitting up in bed to gather his thoughts, Scrooge didn't need anyone to tell him that the clock was about to strike one. He sensed he had regained consciousness in the nick of time, specifically to meet the second messenger dispatched through Jacob Marley's intervention. But when he started wondering which bed curtain this new phantom would pull back, an uncomfortable chill ran down his spine. So he pushed every single curtain aside with his own hands, lay back down, and set up a sharp lookout all around the bed. He wanted to confront the Spirit the very instant it appeared, refusing to be caught off guard and rattled.

Carefree, swaggering gentlemen who pride themselves on being street-smart and up for anything often express their appetite for adventure by claiming they're game for anything from a coin toss to manslaughter—extremes between which, no doubt, lies a comfortably wide territory. Without making quite such a bold claim for Scrooge, I don't hesitate to ask you to believe that he was ready for a broad variety of strange spectacles, and that nothing from a newborn baby to a rhinoceros would have surprised him all that much.

Yet, while he was prepared for almost anything, he was not at all prepared for nothing. Consequently, when the bell struck one and no ghostly shape appeared, he began to tremble violently. Five minutes, ten minutes, a quarter of an hour slipped by, and still nothing showed up. All this time he lay in bed, right at the glowing heart of a blaze of reddish light that had flooded over him the moment the clock chimed the hour. Being merely light, it was somehow more alarming than a dozen ghosts, since he couldn't figure out what it meant or what it was doing. At moments, he even worried he might be an intriguing case of spontaneous human combustion, without even having the comfort of knowing it. At last, however, he started thinking—just as you or I would have thought right from the start, for the person sitting comfortably outside a crisis always knows exactly what should have been done and would certainly have done it—at last, as I said, he began to suspect that the source of this eerie glow lay in the adjoining room, which was where the light seemed to be streaming from. Once this thought took hold, he got up quietly and shuffled in his slippers to the door.

The moment Scrooge's hand touched the handle, an unfamiliar voice called his name and told him to enter. He obeyed.

It was his own room—there was no doubt about that. But it had undergone an astonishing transformation. The walls and ceiling were draped in living greenery, turning it into a lush woodland grove, studded everywhere with bright, gleaming berries. Crisp leaves of holly, mistletoe, and ivy reflected the light like hundreds of tiny mirrors. A roaring fire blazed in the chimney, the likes of which that cold, stony hearth had never seen in Scrooge's time, or Marley's, or across countless winters gone by. Piled high on the floor like a magnificent throne was an incredible feast: turkeys, geese, wild game, poultry, cured meats, massive roasts, suckling pigs, long garlands of sausages, mince pies, plum puddings, barrels of oysters, piping-hot chestnuts, rosy-cheeked apples, juicy oranges, luscious pears, towering holiday cakes, and steaming bowls of punch that misted the room with a delicious aroma. Lounging comfortably on this feast was a jolly Giant, glorious to behold. He held a glowing torch shaped like a horn of plenty, raising it high to shine down on Scrooge as the old man peeked around the door.

"Come in!" exclaimed the Ghost. "Come in, and get to know me better, man!"

Scrooge stepped inside timidly and bowed his head before the Spirit. He was no longer the stubborn, obstinate Scrooge he used to be, and though the Spirit’s eyes were clear and kind, he hesitated to look directly into them.

"I am the Ghost of Christmas Present," said the Spirit. "Look at me!"

Scrooge looked up with deep respect. The Spirit wore a simple, deep-green robe bordered with white fur. The garment hung so loosely around him that his broad chest was bare, as if refusing to hide behind any armor or disguise. His bare feet peeked out from beneath the robe's generous folds, and upon his head rested only a simple wreath of holly, sparkling here and there with gleaming icicles. His long, dark brown curls flowed freely—as free and open as his genial face, his sparkling eyes, his welcoming hands, his cheery voice, and his joyful, uninhibited manner. Buckled around his waist was an antique scabbard, but there was no sword inside, and the ancient sheath was rusted through.

"You've never seen anyone like me before!" the Spirit exclaimed.

"Never," Scrooge answered.

"And you have never walked with the younger members of my family—by which I mean, since I am very young, my elder brothers born in recent years?" the phantom pressed.

"I don't think I have," said Scrooge. "I'm afraid not. Have you had many brothers, Spirit?"

"More than eighteen hundred," said the Ghost.

"What a tremendous family to provide for!" muttered Scrooge.

The Ghost of Christmas Present stood up.

"Spirit," Scrooge said submissively, "take me wherever you wish. Last night I was taken against my will, but I learned a lesson that is already changing me. Tonight, if you have anything to teach me, let me learn from it."

"Touch my robe!"

Scrooge did as he was told, gripping it tightly.

Instantly, the holly, mistletoe, red berries, ivy, turkeys, geese, game, roasts, pigs, sausages, oysters, pies, puddings, fruit, and punch all vanished. Gone too were the room, the fire, the warm glow, and the midnight hour. Instead, they stood right in the city streets on Christmas morning. The weather was biting cold, and the townsfolk created a rough, brisk, cheerful sort of music by shoveling snow off the sidewalks in front of their doorsteps and scraping it from their rooftops. Neighborhood boys were thrilled to watch great clumps plunge onto the street below, bursting into miniature artificial blizzards.

The building facades looked dark, and the windows even darker, standing out against the smooth blankets of white snow on the roofs and the slushy, grimy snow down on the ground. Below, heavy cart and wagon wheels had plowed the snow into deep ruts that crisscrossed hundreds of times at major intersections, turning into tangled channels of thick yellowish mud and freezing water. The sky hung gray and gloomy, and the narrower side streets were choked with a murky mist—half-thawed, half-frozen—whose heavier particles drifted down like soot, as though every chimney across Britain had agreed to catch fire at once and blaze to its heart's content. There was certainly nothing cheerful about the weather or the town, yet an undeniable spirit of joy filled the air, the kind that even the brightest summer sun and clearest blue skies could never quite match.

The folks shoveling snow from the rooftops were hearty and full of cheer, shouting out to one another across the gutters and playfully hurling snowballs—far friendlier missiles than many a biting joke—laughing just as hard whether their aim was true or wildly off. The poultry shops were still half-open, and the produce markets were radiant in their festive glory. Big, round, pot-bellied baskets of chestnuts, shaped like the vests of jolly old gentlemen, sprawled by the doorways and spilled out onto the sidewalk in bursting abundance. Plump, brown, wide-girthed Spanish onions shone with rich ripeness like cheerful monks, winking cheekily from the shelves at passing girls who glanced shyly at the sprigs of mistletoe hanging overhead. Pears and apples were stacked high in mouthwatering pyramids. Bunches of grapes hung dangling from prominent hooks through the grocers' good graces, making passersby drool free of charge. Piles of brown, earthy hazelnuts gave off a scent that stirred memories of forest walks and kicking ankle-deep through fallen autumn leaves. Squat, dark-red baked apples stood out against the bright yellow of oranges and lemons, begging so earnestly in their plump juiciness to be carried home in paper sacks for dessert. Even the goldfish and silver fish swimming in bowls among the fruit displays, cold-blooded and sluggish by nature, seemed to sense the excitement, swimming round and round their little glass bowls in slow, calm fascination.

And the grocery shops! Oh, the grocery shops! They were almost closed, with only a shutter or two left open, but what wonderful sights peeked through the gaps! It wasn't just the merry clinking of brass scales hitting the counter, or the rapid snap of twine unrolling from its spool, or the spice tins rattling in rhythmic tricks like a juggler's props. It wasn't even the rich aroma of tea and coffee blending in the air, or the abundant raisins, the snow-white almonds, the long, straight cinnamon sticks, the fragrant spices, or the candied fruits so glazed and crusted with sugar that they made passersby dizzy with craving. Nor was it simply that the figs were plump and juicy, or that the French plums blushed from their decorative boxes, or that every single treat looked dressed for the holiday. It was the shoppers themselves—everyone was in such a delightful hurry, buzzing with anticipation, bumping into one another at the entrance with a clatter of wicker baskets, leaving their bags on the counters and dashing back to grab them, making a hundred harmless blunders in the best spirits imaginable. Meanwhile, the grocer and his staff were so warm and genuine that the shiny metal clasps fastening their aprons behind their backs might as well have been their own open hearts, displayed for the whole world to see without a single care.

Soon, church bells began calling everyone to worship, and families came flocking through the streets dressed in their Sunday best, their faces beaming. At the very same moment, out from countless side alleys, narrow lanes, and unnamed courts came crowds of working-class folks carrying their prepared holiday meals to the local bakeries to be roasted in the big ovens. This sight seemed to fascinate the Spirit. Standing with Scrooge beside a baker's doorway, he lifted the covers as people carried their pots and pans past, sprinkling incense over their dinners from his torch. It was no ordinary torch either: once or twice, when people bumped into each other and began to exchange sharp words, the Spirit flicked a few drops of moisture over them from the torch, and their smiles returned instantly. They reminded themselves that it was a crying shame to quarrel on Christmas Day. And indeed it was! God bless them, it truly was!

Eventually the church bells fell silent and the bakeries closed their doors. Yet a delicious preview of all those roasting feasts lingered on in the thawed, steamy patch of sidewalk directly above each baker’s basement oven, where the street pavers smoked as if the cobblestones themselves were being baked.

"Is there a special flavor in what you sprinkle from your torch?" Scrooge asked.

"There is. My own."

"Does it work on any meal today?" Scrooge asked.

"On any meal offered with kindness. Most of all, on a poor family's dinner."

"Why most of all on a poor one?" asked Scrooge.

"Because it needs it most."

"Spirit," Scrooge said after thinking for a moment, "it surprises me that someone like you, of all beings, would want to restrict these people's simple pleasures."

"Me?" cried the Spirit.

"You'd take away their only chance to cook a hot meal every seventh day—often the only day they can afford to eat well at all," said Scrooge. "Wouldn't you?"

"Me?" cried the Spirit.

"You want to shut these bakeries on the Sabbath," Scrooge said. "And that amounts to the same thing."

"I want that?" exclaimed the Spirit.

"Forgive me if I'm mistaken, but it's done in your name, or at least in the name of your family," Scrooge replied.

"There are people on this earth of yours," the Spirit answered, "who claim to speak for us, yet carry out acts of cruelty, pride, malice, hatred, envy, bigotry, and selfishness in our name! They are as foreign to us and our kin as if they had never existed. Remember that, and blame their deeds on them, not on us."

Scrooge promised he would keep that in mind, and the two walked on, invisible as before, out toward the city outskirts. A remarkable thing about the Ghost—which Scrooge had already noticed at the bakery—was that despite his massive size, he could easily fit into any space. He stood beneath a low ceiling with the same natural grace and majestic presence he would have shown in a towering palace hall.

Perhaps the good Spirit took joy in demonstrating this power, or perhaps it was simply his generous, loving nature and deep empathy for the poor that guided him straight to the home of Scrooge’s clerk. For there he went, carrying Scrooge along as the old man held tightly to his robe. Pausing at the doorstep, the Spirit smiled and blessed Bob Cratchit’s modest home with a sprinkle from his torch. Just imagine: Bob earned only fifteen shillings a week—taking home barely fifteen coins sharing his own nickname every Saturday—yet the Ghost of Christmas Present blessed his humble four-room house!

Inside, Mrs. Cratchit, Bob's wife, was already on her feet. She wore a modest dress that had been turned inside out and resewn twice to hide the wear, but she was adorned bravely with bright ribbons—which cost only pennies yet make a cheerful display. She spread the tablecloth with help from Belinda, her second daughter, who was likewise decked out in proud ribbons. Young Peter Cratchit poked a fork into the pot of boiling potatoes, accidentally getting the stiff corners of his enormous shirt collar into his mouth. The oversized collar was Bob's own property, loaned to his son and heir in honor of the holiday, and Peter felt so dashingly dressed that he dreamed of showing off his fine shirt in the city's fashionable parks. Just then, the two youngest Cratchits, a boy and a girl, came bursting in, shrieking with excitement that they had caught the scent of the roast goose outside the bakery and knew instantly it was theirs! Lost in glorious daydreams of sage and onion stuffing, the little Cratchits danced around the table, praising Peter to the skies. Peter—humble despite the collar that nearly strangled him—stoked the fire until the boiling potatoes began to rattle the pot lid, banging to be taken out and peeled.

"Whatever is keeping your dear father?" Mrs. Cratchit said. "And your brother Tiny Tim! And Martha wasn't this late last Christmas by half an hour!"

"Here I am, Mother!" said a young woman, walking through the door.

"Here's Martha, Mother!" yelled the two little Cratchits. "Hurrah! Martha, you should see the goose!"

"Bless your heart, my darling, you're so late!" cried Mrs. Cratchit, showering her with hugs and kisses and bustling about to help her off with her shawl and bonnet.

"We had so much work to finish up late last night," Martha explained, "and we had to clean up the shop this morning, Mother!"

"Well, never mind now that you're here!" said Mrs. Cratchit. "Sit right down by the fire, dear, and get warm, bless your heart!"

"No, no! Father's coming!" cried the two little Cratchits, darting everywhere at once. "Hide, Martha, hide!"

So Martha ducked out of sight just as little Bob, the father, walked in. A long woolen scarf hung down past his knees, its fringe dangling toward the floor. His worn, threadbare coat was brushed and patched to look as neat as possible for the holiday, and Tiny Tim was perched upon his shoulder. Poor Tiny Tim carried a small crutch, and his fragile legs were supported by an iron frame.

"Why, where's our Martha?" Bob called out, looking around the room.

"She's not coming," said Mrs. Cratchit.

"Not coming?" repeated Bob, his high spirits instantly crashing. He had played racehorse carrying Tim on his shoulders all the way from church, galloping home full of cheer. "Not coming on Christmas Day!"

Martha couldn't bear to see him heartbroken, even for a moment's prank. She popped out from behind the pantry door and ran straight into his open arms. Meanwhile, the two little Cratchits whisked Tiny Tim away to the washhouse so he could listen to the Christmas pudding bubbling and singing in the boiler.

"And how did little Tim behave?" asked Mrs. Cratchit, once she had teased Bob for falling for the joke and Bob had embraced his daughter to his heart’s content.

"As good as gold," Bob answered, "and even better. Spending so much quiet time sitting by himself makes him thoughtful, and he comes up with the sweetest ideas you've ever heard. On the walk home, he told me he hoped the congregation saw him at church, because being disabled, it might remind people on Christmas Day of the One who made lame beggars walk and gave sight to the blind."

Bob’s voice wavered as he shared this, and it trembled all the more when he insisted that Tiny Tim was growing stronger and healthier every day.

The tap-tap of a little crutch echoed across the floorboards, and back came Tiny Tim before another word was spoken. His brother and sister guided him proudly to his stool by the warm hearth. Bob rolled up his cuffs—as though the poor fellow's frayed sleeves could look any more worn than they already were—and mixed up a hot spiced drink in a pitcher with gin and lemons, stirring it well before setting it on the stove ledge to simmer. Meanwhile, Peter and the two energetic little Cratchits hurried off to the bakery to fetch the goose, returning shortly in a grand, triumphant parade.

Such excitement broke out that you'd think a roast goose was the rarest bird on earth—a miraculous creature compared to which even a black swan seemed ordinary. And truthfully, in that house, it was pretty close to a miracle! Mrs. Cratchit got the gravy hissing hot in a saucepan; Peter mashed the potatoes with extraordinary muscle; Belinda stirred extra sweetness into the applesauce; Martha warmed and dusted the serving plates; Bob pulled Tiny Tim close beside him at a cozy corner of the table; and the two younger Cratchits set chairs for everyone, making sure not to leave themselves out. Taking their stations like guards, the two youngsters shoved spoons into their mouths so they wouldn't scream for goose before being served. Finally, the platters were set down, and they said grace. A breathless hush followed. Mrs. Cratchit slowly ran her eyes along the edge of the carving knife, poised to plunge it into the bird's breast. When she sliced in, and a savory wave of fragrant stuffing burst forth, a collective cheer of delight swept around the table. Tiny Tim, egged on by the younger two, banged the table with the wooden handle of his knife and cheered a frail, joyful, "Hurrah!"

There was never a goose like it. Bob declared that he didn't believe a finer bird had ever been roasted. Its tenderness, flavor, size, and bargain price were admired by everyone at the table. Stretched out with plenty of applesauce and mashed potatoes, it made a complete and satisfying feast for the whole family. In fact, as Mrs. Cratchit pointed out with immense pride while eyeing a single tiny scrap of bone left on the platter, they hadn't even eaten every last bit! Everyone was completely full, and the two youngest Cratchits were stuffed with sage and onion right up to their eyebrows. With Belinda clearing the dinner plates, Mrs. Cratchit slipped out of the room by herself—far too nervous to let anyone watch—to unmold the Christmas pudding and carry it in.

What if it wasn't cooked through? What if it cracked when she turned it out of the bowl? What if an intruder had climbed the backyard wall and snatched it while they were busy eating the goose?—a horrifying thought that made the two youngest Cratchits turn pale with terror! Every imaginable disaster was feared.

Look out! Clouds of steam poured from the doorway! The pudding was out of the boiler! A scent like laundry day wafted in—that was the boiling cloth. Then came an aroma like a diner and a pastry shop side by side, with a laundromat right next door—that was the pudding itself! Within thirty seconds, Mrs. Cratchit reentered the room. Her cheeks were flushed, but she was smiling proudly, carrying the pudding like a speckled cannonball—dense and firm, blazing with glowing blue flames from a splash of lit brandy, and crowned with a cheerful sprig of Christmas holly right on top.

Oh, what a wonderful pudding! Bob Cratchit announced, with absolute sincerity, that he considered it the greatest achievement of Mrs. Cratchit's entire married life. Mrs. Cratchit admitted that now that the pressure was off, she'd had secret worries about whether she had used enough flour. Everyone heaped praise upon it, and nobody said—or even thought—that it was a small pudding for such a big family. To suggest such a thing would have been outright treason. Any Cratchit would have died of embarrassment before hinting at it.

At last the feast was finished. The tablecloth was cleared away, the hearth swept clean, and the fire stoked up. Bob sampled the warm spiced punch in the pitcher, pronounced it absolute perfection, and set out apples and oranges on the table while shoveling fresh chestnuts onto the coals. Then the entire Cratchit family gathered around the hearth in what Bob called a circle—though it was really a semicircle. Right beside Bob's elbow sat the family's finest glassware: two everyday tumblers and a little custard cup missing its handle.

Yet these simple cups held the steaming drink just as sweetly as golden goblets could have done. Bob poured with a radiant smile while the chestnuts hissed and popped over the fire. Raising his glass, Bob toasted:

"A Merry Christmas to us all, my dears! God bless us!"

The whole family echoed his toast with joy.

"God bless us, every one!" said Tiny Tim, the last of all.

He sat right beside his father on his little stool. Bob cradled the boy's frail, thin hand in his own, as if he loved him with all his soul, yearning to keep him close and terrified that he might soon be taken away.

"Spirit," Scrooge said, feeling a compassion he had never known before, "tell me if Tiny Tim will live."

"I see an empty chair," the Ghost answered, "in that humble chimney corner, and a crutch kept with loving care, but with no owner to hold it. If these shadows remain unchanged by the Future, the child will die."

"No, no!" cried Scrooge. "Oh no, kind Spirit! Tell me he'll be spared!"

"If these shadows remain unchanged by the Future, none of my kind will ever find him here again," the Ghost replied. "What of it? If he is likely to die, he had better do it, and decrease the surplus population!"

Scrooge hung his head, devastated to hear his own cruel words thrown back at him, overcome with remorse and grief.

"Man," the Ghost rebuked him, "if you truly have a heart of flesh and not cold stone, stop repeating that wicked nonsense until you learn what this 'surplus' really is and where to find it! Who are you to decide who should live and who should die? In the eyes of Heaven, you may be far less worthy of life than millions just like this poor man's child! Good heavens! To hear an insect perched on a leaf lecturing on the 'excess population' of its starving brothers down in the dirt!"

Scrooge bowed beneath the Ghost's scolding, his eyes fixed trembling on the floor. But he quickly looked up when he heard his own name.

"To Mr. Scrooge!" Bob announced, raising his glass. "I give you Mr. Scrooge, the Founder of our Feast!"

"The Founder of the Feast, indeed!" Mrs. Cratchit scoffed, her face turning red. "I only wish I had him here right now! I'd give him a piece of my mind to feast on, and I certainly hope he'd have the stomach for it!"

"My dear," pleaded Bob gently, "remember the children! It's Christmas Day."

"It would take Christmas Day to make anyone drink to the health of an odious, stingy, hard-hearted, unfeeling man like Mr. Scrooge!" she retorted. "You know very well he is, Robert! Nobody knows it better than you, my poor husband!"

"My love," Bob said softly, "it's Christmas Day."

"I'll drink to his health for your sake and for the holiday," said Mrs. Cratchit, "but certainly not for his! Long life to him! A merry Christmas and a happy New Year! I'm sure he'll be positively thrilled and overjoyed!"

The children drank the toast after her, but for the first time all day, there was no joy in it. Tiny Tim drank last of all, though he couldn't have cared less. Scrooge was the bogeyman of the family. The mere sound of his name cast a dark chill over the gathering that didn't lift for five full minutes.

Once the shadow passed, however, they were ten times happier than before, purely out of relief that they were done talking about Scrooge the Terrible. Bob told the family about a job lead he had found for Peter, which would bring in a handsome five shillings and sixpence a week if he landed it. The two young Cratchits burst into giggles at the thought of Peter becoming a businessman, while Peter stared thoughtfully into the fire from between his oversized collars, looking for all the world like a mogul deciding which big investments to make once that staggering fortune rolled in. Martha, who worked as an apprentice at a hat shop, described the endless hours of sewing she had to do and how she planned to sleep in late tomorrow morning for a well-deserved rest, since tomorrow was a day off she could spend at home. She also shared how she had spotted a countess and a lord a few days earlier, and that the lord was "about the same height as Peter"—at which Peter yanked his collars so high that you wouldn't have been able to see his head at all. All this time, the warm punch and roasted chestnuts made their rounds. Soon, Tiny Tim sang a sweet, touching song about a child lost in the snow; his little voice was clear and plaintive, and he sang it beautifully.

There was nothing fancy or grand about any of this. They weren't an exceptionally handsome family, and they were certainly poorly dressed. Their shoes leaked in the rain, their clothing was threadbare, and Peter almost certainly knew the way to the pawnshop. But they were joyful, deeply thankful, devoted to one another, and content with what little they had. As their home began to fade—looking all the brighter under a parting shower of sparkles from the Spirit’s torch—Scrooge kept his eyes glued to them, watching Tiny Tim until they disappeared completely.

By now dusk had settled, and the snow was falling heavily. As Scrooge and the Spirit moved through the streets, the glow of roaring fires in kitchens, living rooms, and parlors shone brilliantly. Through one window, flickering flames revealed preparations for an intimate dinner, with plates warming before the hearth and deep red curtains ready to be pulled against the freezing dark. In another yard, children came running out into the snow to greet married siblings, cousins, aunts, and uncles, competing to be the first to welcome them with open arms. Across the street, silhouettes of arriving guests danced against frosted window blinds. Down the path, a lively group of young women in warm hoods and fur-lined boots hurried along, talking and laughing all at once on their way to a neighbor’s party—and heaven help any bachelor who saw them arrive, glowing and charming, well aware of the spell they cast!

If you had judged by the sheer number of folks hurrying off to parties, you might have worried that no one was left at home to greet them—when in fact every single house was eagerly awaiting guests and piling its fireplace high with logs. Bless its heart, how the Spirit rejoiced! He opened his arms and spread his wide hands, gliding forward and showering bright, pure joy over everything around him. Even the lamplighter, running ahead with his ladder to dot the evening twilight with sparks of streetlamp light—dressed up for a holiday party of his own—burst out laughing as the Spirit drifted by, with no idea he was brushing shoulders with Christmas itself!

Suddenly, without a single word of warning, they were standing on a bleak, desolate moor. Enormous boulders lay scattered across the wasteland as though it were a graveyard for ancient giants. Water pooled everywhere it could, frozen solid by the bitter frost, and nothing grew except patches of moss, gorse, and stiff wild grass. To the west, the setting sun left a streak of fiery crimson that glared over the wasteland like a brooding eye before sinking lower and lower, finally vanishing into the pitch-black night.

"Where are we?" asked Scrooge.

"A place where miners live, men who labor deep in the bowels of the earth," the Spirit replied. "Yet they know me. Look!"

A light flickered from the window of a small hut, and they moved swiftly toward it. Passing effortlessly through the stone and mud wall, they found a cheerful family gathered around a crackling hearth. An elderly great-grandfather and great-grandmother sat surrounded by their children, grandchildren, and great-grandchildren, all dressed up in colorful holiday clothes. The old man, his voice barely louder than the howling wind outside, was singing an ancient Christmas carol—one that had been old even when he was a boy—and every few verses the whole family joined in the chorus. Whenever their voices swelled, the old man grew lively and strong; whenever they paused, his energy faded back into frail old age.

The Spirit did not linger long. Telling Scrooge to grip his robe, he swept up into the air above the moor and sped away—where? Surely not out to sea? Yes, out to sea! To Scrooge's sheer terror, when he looked back, the last sliver of land—a jagged reef of dangerous cliffs—was receding behind them. His ears roared with the thunderous crash of the ocean, raging against sea-carved caverns as if trying to tear the earth down into the deep.

Perched on a treacherous reef a few miles off the coast, where waves battered and smashed against the stone all year long, stood a lonely lighthouse. Massive mats of seaweed clung to its foundation, and sea birds—seeming born of the wind just as the kelp was born of the sea—wheeled and dove around the tower like the surging waves below.

Yet even out here, the two lightkeepers had built a cozy fire that beamed through a slit in the thick stone wall, casting a ray of warmth out across the churning ocean. Grasping their calloused hands across a weathered table, they clinked their mugs of rum and wished each other a Merry Christmas. Then the older keeper—his face weathered and scarred by sea storms like the carved figurehead of a wooden ship—struck up a booming sea shanty that sounded like a gale all on its own.

Onward the Ghost flew across the pitch-black, pitching sea—on and on—until, far from any coast, they touched down on a ship. They stood beside the helmsman at the steering wheel, the lookout stationed at the bow, and the watch officers on duty. Dark, shadowy figures at their posts, yet each and every man hummed a Christmas carol, harbored a warm holiday thought, or whispered memories of Christmases past to his crewmate, dreaming of returning home. Every soul aboard, whether awake or sleeping, good or flawed, spoke with greater kindness that day than any other day of the year. Each shared in the holiday cheer, thought lovingly of family far away across the sea, and knew that their loved ones were thinking of them in turn.

It was an incredible shock to Scrooge, while listening to the wailing wind and pondering how chilling it was to drift through the black void above an endless ocean as deep and mysterious as death itself, suddenly to hear a booming burst of laughter! It was an even greater shock to recognize that laughter as belonging to his own nephew Fred—and to find himself standing in a bright, dry, glowing living room, with the Spirit smiling beside him and watching Fred with delighted approval!

"Ha, ha!" Fred roared with laughter. "Ha, ha, ha!"

If you happen, by some slim chance, to know anyone blessed with a better laugh than Scrooge's nephew, all I can say is that I’d love to meet him. Introduce us, and I’ll gladly become his best friend.

It is one of life’s most wonderful, fair-minded truths that while sickness and sadness can be contagious, absolutely nothing on earth spreads faster or more irresistibly than genuine laughter and good cheer. When Scrooge’s nephew laughed like this—holding his sides, shaking his head, his face contorting into hilarious expressions—his wife laughed just as hard. And their circle of friends, refusing to be left behind, joined right in, roaring with delight:

"Ha, ha! Ha, ha, ha, ha!"

"As I live and breathe, he said Christmas was a humbug!" Fred cried. "And what's more, he actually meant it!"

"Shame on him, Fred!" his wife said indignantly. Bless women like her; they never do anything halfway. They mean every word they say.

She was very pretty—extraordinarily pretty. She had a dimpled, wide-eyed, delightful face, a sweet mouth clearly made for kissing (and undoubtedly kissed often), charming little beauty marks on her chin that crinkled together when she laughed, and the sparkliest, sunniest eyes you could ever hope to see. Altogether, she was playful, teasing, and utterly enchanting. Truly, completely captivating.

"He's a funny old guy, that's the honest truth," Fred said, "and certainly not as pleasant as he could be. Still, his bad habits are their own punishment, so I have no real grudge against him."

"I know he's very wealthy, Fred," his wife pointed out. "At least, that's what you always tell me."

"And what good does that do him, my dear?" Fred replied. "His money is totally useless to him. He doesn't do any good with it, he doesn't use it to make his own life comfortable, and he doesn't even have the joy of thinking—ha, ha, ha!—that he’s ever going to leave any of it to us!"

"I have zero patience for him," Fred's wife remarked, and her sisters and the other ladies in the room completely agreed.

"Oh, I do!" Fred said. "I feel sorry for him. I couldn't stay angry at him if I tried. Who suffers from his grumpy moods? He does, every single time! Look, he gets it into his head to dislike us, so he refuses to come have Christmas dinner with us. What's the result? Well, he doesn't miss out on much of a meal."

"Actually, he missed out on a wonderful dinner!" his wife chimed in. Everyone else immediately agreed, and they were certainly qualified to judge, having just eaten it themselves and now sitting around the hearth by lamplight with dessert on the table.

"Well, I'm delighted to hear that," Fred laughed, "because I never place too much trust in these new young homemakers! What do you say, Topper?"

Topper clearly had a crush on one of Fred's sisters-in-law, because he answered that an unmarried bachelor was a pathetic outcast who had no right to have an opinion on home cooking. At that, the sister-in-law—the curvy one wearing the lace collar, not the one with the roses in her hair—blushed bright red.

"Do go on, Fred!" his wife urged, clapping her hands. "He never finishes a story! He's utterly ridiculous!"

Fred burst into another fit of laughter, and because his joy was so contagious—even though the blushing sister tried to hide her giggles behind a scented handkerchief—the entire room dissolved into laughter with him.

"All I was going to say," Fred explained, "is that the only result of his disliking us and refusing to celebrate is that he misses out on some happy moments, which would do him a world of good. He certainly loses out on better company than he'll ever find in his own bitter thoughts, trapped in his dreary old office or his dusty rooms. I plan to give him the exact same invitation every single year, whether he likes it or not, because I truly feel for him. He can grumble about Christmas until his dying day, but he won't be able to ignore it if I show up year after year with a cheerful smile, saying, 'Uncle Scrooge, how are you?' If it softens him enough to leave fifty pounds to his poor clerk, that's already something! And I think I made a bit of an impression on him yesterday."

Everyone laughed at the idea of Fred rattling Scrooge. But being thoroughly good-natured and simply wanting everyone to be happy, Fred laughed right along with them and cheerfully poured more drinks.

After tea, they enjoyed some music. They were a talented family, and when they harmonized on classic tunes, they really knew what they were doing—especially Topper, who could rumble out rich bass notes effortlessly, without breaking a sweat or turning red in the face. Fred's wife played beautifully on the harp, and among the songs she played was a simple little melody—a sweet little tune you could learn to whistle in two minutes. It was the very same song Scrooge's little sister had loved when she came to bring him home from boarding school, just as the Ghost of Christmas Past had shown him. As the delicate notes drifted through the room, everything the first Spirit had shown him came flooding back. Scrooge's heart softened deeper and deeper. He realized that if he had only listened to music like that more often over the years, he might have nurtured life’s simple kindnesses for his own happiness with his own hands, instead of waiting for the grave-digger's spade that buried Jacob Marley to remind him of mortality.

They didn't spend the whole evening just singing, though. Before long they moved on to party games. It does people good to act like children sometimes, and never more so than at Christmas, when its great Founder was a child Himself. Wait—first they played blindman's buff! Of course they did. And I don't believe for a single second that Topper was genuinely blindfolded, any more than I believe he had eyes in his boots. In my view, it was all arranged between him and Fred, and the Ghost of Christmas Present knew it too! The way Topper chased after that pretty, curvy sister in the lace collar was an absolute insult to common sense! Knocking over fireplace tools, tripping over chairs, bumping into the piano, tangling himself in window drapes—wherever she ran, there he went! He always knew exactly where she was. He stubbornly refused to tag anyone else. If someone deliberately bumped into him (as several people did), he made a ridiculous half-hearted grab that fooled nobody, then immediately veered back toward the curvy sister. She shrieked repeatedly that it wasn't fair—and it certainly wasn't! But when he finally trapped her in a corner with no way out, despite all her rustling silk skirts and quick dodges, his behavior was delightfully shameless! He pretended he didn't know who she was, insisting he had to touch her hair ribbons, inspect a specific ring on her finger, and feel a particular necklace around her throat just to confirm her identity! Outrageous! Of course, she gave him a piece of her mind later when someone else took a turn wearing the blindfold—while the two of them whispered secrets together behind the drapes.

Fred's wife didn't join the blindman's buff, resting comfortably in a plush armchair with a footstool in a cozy corner, where the Ghost and Scrooge stood right behind her. But she enthusiastically joined in the guessing games, reciting word puzzles through every letter of the alphabet with flying colors. She was fantastic at the question-and-answer games too, thoroughly beating her sisters—much to Fred's delight, even though her sisters were plenty quick-witted themselves, as Topper could attest. There were about twenty guests, young and old, and every single one played—including Scrooge! Caught up in the fun and completely forgetting that they couldn't hear him, Scrooge shouted his guesses out loud, and more often than not, he guessed correctly! For all his stubborn bluntness, Scrooge was still as sharp as a tack.

The Ghost was thrilled to see Scrooge enjoying himself like this, looking on him with such warmth that Scrooge pleaded like an excited schoolboy to stay until the guests left. But the Spirit told him that wasn't possible.

"Wait, here's a new game!" Scrooge begged. "Just thirty more minutes, Spirit—only half an hour!"

It was a game called "Yes and No," where Fred had to think of something and everyone else had to guess what it was by asking questions that he could only answer with yes or no. A rapid fire of questions revealed that he was thinking of an animal—a living animal, a rather unpleasant animal, a savage animal, one that growled and grunted, but also talked! It lived in London, roamed the city streets, wasn't put on display in a circus, wasn't led around on a leash, didn't live in a zoo, was never butchered for meat, and was definitely not a horse, a donkey, a cow, a bull, a tiger, a dog, a pig, a cat, or a bear. With every new question, Fred collapsed in fresh roars of laughter, so thoroughly amused that he jumped up from the sofa and stomped his feet. Finally, the curvy sister, catching his infectious laughter, shrieked:

"I’ve got it! I know what it is, Fred! I know!"

"What is it?" cried Fred.

"It's your Uncle Scro-o-o-oge!"

And so it was! Everyone cheered in delight, though a few jokingly argued that when asked, "Is it a bear?" Fred really should have said yes, since saying no had thrown everyone off track!

"He's certainly given us plenty of laughs tonight," Fred said, "and it would be ungrateful not to raise a glass to him. Here's a mug of warm mulled wine right in front of us, and I propose: Uncle Scrooge!"

"Hear, hear! Uncle Scrooge!" they all echoed.

"A Merry Christmas and a Happy New Year to the old man, whatever his faults!" Fred declared. "He refused my wishes in person, but I hope he finds joy nonetheless. To Uncle Scrooge!"

Scrooge had grown so lighthearted and merry that he would have gladly raised a toast right back to the oblivious party and thanked them with an earnest speech if the Ghost had given him the chance. But the entire room vanished on the very breath of Fred's final words, and Scrooge and the Spirit were traveling once more.

They saw so much and journeyed far, visiting countless homes, and each visit brought comfort and joy. The Spirit stood beside hospital sickbeds, and the ill found peace and good cheer; he visited foreign lands, and travelers felt close to home; he paused beside weary, struggling workers, and they found patience in a brighter hope; he touched deep poverty, and made it feel spiritually rich. In shelters, hospitals, and prisons—in every sanctuary of human sorrow where arrogant mortals in their brief moments of power hadn't locked the doors to shut the Spirit out—he left his blessing, teaching Scrooge how love and kindness can heal the world.

It felt like an extraordinarily long night—if indeed it was only a single night. Scrooge began to doubt that it was, for the entire holiday season seemed packed into the time they spent wandering together. Stranger still, while Scrooge didn't change at all on the outside, the Ghost was visibly aging. Scrooge had noticed this gradual shift but kept quiet about it until they left a festive Twelfth Night party for children. Standing together in an open square, Scrooge looked up and saw that the Spirit’s hair had turned silvery gray.

"Are a spirit's days so short?" Scrooge asked.

"My life on this earth is very brief," the Ghost answered softly. "It ends tonight."

"Tonight!" cried Scrooge.

"Tonight at midnight. Listen! The hour is almost here."

Right on cue, church bells rang out quarter to twelve.

"Pardon me if I have no right to ask," said Scrooge, staring intently at the hem of the Spirit’s robe, "but I notice something strange peeking out from beneath your folds. Is that a foot or a claw?"

"It might as well be a claw, given how little flesh is on it," the Spirit replied with profound sorrow. "Look here."

From beneath the folds of his green robe, the Spirit revealed two children. They were wretched, starving, terrified, and heartbreakingly miserable. They dropped to their knees at his feet, clinging desperately to his garment.

"Look, O Mankind! Look down here!" the Ghost cried.

They were a boy and a girl. Sallow, gaunt, dressed in rags, scowling and feral, yet bowed low in desperate pleading. Where youthful beauty should have rounded their hollow cheeks and painted them with healthy color, a cruel, withered hand—like the touch of bitter old age—had pinched and twisted their features into misery. Where the light of innocence should have shone, despair and menace lurked. In all the vast wonders of creation, no decay, no corruption, no distortion of human nature could produce monsters half so terrifying and tragic.

Scrooge recoiled in horror. He wanted to say they were handsome children, but the words caught in his throat, refusing to utter such a monstrous lie.

"Spirit! Are they yours?" Scrooge choked out.

"They belong to Man," the Spirit answered, looking down at them. "They cling to me, crying out against the cruelty of their fathers. This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want. Beware them both, and all who follow them! But most of all, beware this boy, for across his forehead I see written DOOM, unless that curse is wiped away! Deny it!" shouted the Spirit, thrusting an accusing hand toward the distant city. "Slander those who speak the truth! Twist it for your political gain and make it worse—and suffer the consequences when the end arrives!"

"Do they have nowhere to go? No help at all?" cried Scrooge.

"Are there no prisons?" the Spirit turned on him, piercing him for the last time with his own cold words. "Are there no workhouses?"

The clock struck midnight.

Scrooge looked around for the Ghost, but he was gone. As the final chime faded into silence, he remembered Jacob Marley's warning. Lifting his eyes, he saw a dark, solemn Phantom, draped and hooded, gliding toward him like a thick mist sweeping along the ground.

# Chapter 4: The Last of the Spirits

The Phantom slowly, gravely, and silently approached. When it drew near, Scrooge dropped to his knees, for the very air through which this Spirit moved seemed to scatter gloom and mystery.

It was shrouded in a deep black robe that concealed its head, face, and form, leaving nothing visible except a single outstretched hand. If not for that hand, it would have been difficult to distinguish its dark shape from the night, or separate it from the deep shadows surrounding it.

Scrooge felt that it was tall and stately as it stood beside him, and its mysterious presence filled him with a solemn dread. He knew nothing more, for the Spirit neither spoke nor moved.

"I am in the presence of the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come?" said Scrooge.

The Spirit did not answer, but pointed forward with its hand.

"You are about to show me shadows of things that have not happened yet, but will happen in the time before us," Scrooge continued. "Is that true, Spirit?"

The upper folds of the dark robe shifted for a moment, as if the Spirit had bowed its head. That was the only answer he received.

Although he was quite used to ghostly company by now, Scrooge feared this silent shape so much that his legs trembled beneath him. When he prepared to follow, he found he could hardly stand. The Spirit paused for a moment, observing his condition and giving him time to steady himself.

Yet this only made things worse for Scrooge. A vague, unsettling dread chilled him, knowing that behind that dusky shroud, ghostly eyes were fixed intently upon him—while no matter how hard he strained his own eyes, he could see nothing but a spectral hand and a great mass of black.

"Ghost of the Future!" he cried. "I fear you more than any spirit I have seen. But because I know your purpose is to do me good, and because I hope to live as a different man from what I once was, I am ready to go with you, and to do so with a thankful heart. Won't you speak to me?"

It gave him no reply. The hand simply pointed straight ahead.

"Lead on!" said Scrooge. "Lead on! The night is fading fast, and I know this time is precious to me. Lead on, Spirit!"

The Phantom moved away just as it had approached. Scrooge followed in the shadow of its robe, which seemed to lift him up and carry him along.

They hardly seemed to enter the city. Instead, the city seemed to spring up around them and swallow them whole. But there they were, right in the heart of it—on the Exchange, among the merchants and businessmen. Men hurried back and forth, jingling coins in their pockets, gathering in small groups, checking their pocket watches, and absently fingering their heavy gold watch seals, just as Scrooge had seen them do a thousand times before.

The Spirit stopped beside one little cluster of businessmen. Seeing that the hand was pointed toward them, Scrooge stepped forward to listen to their conversation.

"No," said a large, stout man with an enormous double chin. "I don't know much about it either way. I only know he's dead."

"When did he die?" asked another.

"Last night, I believe."

"Why, what was the matter with him?" asked a third, taking a huge pinch of snuff from an oversized snuffbox. "I thought he'd never die."

"God only knows," said the first man with a yawn.

"What has he done with all his money?" asked a red-faced gentleman with a pendulous wart hanging from the tip of his nose, which shook like the wattles of a turkey.

"I haven't heard," said the man with the large chin, yawning again. "Left it to his company, perhaps. He certainly hasn't left it to me. That's all I know."

This little joke was received with general laughter.

"It will probably be a very cheap funeral," the same speaker added. "Because on my life, I don't know a single soul who will go. What do you say we get a group together and volunteer?"

"I don't mind going if a lunch is provided," observed the gentleman with the wart on his nose. "Feed me, and I'll make one of the party."

Another laugh went round.

"Well, I suppose I am the most selfless among you after all," said the first speaker. "For I never wear black gloves to funerals, and I never eat lunch. But I'll offer to go if anyone else will. When I come to think of it, I'm not at all sure I wasn't his closest friend. We used to stop and chat whenever we met on the street. Goodbye, gentlemen!"

The speakers and listeners strolled away, blending into other crowds. Scrooge recognized every one of those men and turned to the Spirit for an explanation.

The Phantom glided on into another street. Its finger pointed to two men meeting. Scrooge listened closely again, thinking the explanation might lie here.

He knew these men intimately as well. They were wealthy, prominent figures in commerce. Scrooge had always made a point of standing high in their esteem—strictly from a business point of view, of course.

"How are you?" asked one.

"How are you?" replied the other.

"Well!" said the first. "Old Scratch has finally claimed his own, eh?"

"So I am told," replied the second. "Cold out, isn't it?"

"Normal for Christmas time. You're not a skater, I suppose?"

"No, no. Far too much else to think about. Good morning!"

Not another word. That was the entirety of their meeting, their conversation, and their parting.

At first, Scrooge was surprised that the Spirit should attach any importance to conversations so seemingly trivial. But feeling certain they must have a hidden purpose, he set his mind to consider what it could be. They could hardly have anything to do with the death of Jacob Marley, his old partner, for Marley was long in the past, and this Ghost's domain was the Future. Nor could he think of anyone close to him to whom the remarks might apply. Still, never doubting that whoever was being spoken of held some moral lesson for his own redemption, he resolved to treasure up every word he heard and everything he saw—and especially to watch for the shadow of himself when it appeared. He expected that the conduct of his future self would provide the missing key and solve these riddles easily.

He looked around that familiar place for his own reflection, but another man stood in his accustomed corner. And though the clock showed his usual time of day for being there, he saw no likeness of himself among the multitudes bustling through the entrance. It gave him little surprise, however. He had already been contemplating a complete change in his life, and he hoped this absence meant his new resolutions were already being carried out.

Silent and dark beside him stood the Phantom, its hand still outstretched. When Scrooge broke away from his search, he sensed from the turn of the hand and its position toward him that the Spirit's unseen eyes were watching him keenly. A sudden shudder ran through him, leaving him feeling bitterly cold.

They left the busy scene and moved into an obscure, rundown part of town where Scrooge had never set foot before, though he knew well enough where it was and what a wretched reputation it held. The alleys were narrow and foul, the shops and tenements rotting and filthy, and the people half-clothed, drunken, ragged, and rough. Dark passageways and archways, like open sewers, poured out their stench, dirt, and miserable lives onto the crooked streets. The entire quarter reeked of crime, filth, and misery.

Deep within this den of thieves and scoundrels sat a low-ceilinged, crooked shop under an overhanging roof, where scrap iron, old rags, empty bottles, bones, and tubs of foul animal fat were bought and sold. Heaped across the floor inside were piles of rusted keys, nails, chains, hinges, files, scales, counterweights, and scrap metal of every description. Secrets that few would ever care to uncover festered and hid beneath mountains of filthy rags, mounds of spoiled grease, and heaps of dry bones.

Sitting among the wares he traded in, beside a charcoal burner made of stacked old bricks, was a gray-haired rascal nearly seventy years old. To block out the biting cold from the street, he had hung up a greasy curtain of tattered rags along a line, and now he smoked his pipe with all the quiet satisfaction of a gentleman in luxury.

Scrooge and the Phantom entered just as a woman carrying a heavy bundle slipped inside. But she had barely stepped through the doorway when another woman, similarly loaded down, came in right behind her. Hard on her heels came a man dressed in faded black clothes, looking just as startled to see the women as they were to see each other. After a moment of blank astonishment, in which the old man with the pipe joined in, all three burst out laughing.

"Trust the cleaning woman to be first!" cried she who had entered first. "Trust the laundry woman to be second, and trust the undertaker's assistant to be third! Look at this, Old Joe—talk about a coincidence! What are the odds of all three of us meeting here without meaning to?"

"You couldn't have met in a better place," Old Joe said, removing the pipe from his mouth. "Come on into the parlour. You've had the run of the place for years, and the other two aren't strangers either. Hold on while I shut the shop door. Listen to how it screeches! I swear there isn't a rustier piece of iron in this whole place than those hinges, and I know there aren't any older bones in here than mine! Ha, ha! We all suit our trade nicely—we're a perfect match. Come on back. Come into the parlour."

The "parlour" was simply the small space behind the rag curtain. The old man poked the embers together using an old iron stair rod. After trimming his smoky oil lamp with the stem of his pipe—for night had fallen—he put the pipe back between his teeth.

While he did this, the first woman tossed her heavy bundle onto the floor and planted herself boldly on a stool. Resting her elbows on her knees, she stared down the other two with defiant eyes.

"What does it matter, Mrs. Dilber?" she said. "Every person has a right to take care of themselves. He always did!"

"That's the honest truth," the laundress agreed. "No man more so."

"Then don't stand there staring like you're frightened, woman! Who's going to know? We're not about to go picking holes in each other's coats, are we?"

"No, indeed!" Mrs. Dilber and the man answered together. "We should hope not!"

"Right then!" the woman cried. "That settles it. Who's any worse off for losing a few things like these? Not a dead man, I suppose!"

"No, indeed," Mrs. Dilber laughed.

"If that wicked old miser wanted to keep his things after he died," the woman continued, "why wasn't he decent while he was alive? If he had been human, he would have had someone to look after him when death struck, instead of lying there gasping out his last breath all alone in that empty room."

"Truer words were never spoken," said Mrs. Dilber. "It's a judgment on him."

"I only wish it were a heavier judgment," the woman snapped. "And it would have been, you can count on that, if I could have laid my hands on anything else. Open that bundle, Old Joe, and tell me what it's worth. Speak plainly. I'm not afraid to go first, and I don't care if they see it. We all know we were helping ourselves before we ran into each other here. It's no sin. Open the bundle, Joe."

Her companions' sense of manners would not allow her to be first, however. The man in faded black stepped up to the front and produced his loot. It was not extensive: a seal or two, a pencil case, a pair of cuff links, and a cheap brooch of little value. Old Joe inspected each item carefully, chalking the price he was willing to pay on the wall, and tallied the total when the man had emptied his pockets.

"That's your account," Joe said. "And I wouldn't give you another sixpence if I were to be boiled alive for refusing. Who's next?"

Mrs. Dilber went next. She had sheets, towels, a few articles of clothing, two antique silver teaspoons, a pair of sugar tongs, and a couple of boots. Her total was chalked up on the wall beside the first.

"I always give too much to the ladies. It's a weakness of mine—it'll ruin me one of these days," Old Joe joked. "That's your money. If you ask for a single penny more, I'll regret being so generous and knock off half a crown."

"And now unpack mine, Joe," the first woman said.

Joe dropped to his knees to untie the heavy bundle. After working loose knot after knot, he hauled out a thick, heavy roll of dark fabric.

"What on earth do you call this?" Joe asked. "Bed curtains?"

"You got it!" the woman laughed, leaning forward over her crossed arms. "Bed curtains!"

"You don't mean to tell me you took them down, rings and all, while he was lying right there on the bed?" Joe gasped.

"Yes, I do," she replied. "Why not?"

"You were born to strike it rich," said Joe, "and you certainly will."

"I certainly wasn't going to hold back when all I had to do was reach out my hand, not for the sake of a man like him, I can promise you that, Joe," she said coolly. "Watch it! Don't drip that lamp oil on those blankets."

"His blankets?" asked Joe.

"Whose else do you think?" she scoffed. "He's hardly going to catch a cold without them now, is he?"

"I hope he didn't die of anything contagious, did he?" Old Joe asked, stopping in his work and looking up with concern.

"Don't you worry your head about that," she replied. "I wasn't so fond of his company that I'd linger around his bed if he had something catching! Go ahead, look through that shirt until your eyes ache—you won't find a single hole or a threadbare patch anywhere. It was the finest shirt he owned, and a beauty too. They would have thrown it away if it hadn't been for me."

"What do you call throwing it away?" asked Old Joe.

"Putting it on him to be buried in, of course!" the woman laughed. "Somebody was fool enough to dress him in it, but I took it right back off. If plain cotton isn't good enough to rot in the ground, it isn't good enough for anything. It looks just as decent on a corpse. He couldn't look any uglier than he already did!"

Scrooge listened to this conversation in sheer horror. Watching them huddled around their stolen plunder in the dim glow of the old man's lamp, he felt a wave of disgust and revulsion so overpowering they might as well have been vile demons haggling over the dead man's flesh.

"Ha, ha!" the woman laughed again as Old Joe pulled out a flannel money pouch and counted out their coins onto the ground. "Look how it ends! He spent his whole life scaring everyone away just so we could profit off his death! Ha, ha, ha!"

"Spirit!" cried Scrooge, trembling from head to foot. "I see! I see! The fate of this miserable man could be my own. The life I am living is heading straight toward this very end. Merciful Heaven, what is this!"

He shrank back in terror, for the scene vanished in an instant, and now he found himself standing beside a bed—a bare, curtainless bed. Beneath a ragged sheet lay a shrouded shape that, without speaking a word, delivered an awful, chilling message.

The room was pitch black, too dark to make out clearly, though Scrooge glanced around instinctively, desperate to see what kind of room it was. A pale sliver of daylight crept through from the outside, falling straight across the bed. And there, robbed, abandoned, unwatched, unwept, and unmourned, lay the body of this man.

Scrooge looked at the Phantom. Its steady hand pointed directly at the corpse's head. The sheet had been pulled so loosely over the face that the slightest touch—the mere lift of Scrooge's finger—would have disclosed who it was. The thought crossed his mind; he knew how easily it could be done, and part of him burned to know. Yet he had no more power to lift that cloth than he had to banish the spirit standing beside him.

O cold, cold, rigid, dreadful Death! Set up your altar here and drape it with all the terrors at your command, for this is your kingdom! But over a beloved, respected, and honored head, you cannot turn a single hair to your cruel will, or make a single feature repulsive. It is not just that the hand is heavy and falls limp when released; it is not just that the heart and pulse are still. It is that the hand was once open, generous, and true; the heart brave, warm, and tender; and the life full of humanity. Strike, Shadow, strike! And see how good deeds burst forth from the wound, scattering seeds of eternal life across the world!

No living voice spoke those words into Scrooge's ears, yet he heard them ringing clearly in his mind as he gazed upon that lonely bed. He wondered: if this man could be raised up this very second, what would his first thoughts be? Greed? Ruthless deals? The endless pursuit of pennies? Look where all that clawing had brought him—to this grand, lonely end!

There he lay, in a dark, empty house, without a single man, woman, or child to say, "He was kind to me in this or that, and for the memory of one kind word, I will show kindness to him now." Outside the door, a stray cat clawed and scratched, while beneath the hearthstones, rats scurried and gnawed. What they wanted in that chamber of death, and why they were so frantic, Scrooge did not dare imagine.

"Spirit!" he pleaded. "This is a fearful place. In leaving it, I swear I will not forget its lesson. Please, let us go!"

Still the Ghost pointed with an unmoving finger at the head.

"I understand what you want," Scrooge answered. "And I would do it if I could. But I don't have the power, Spirit. I don't have the strength."

Again, the silent gaze of the Phantom seemed to bore into him.

"If there is anyone in this entire city who feels any emotion caused by this man's death," Scrooge cried in agony, "show them to me, Spirit, I beg of you!"

The Phantom spread its dark robe before him like a wing. When it drew back, a sunlit room appeared, where a young mother waited with her children.

She was waiting for someone with anxious suspense. She paced back and forth, jumping at every faint sound, looking out through the window, glancing at the clock, and trying in vain to work with her needle. Even the joyful voices of her children at play seemed more than her frayed nerves could bear.

At length, a long-expected knock sounded at the door. She hurried to open it and met her husband. He was a young man, but his face was worn with worry and exhaustion. Yet now, an unusual look rested upon his features—a strange flicker of quiet relief, something he seemed almost ashamed of and fought to hold back.

He sat down to the simple dinner that had been kept warm for him by the fire. When his wife softly asked him for news—only after a long, tense silence—he seemed at a loss for how to answer.

"Is it good news?" she asked, trying to help him speak. "Or bad?"

"Bad," he answered.

"Are we completely ruined?"

"No. There is still hope, Caroline."

"If he relents," she gasped in amazement, "then there is! Nothing is beyond hope if such a miracle has happened!"

"He is past relenting," her husband replied. "He is dead."

Her face was gentle and kind, the face of a patient soul; yet hearing those words, her heart filled with overwhelming gratitude. She clasped her hands together in thanksgiving. A heartbeat later, she bowed her head, asking forgiveness for feeling glad, and felt truly sorry. But the first, involuntary reaction of her heart had been pure relief.

"What that half-drunken woman told me last night—the one I mentioned when I tried to see him for a one-week delay on our payment—turns out to be completely true," her husband explained. "I thought she was just making excuses so he wouldn't have to talk to me. But he wasn't just sick. He was dying then."

"To whom will our debt be transferred?" she asked.

"I don't know. But by the time the estate is settled, we'll have the money ready. And even if we don't, we would have to suffer terrible misfortune to find a creditor as heartless as he was. We can sleep tonight with light hearts, Caroline!"

Yes. Disguise it however they tried, their burdens had lifted. The children gathered around, quieted by matters they could barely comprehend, their little faces brightening at their parents' eased spirits. It was a happier home because that man had died! The only emotion the Ghost could show him, sparked by his passing, was one of relief and joy.

"Let me see some tenderness connected with a death," Scrooge pleaded. "Or that dark room we just left will haunt me forever."

The Ghost led him through several streets familiar to his feet. As they walked, Scrooge searched everywhere for any glimpse of himself, but he was nowhere to be seen. Soon they entered poor Bob Cratchit's humble house—the same dwelling Scrooge had visited before. Inside, the mother and children sat gathered around the fire.

Quiet. Very quiet. The usually lively little Cratchits sat still as statues in the corner, gazing up at Peter, who held an open book before him. Mrs. Cratchit and her daughters were busy sewing. But how strangely quiet they were!

"'And He took a child, and set him in the midst of them.'"

Where had Scrooge heard those words before? He had not dreamed them. The boy must have just read them aloud as Scrooge and the Spirit crossed the threshold. Why didn't he keep reading?

The mother laid her needlework on the table and pressed her hand against her face.

"The color of this cloth hurts my eyes," she murmured.

The color? Ah, poor Tiny Tim!

"They're better now," Cratchit's wife said after a moment, trying to smile. "Working by candlelight makes them weak, and I wouldn't want your father to see me with weak eyes when he comes home for the world. It must be near his time."

"Past time, rather," Peter answered, shutting the book. "Though I think he has been walking a little slower than he used to these past few evenings, Mother."

Silence settled over the room once more. At last, Mrs. Cratchit spoke in a steady, cheerful voice that only faltered once:

"I have seen him walk with—I have seen him walk with Tiny Tim upon his shoulder, very fast indeed."

"So have I," cried Peter softly. "Often."

"So have I," exclaimed another child. They all remembered.

"He was so light to carry," she continued, intent upon her work, "and his father loved him so dearly that it was no trouble—no trouble at all. And look, there is your father at the door!"

She hurried out to meet him. Bob entered, wrapped in his long woolen scarf—he had great need of it, poor fellow. His hot tea was waiting by the hearth, and all the children rushed to help him take his coat and pour his cup. Then the two youngest Cratchits climbed onto his knees and laid their cheeks against his face, as if whispering without words, "Don't mind it, Father. Don't be grieved!"

Bob put on a cheerful face for them and spoke pleasantly to the whole family. He looked at the sewing spread across the table and praised the industry and speed of Mrs. Cratchit and the girls. He said they would surely be finished with the mourning clothes long before Sunday.

"Sunday! Did you go there today, Robert?" asked his wife.

"Yes, my dear," Bob replied. "I wish you could have gone. It would have done your heart good to see how green and peaceful a place it is. But you'll see it often. I promised him that I would walk out to visit him every Sunday. My little, little child!" cried Bob suddenly. "My little child!"

The grief broke through all at once. He couldn't help it. If he could have held back those tears, he and his little boy would have been farther apart in spirit than they were.

He stood up, left the room, and went upstairs to the small bedroom above. It was brightly lit and decorated with fresh Christmas greenery. Beside the bed sat a single chair, and there were signs that someone had been sitting there recently. Poor Bob sat down in it, and after sitting in quiet thought to compose himself, he leaned over and kissed the sweet little face. He found peace with what had happened, and when he came back downstairs, he was calm and gently smiling.

They gathered around the fire together to talk while the girls and their mother continued their stitching. Bob told them about an extraordinary act of kindness from Mr. Scrooge's nephew. Bob had scarcely seen him but once before, but when the nephew ran into him on the street that afternoon and noticed Bob looking down—"just a little down, you know," Bob said—he stopped and asked what had happened to distress him.

"And when I told him," Bob said, "for he is the pleasantest, warmest gentleman you could ever hope to hear, he took my hand and said, 'I am heartily sorry for your loss, Mr. Cratchit, and heartily sorry for your good wife.' By the way, how he ever knew you were a good wife, I don't know!"

"Knew what, my dear?" asked Mrs. Cratchit.

"Why, that you are such a wonderful wife!" Bob laughed gently.

"Everybody knows that!" Peter said proudly.

"Very well observed, my boy!" cried Bob. "I certainly hope they do. 'Heartily sorry,' he said, 'for your good wife. If I can be of service to you in any way,' he said, giving me his card, 'that's where I live. Please come see me.' Now," Bob explained, "it wasn't for the sake of anything he might be able to do for us, so much as for his sincere, heartfelt way, that made it so comforting. It really seemed as if he had known our Tiny Tim himself and felt our heartbreak."

"I'm sure he's a good soul," said Mrs. Cratchit softly.

"You would be even surer of it, my dear," returned Bob, "if you saw and spoke to him. In fact, mark my words—I wouldn't be at all surprised if he helped Peter find a better position."

"Listen to that, Peter!" beamed Mrs. Cratchit.

"And then," teased one of the girls, "Peter will be keeping company with someone and setting up a home of his own!"

"Get along with you!" retorted Peter, grinning and blushing.

"It's just as likely as not one of these days," Bob chuckled, "though there's plenty of time for that, my dear. But no matter how or when we part from one another, I know none of us will ever forget poor Tiny Tim—will we?—or this first parting that has come among us."

"Never, Father!" they all cried together.

"And I know," said Bob tenderly, "I know, my children, that when we remember how patient and how gentle he was—even though he was just a little, little child—we will not quarrel easily among ourselves, and forget poor Tiny Tim in doing it."

"No, never, Father!" they all promised again.

"I am very happy," whispered Bob. "I am very happy!"

Mrs. Cratchit kissed him, his daughters kissed him, the two young Cratchits wrapped their arms around him, and Peter shook his father's hand. Spirit of Tiny Tim, your innocent, loving soul was a gift straight from God!

"Spectre," said Scrooge, "something informs me that our parting moment is at hand. I know it, though I know not how. Tell me: what man was that whom we saw lying dead upon the bed?"

The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come transported him once again through the city. As before, Scrooge tried to understand the flow of time, though there seemed to be no order in these visions, except that they all took place in the Future. The Spirit paused for nothing, moving straight ahead as if driving toward the answer Scrooge sought, until Scrooge begged him to stop for a moment.

"This court," said Scrooge, "through which we are hurrying right now—this is where my place of business is, and has been for years. I see the building! Let me look inside and see what I shall be in the days to come!"

The Spirit stopped, but its hand pointed in a different direction.

"The building is right over there!" Scrooge urged. "Why do you point away?"

The unyielding finger underwent no change.

Scrooge hurried over to the window of his office and peered inside. It was an office still, but no longer his. The furniture was different, and the figure sitting in the chair was not himself. The Phantom stood pointing as before.

Scrooge rejoined it once again, wondering where he had gone and what could have happened, following the Spirit until they reached an old iron gate. He paused to look around before entering.

A graveyard.

Here, then, beneath the ground, lay the wretched man whose name he had now to learn. And what a fitting place it was! Hemmed in on all sides by crowded houses, overgrown with weeds and wild grass—the kind of vegetation that feeds on decay rather than life—choked with too many graves, bloated from decades of burials. A fitting place indeed!

The Spirit stood among the graves and pointed down at one neglected mound. Scrooge advanced toward it, trembling with every step. The Phantom remained exactly as it had been, wrapped in its solemn shroud, but Scrooge dreaded that he saw a terrifying new meaning in its posture.

"Before I step any closer to the stone to which you point," said Scrooge, "answer me one question. Are these the shadows of things that *will* be, or are they shadows of things that *may* be, only?"

Still the Ghost pointed downward at the grave beside it.

"A person's path in life foreshadows certain ends, and if they persevere in them, to those ends they must lead," said Scrooge. "But if the path is abandoned, the ends will change! Tell me it is so with what you show me!"

The Spirit remained as immovable as ever.

Scrooge crept forward, shaking from head to foot. Following the direction of that dark finger, he looked upon the weathered headstone of the neglected grave and read his own name:

EBENEZER SCROOGE.

"Am I that man who lay upon the bed?" he cried, falling to his knees.

The finger pointed from the grave to him, and back again.

"No, Spirit! Oh no, no!"

The finger was still there.

"Spirit!" he cried, clutching tightly at its robe. "Hear me! I am not the man I was! I will not be the man I would have been if not for this journey! Why show me this if I am past all hope?"

For the first time, the spectral hand appeared to shake.

"Good Spirit," Scrooge wept, throwing himself face down on the ground before it. "Your own nature pleads for me and pities me! Assure me that I may still change these shadows you have shown me by living an altered life!"

The kind hand trembled.

"I will honor Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year! I will live in the Past, the Present, and the Future. The Spirits of all Three shall strive within me. I will not shut out the lessons that they teach. Oh, tell me I may sponge away the writing on this stone!"

In his agony, he caught the spectral hand. It sought to free itself, but Scrooge was desperate in his plea and held on with all his strength. Yet the Spirit, stronger still, repulsed him.

Holding up his hands in one last prayer to have his fate reversed, Scrooge saw an alteration come over the Phantom's hood and robe.

It shrank, collapsed, and dwindled down...

Until it was nothing more than a bedpost.

# Chapter 5: The End of It

Yes! The bedpost was his own! The bed was his own, the room was his own, and best and happiest of all, the time ahead was his own—time to make amends!

"I will live in the Past, the Present, and the Future!" Scrooge shouted, scrambling out of bed. "The spirits of all three will live within me! Oh, Jacob Marley! Heaven and Christmas time be praised for this! I say it on my knees, old Jacob—on my knees!"

He was trembling with excitement and glowing with good intentions, so choked up that his voice could barely answer his call. He had been sobbing violently during his struggle with the Spirit, and his face was still soaked with tears.

"They aren't torn down!" cried Scrooge, gathering one of his bed curtains into his arms. "They aren't torn down, rings and all! They're still here—I am still here—and the shadows of what might have been can be swept away! They will be! I know they will!"

His hands flew wildly over his clothes the whole time—turning them inside out, pulling them on upside down, tearing them, misplacing them, and subjecting them to every kind of joyful chaos.

"I don't know what to do!" cried Scrooge, laughing and crying in the same breath, completely tangled up in his stockings like a man wrestling sea serpents. "I'm as light as a feather! I'm as happy as an angel! I'm as merry as a schoolboy! I'm as dizzy as a drunken man! Merry Christmas to everybody! Happy New Year to the whole world! Woohoo! Hey! Hello!"

He bounded into the sitting room and stood there, completely out of breath.

"There's the saucepan the gruel was in!" cried Scrooge, taking off again and circling the fireplace. "There's the door the ghost of Jacob Marley walked through! There's the corner where the Ghost of Christmas Present sat! There's the window where I saw the wandering spirits! It's all right, it's all true, it really happened! Ha, ha, ha!"

Really, for a man who had been out of practice for so many years, it was a splendid laugh—a truly magnificent laugh. It was the father of a long, long line of brilliant laughs!

"I don't know what day of the month it is!" said Scrooge. "I don't know how long I've been with the spirits. I don't know anything! I'm practically a baby. Never mind—I don't care! I'd rather be a baby anyway! Hey! Woohoo! Hello there!"

He was stopped in his tracks by church bells ringing out the most joyful peals he had ever heard. Clash, clang, hammer! Ding, dong, bell! Bell, dong, ding! Hammer, clang, clash! Oh, glorious, glorious!

Running to the window, he threw it open and stuck his head out. No fog, no mist! It was clear, bright, cheerful, brisk, and cold—a cold that made the blood dance in your veins! Golden sunlight, a heavenly sky, sweet fresh air, and merry bells. Oh, glorious! Glorious!

"What day is today?" cried Scrooge, calling down to a boy in his Sunday clothes who had paused on the street to look around.

"Huh?" the boy replied, completely baffled.

"What's today, my fine fellow?" Scrooge asked.

"Today?" replied the boy. "Why, it's Christmas Day!"

"It's Christmas Day!" Scrooge said to himself. "I haven't missed it! The spirits did it all in one night! They can do whatever they want. Of course they can—of course they can! Hey, my fine fellow!"

"Hello!" the boy called back.

"Do you know the poultry shop two streets over, on the corner?" Scrooge inquired.

"I sure do!" replied the boy.

"Smart kid!" said Scrooge. "A remarkable boy! Do you know if they've sold the prize turkey hanging in the window? Not the little prize turkey—the big one?"

"What, the one as big as me?" returned the boy.

"What a delightful boy!" said Scrooge. "It's an absolute pleasure to talk to him. Yes, that's the one, my lad!"

"It's hanging there right now," replied the boy.

"Is it?" said Scrooge. "Go and buy it."

"No way! You're kidding!" exclaimed the boy.

"No, no," said Scrooge. "I'm completely serious! Go and buy it, and tell them to bring it right here so I can give them the delivery address. Come back with the delivery man, and I'll give you a shilling. Come back with him in less than five minutes, and I'll give you half a crown!"

The boy was off like a shot. A person would have needed a remarkably quick finger on the trigger to fire off a bullet half so fast.

"I'll send it to Bob Cratchit's!" whispered Scrooge, rubbing his hands together and splitting with laughter. "He won't have a clue who sent it! It's twice the size of Tiny Tim! No comedian in history ever pulled off a better joke than sending this to Bob will be!"

His hand wasn't steady as he wrote the address, but somehow he managed to write it. He hurried downstairs to open the front door, waiting eagerly for the delivery man to arrive. As he stood there waiting, the door knocker caught his eye.

"I'll love you as long as I live!" cried Scrooge, patting it with his hand. "I hardly ever looked at it before! What an honest expression it has on its face! It's a wonderful knocker! Ah, here's the turkey! Hey! Woohoo! How are you? Merry Christmas!"

And what a turkey it was! That bird could never have stood on its own legs—they would have snapped clean in two in a heartbeat, like brittle sticks of sealing wax.

"Why, it's impossible to carry that all the way to Camden Town," said Scrooge. "You'll have to take a cab."

The chuckle with which he said this, the chuckle with which he paid for the turkey, the chuckle with which he paid for the cab, and the chuckle with which he rewarded the boy were only outdone by the chuckle with which he sank back into his armchair, completely out of breath, and chuckled until tears ran down his face.

Shaving was no easy task, because his hand kept shaking terribly, and shaving demands attention even when you aren't dancing around while doing it. But even if he had sliced the tip of his nose right off, he would have stuck a bandage over it and been completely happy.

He dressed himself in his very best clothes and finally stepped out into the streets. Crowds were pouring outside by now, just as he had seen them with the Ghost of Christmas Present. Walking with his hands behind his back, Scrooge looked upon everyone with a delighted smile. In a word, he looked so irresistibly pleasant that three or four good-natured people called out, "Good morning, sir! A Merry Christmas to you!" And Scrooge often said afterward that of all the cheerful sounds he had ever heard, those were the sweetest to his ears.

He hadn't gone far when he saw the portly gentleman coming toward him—the very man who had walked into his office the day before and asked, "Scrooge and Marley's, I believe?" It sent a pang through his heart to think of how this kind gentleman would look at him when they met, but Scrooge knew which path lay straight ahead, and he took it.

"My dear sir," said Scrooge, quickening his pace and taking the gentleman warmly by both hands. "How do you do? I hope your work went well yesterday. It was so very kind of you. A Merry Christmas to you, sir!"

"Mr. Scrooge?"

"Yes," said Scrooge. "That is my name, and I'm afraid it might not sound very pleasant to you. Please allow me to ask your forgiveness. And would you have the goodness to—" Here, Scrooge leaned in and whispered in his ear.

"Lord bless me!" cried the gentleman, his breath completely taken away. "My dear Mr. Scrooge, are you serious?"

"If you please," said Scrooge. "Not a penny less. A great many back-payments are included in it, I assure you. Will you do me that favor?"

"My dear sir," said the gentleman, pumping his hand. "I don't know what to say to such munifi—"

"Don't say anything, please," Scrooge replied. "Just come and visit me. Will you come and see me?"

"I will!" cried the gentleman. And it was clear he truly meant it.

"Thank you," said Scrooge. "I am deeply grateful to you. I thank you fifty times over. God bless you!"

He went to church, walked through the streets, and watched the crowds bustling back and forth. He patted children on the head, spoke kindly with beggars, peered down into basement kitchens, looked up at glowing windows, and found that absolutely everything brought him pleasure. He had never dreamed that any walk—that anything in life—could give him so much happiness. In the afternoon, he made his way toward his nephew's house.

He paced past the front door a dozen times before finding the courage to go up and knock. But he took a breath, stepped forward, and did it.

"Is your master at home, my dear?" Scrooge asked the young maid who opened the door. A lovely girl! Truly lovely.

"Yes, sir."

"Where is he, my dear?" asked Scrooge.

"He's in the dining room with the mistress, sir. I'll show you upstairs, if you please."

"Thank you, but he knows me well," Scrooge said, his hand already resting on the dining room latch. "I'll go right in, my dear."

He turned the handle gently and peeked his face around the door. Fred and his wife were inspecting the dining table, which was lavishly spread out; young hosts are always anxious about such things and want to make sure every little detail is perfect.

"Fred!" said Scrooge.

Bless my heart, how Fred's wife jumped! For a moment Scrooge had forgotten she was sitting right there in the corner with her feet on the footstool, or he wouldn't have startled her for the world.

"Well, bless my soul!" cried Fred. "Who is that?"

"It's me. Your Uncle Scrooge. I've come for dinner. Will you let me in, Fred?"

Let him in! It was a mercy Fred didn't shake his uncle's arm clean off. Scrooge was completely at home within five minutes. Nothing could have been warmer or more welcoming. His niece looked just as charming as she had in the vision, and so did Topper when he arrived, and the plump sister when she arrived, and everyone else as they came in. It was a wonderful party, with wonderful games, wonderful harmony, and won-der-ful happiness!

The next morning, Scrooge arrived at the office early. Oh, he was early! If only he could get there first and catch Bob Cratchit coming in late—that was the one thing he had set his heart upon.

And he did it—yes, he did! The clock struck nine. No Bob. Quarter past nine. No Bob. He was a full eighteen and a half minutes late! Scrooge sat with his office door wide open so he could watch Bob sneak into his chilly little tank.

Bob had his hat off before he even opened the door, and his scarf was off too. He sprang onto his stool in a heartbeat, his pen flying across the paper as if he could somehow catch up to nine o'clock.

"Hey!" growled Scrooge in his old familiar voice, faking it as best he could. "What do you mean by coming in at this time of day?"

"I am very sorry, sir," said Bob. "I know I am late."

"You are?" repeated Scrooge. "Yes, I believe you are! Step this way, sir, if you please."

"It's only once a year, sir," pleaded Bob, stepping nervously out of his tank. "It won't happen again. We were celebrating a bit yesterday, sir."

"Now, let me tell you something, my friend," said Scrooge. "I am not going to stand for this sort of thing any longer! And therefore," he continued, leaping from his stool and giving Bob such a friendly jab in the ribs that Bob stumbled backward into the tank again, "and therefore, I am going to raise your salary!"

Bob trembled, his hand edging closer to the heavy wooden ruler. For a split second, he had half a mind to knock Scrooge flat with it, pin him to the floor, and shout out the window to the courtyard for help and a straitjacket!

"A Merry Christmas, Bob!" said Scrooge with an earnestness that could not be mistaken, clapping him warmly on the back. "A merrier Christmas, Bob, my good fellow, than any I have given you in many a year! I will raise your salary, and do everything I can to help your struggling family, and we will discuss your affairs this very afternoon over a warm bowl of spiced holiday punch, Bob! Build up the fires, and go buy another coal bucket before you dot another 'i', Bob Cratchit!"

Scrooge was better than his word. He did everything he promised, and infinitely more. And to Tiny Tim—who did *not* die—he became a second father. He became as good a friend, as good an employer, and as good a man as the grand old city had ever known, or any other city, town, or village in the good old world.

Some people laughed to see the dramatic change in him, but he let them laugh and paid them little mind. He was wise enough to know that nothing truly good ever happened on this earth without some people laughing at it in the beginning. Knowing that folks like that would be blind to the light anyway, he figured it was just as well for them to wrinkle up their eyes in grins rather than scowl in uglier ways. His own heart laughed, and that was more than enough for him.

He had no further encounters with spirits, but lived on the principle of total abstinence from ghosts ever after. And it was always said of Ebenezer Scrooge that he knew how to celebrate Christmas well, if any person alive possessed that knowledge.

May that be truly said of us, and of all of us!

And so, as Tiny Tim said:

"God bless us, every one!"
